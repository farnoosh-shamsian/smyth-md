# SYLLABLES AND THEIR QUANTITY

##### <a id="s138"></a> §138

There are as many syllables in a Greek word as there are separate vowels or diphthongs: thus, <span lang="grc">ἀ-λή-θει-α</span> _truth_.

##### <a id="s139"></a> §139

The last syllable is called the _ultima;_ the next to the last syllable is called the _penult_ (paen-ultima _almost last_); the one before the penult is called the _antepenult_ (ante-paen-ultima). <!-- page 35 -->

##### <a id="s140"></a> §140

In pronouncing Greek words and in writing (at the end of the line) the rules commonly observed are these:

**a.** A single consonant standing between two vowels in one word belongs with the second vowel: <span lang="grc">ἄ-γω, σο-φί-ζω</span>.

**b.** Any group of consonants that can begin a word, and a group formed by a stop with <span lang="grc">μ</span> or <span lang="grc">ν</span>, and by <span lang="grc">μν</span>, belongs with the second vowel: <span lang="grc">τύ-πτω, ὄ-γδοος, ἄ-στρον, ἔ-χθος; πρᾶ-γμα, ἔ-θνος, λί-μνη</span>.

**c.** A group of consonants that cannot begin a word is divided between two syllables: <span lang="grc">ἄν-θος, ἐλ-πίς, ἔρ-γμα</span>. Doubled consonants are divided: <span lang="grc">θάλατ-τα</span>.

**d.** Compounds divide at the point of union: <span lang="grc">εἰσ-φέρω, προσ-φέρω; ἀν-άγω, εἰσάγω, συν-έχω</span>. (But the ancients often wrote <span lang="grc">ἀ-νάγω, εἰ-σάγω, προ-σελθεῖν, ἐ-ξάγω, δυ-σάρεστος</span>.)

**e.** <span lang="grc">ς</span>, when followed by one or more consonants, is either attached to the preceding vowel (<span lang="grc">ἄ-ρισ-τος</span>), or, with the consonant, begins the following syllable (<span lang="grc">ἄ-ρι-στος</span>). (The ancients were not consistent, and there is evidence for the pronunciation <span lang="grc">ἄ-ρισ-στος</span>.)

**f.** The ancients divided <span lang="grc">ἐκ τούτου</span> as <span lang="grc">ἐ-κ τού-του</span>. This practice is now abandoned.

##### <a id="s141"></a> §141

A syllable ending in a vowel is said to be open; one ending in a consonant is closed. Thus, in <span lang="grc">μή-τηρ</span> _mother_ the first syllable is open, the second closed.

## QUANTITY OF SYLLABLES

##### <a id="s142"></a> §142

A syllable is short when it contains a short vowel followed by a vowel or a single consonant: <span lang="grc">θε-ός</span> _god_, <span lang="grc">ἐ-νό-μι-σα</span> _I thought_.

##### <a id="s143"></a> §143

A syllable is long by _nature_ when it contains a long vowel or a diphthong: <span lang="grc">χώ-ρᾱ</span> _country_, <span lang="grc">δοῦ-λος</span> _slave_.

##### <a id="s144"></a> §144

A syllable is long by _position_ when its vowel precedes two consonants or a double consonant: <span lang="grc">ἵππος</span> _horse_, <span lang="grc">ἐξ</span> _out of_.

**a.** One or both of the two consonants lengthening a final syllable by position may belong to the next word: <span lang="grc">ἄλλο̄ς πολί̄της, ἄλλο̄ κτῆμα</span>.

**b.** Length by position does not affect the natural quantity of a vowel. Thus, both <span lang="grc">λέ-ξω</span> _I shall say_ and <span lang="grc">λή-ξω</span> _I shall cease_ have the first _syllable_ long by position; but the first _vowel_ is short in <span lang="grc">λἐξω</span>, long in <span lang="grc">λήξω</span>.

##### <a id="s144D"></a> §144 D

<span lang="grc">ϝ</span> may be one of the two consonants: <span lang="grc">πρός</span> (<span lang="grc">ϝ</span>) <span lang="grc">οἶκον</span> (¯ ¯ ˘).

##### <a id="s145"></a> §145

A stop with a liquid after a short vowel need not make the preceding syllable long by position. A syllable containing a short vowel before a stop and a liquid is _common_ (either short or long). When short, such syllables are said to have _weak_ position.

Thus, in <span lang="grc">δάκρυ, πατρός, ὅπλον, τέκνον, τί δρᾷ</span> the first syllable is either long or short as the verse requires. In Homer the syllable before a stop with a liquid is usually long; in Attic it is usually short. <!-- page 36 -->

**a.** The stop and the liquid making weak position must stand in the same word or in the same part of a compound. Thus, in <span lang="grc">ἐκ-λύ̄ω</span> _I release_ the first syllable is always long, but in <span lang="grc">ἔ-κλυε</span> _he heard_ it is common.

**b.** <span lang="grc">β, γ, δ</span> before <span lang="grc">μ</span>, or <span lang="grc">ν</span>, and usually before <span lang="grc">λ</span>, make the preceding syllable long by position. Thus, <span lang="grc">ἁγνός</span> (¯˘) _pure_, <span lang="grc">βιβλίον</span> (×˘˘) _book_.

N.—‘Common’ quantity has been explained as due to a difference in syllabic division. Thus, in <span lang="grc">τέ̄κνον</span>, the first syllable is closed (<span lang="grc">τέκ-νον</span>); while in <span lang="grc">τέ̆κνον</span> the first syllable is open (<span lang="grc">τέ-κνον</span>). Cp. [141](#s141).

##### <a id="s146"></a> §146

The quantity of most syllables is usually apparent. Thus, syllables

**a.** with <span lang="grc">η, ω</span>, or a diphthong, are long.

**b.** with <span lang="grc">ε, ο</span>, before a vowel or a single consonant, are short.

**c.** with <span lang="grc">ε, ο</span>, before two consonants, or a double consonant, are long.

**d.** with <span lang="grc">α, ι, υ</span>, before two consonants, or a double consonant, are long.

N.—But syllables with <span lang="grc">ε, ο</span>, or <span lang="grc">α, ι, υ</span> before a stop and a liquid may be short ([145](#s145)). Cp. also 147 c.

##### <a id="s146D"></a> §146 D

In Hom. an initial liquid, nasal, and digamma (3) was probably doubled in pronunciation when it followed a short syllable carrying the rhythmic accent. Here a final short vowel appears in a long syllable: <span lang="grc">ἐνὶ μεγάροισι</span> (˘\[macrdot\]˘˘\[macrdot\]˘), cp. [28 D](01-the-letters.md#s28). The lengthening is sometimes due to the former presence of <span lang="grc">ς</span> or <span lang="grc">ϝ</span> before the liquid or nasal: <span lang="grc">ὅτε λήξειεϝ</span> (˘\[macrdot\]¯\[macrdot\]˘) (cp. <span lang="grc">ἄλληκτος</span> _unceasing_ for <span lang="grc">ἀ-σληκτος</span>), <span lang="grc">τε ῥήξειν</span> (\[macrdot\]¯\[macrdot\]) (cp. <span lang="grc">ἄρρηκτος</span> _unbroken_ for <span lang="grc">ἀ-ϝρηκτος</span>). (Cp. [80 a](03-euphony-of-consonants.md#s80), [80](03-euphony-of-consonants.md#s80) D., 81 D.)

##### <a id="s147"></a> §147

The quantity of syllables containing <span lang="grc">α, ι, υ</span> before a vowel or a single consonant must be learned by observation, especially in poetry. Note, however, that <span lang="grc">α, ι, υ</span> are always long

**a.** when they have the circumflex accent: <span lang="grc">πᾶς, ὑ̄μῖν</span>.

**b.** when they arise from contraction ([59](02-euphony-of-vowels.md#s59)) or crasis ([62](02-euphony-of-vowels.md#s62)): <span lang="grc">γέρᾱ</span> from <span lang="grc">γέραα, ἀ̄ργός</span> _idle_ from <span lang="grc">ἀ-εργος</span> (but <span lang="grc">ἀ̆ργός</span> _bright_), <span lang="grc">κἀ̄γώ</span> from <span lang="grc">καί ἐγώ</span>.

**c.** <span lang="grc">ι</span> and <span lang="grc">υ</span> are generally short before <span lang="grc">ξ</span> (except as initial sounds in augmented forms, [435](../part-2-inflection/09-verbs/05-augment.md#s435)) and <span lang="grc">α, ι, υ</span> before <span lang="grc">ζ</span>. Thus, <span lang="grc">κῆρῠξ, ἐκήρῠξα, πνί̆ξω, ἁρπά̆ζω, ἐλπί̆ζω</span>.

**d.** <span lang="grc">ας, ις</span>, and <span lang="grc">υς</span> are long when <span lang="grc">ν</span> or <span lang="grc">ντ</span> has dropped out before <span lang="grc">ς</span> ([96](03-euphony-of-consonants.md#s96), [100](03-euphony-of-consonants.md#s100)).

**e.** The accent often shows the quantity ([163](07-accent-general-principles.md#s163), [164](07-accent-general-principles.md#s164), [170](07-accent-general-principles.md#s170)).

##### <a id="s147D"></a> §147 D

<span lang="grc">α, ι, υ</span> in Hom. sometimes show a different quantity than in Attic. Thus, Att. <span lang="grc">κᾰλός, τί̆νω, φθά̆νω, λύ̄ω, ἵ̄ημι</span>, Hom. <span lang="grc">κᾱλός, τί̄νω, φθά̄νω</span> ([28](01-the-letters.md#s28)), and <span lang="grc">λύ̆ω</span> and <span lang="grc">ἵ̆ημι</span> usually.

##### <a id="s148"></a> §148

A vowel standing before another vowel in a Greek word is not necessarily short (as it usually is in classical Latin).

##### <a id="s148D"></a> §148 D

\1. In Hom., and sometimes in the lyric parts of the drama, a syllable ending in a long vowel or diphthong is shortened before an initial vowel: <span lang="grc">ἄξω ἑλών</span> (\[macrdot\]˘˘\[macrdot\]), <span lang="grc">εὔχεται εἶναι</span> (\[macrdot\]˘˘\[macrdot\]¯), <span lang="grc">κλῦθί μευ ἀργυρότοξ᾽</span> (\[macrdot\]:˘˘\[macrdot\]˘˘\[macrdot\]). Here <span lang="grc">ι</span> and <span lang="grc">υ</span> have become semivowels ([20](01-the-letters.md#s20), [43](01-the-letters.md#s43)); thus, <span lang="grc">εὔχετα</span> | _y_<span lang="grc">εἶναι</span>, cp. [67](02-euphony-of-vowels.md#s67). <span lang="grc">-ᾳ, -ῃ, -ῳ</span> were shortened like <span lang="grc">ᾱ, η, ω</span>. Thus, <span lang="grc">ἀσπέτῳ ὄμβρῳ</span> (\[macrdot\]˘˘\[macrdot\]¯).

\2. This shortening does not occur when the rhythmic accent falls upon the final syllable: <span lang="grc">ἀντιθίῳ ᾿ Οδυσῆι</span> (\[macrdot\]˘˘\[macrdot\]˘˘\[macrdot\]˘), <span lang="grc">ᾧ ἔνι</span> (\[macrdot\]˘˘).

\3. The shortening rarely occurs in the interior of a word. Thus, Hom. <span lang="grc">ἥρωος</span> (¯˘˘), <span lang="grc">υἱόν</span> (˘˘), in the Attic drama <span lang="grc">αὑτηΐ</span> (¯˘¯), <span lang="grc">τοιοῦτος</span> (˘:¯˘), <span lang="grc">ποιῶ</span> (˘¯), often written <span lang="grc">ποῶ</span> in inscriptions (cp. [43](01-the-letters.md#s43)).
