# ACCENT: GENERAL PRINCIPLES

<!-- page 37 -->

##### <a id="s149"></a> §149

There are three accents in Greek. No Greek accent can stand farther back than the antepenult.

\1. **Acute** (/): over short or long vowels and diphthongs. It may stand on ultima, penult, or antepenult: <span lang="grc">καλός, δαίμων, ἄνθρωπος</span>.

\2. **Circumflex** (=): over vowels long by nature and diphthongs. It may stand on ultima or penult: <span lang="grc">γῆ, θεοῦ, δῶρον, τοῦτο</span>.

\3. **Grave** (\\): over short or long vowels and diphthongs. It stands on the ultima only: <span lang="grc">τὸν ἄνδρα, τὴν τύχην, οἱ θεοὶ τῆς ῾ Ελλάδος</span>.

##### <a id="s150"></a> §150

The acute marks syllables pronounced in a raised tone. The grave is a low-pitched tone as contrasted with the acute. The circumflex combines acute and grave.

##### <a id="s151"></a> §151

Accented syllables in Ancient Greek had a higher _pitch_ (<span lang="grc">τόνος</span>) than unaccented syllables, and it was the rising and falling of the pitch that made Ancient Greek a musical language. The Greek word for _accent_ is <span lang="grc">προσῳδίᾱ</span> (Lat. _accentus:_ from _ad-cano_), i.e. ‘song accompanying words.’ Musical accent (elevation and depression of tone) is to be distinguished from quantity (duration of tone), and from rhythmic accent (stress of voice at fixed intervals when there is a regular sequence of long and short syllables).

N.—The accent heard in Modern Greek and English is a _stress-accent_. Stress is produced by strong and weak expiration, and takes account of accented syllables to the neglect of the quantity of unaccented syllables. Thus, shortly after Christ, <span lang="grc">ἄνθρωπος</span> was often pronounced like a dactyl, <span lang="grc">φίλος</span> like a trochee; and <span lang="grc">πρόσωπον, ἐννέα</span>, were even written <span lang="grc">πρόσοπον, έννήα</span>.

##### <a id="s152"></a> §152

The marks of accent are placed over the vowel of the accented syllable. A diphthong has the accent over its second vowel (<span lang="grc">τοῦτο</span>), except in the case of capital <span lang="grc">ᾳ, ῃ, ῳ</span> (as <span lang="grc">῞ Αιδης</span>, 5), where the accent stands before the first vowel.

##### <a id="s153"></a> §153

A breathing is written before the acute and grave (<span lang="grc">οἵ, ἤ</span>), but under the circumflex (<span lang="grc">ὦ, οὗτος</span>). Accents and breathings are placed before capitals: <span lang="grc">῞ Ομηρος, ῟ Ωραι</span>. The accent stands over a mark of diaeresis (8): <span lang="grc">κληῗδι</span>.

##### <a id="s154"></a> §154

The grave is written in place of a final acute on a word that is followed immediately by another word in the sentence. Thus, <span lang="grc">μετὰ τὴν μάχην</span> _after the battle_ (for <span lang="grc">μετά τήν μάχην</span>). It is also sometimes placed on <span lang="grc">τὶς, τὶ</span> ([334](../part-2-inflection/06-pronouns.md#s334)), to distinguish these indefinite pronouns from the interrogatives <span lang="grc">τίς, τί</span>.

**a.** An oxytone ([157](#s157)) changes its acute to the grave when followed by another word, except: (1) when the oxytone is followed by an enclitic ([183 a](#s183)); (2) in <span lang="grc">τίς, τί</span> interrogative, as <span lang="grc">τίς οὗτος</span>; _who's this?_ (3) when an elided syllable follows <!-- page 38 --> the accented syllable: <span lang="grc">νύχθ᾽ ὅλην</span> ([124](03-euphony-of-consonants.md#s124)), not <span lang="grc">νὺχθ᾽ ὅλην</span> ([174 a](#s174)); (4) when a colon or period follows. (Usage varies before a comma.)

##### <a id="s155"></a> §155

The ancients regarded the grave originally as belonging to every syllable not accented with the acute or circumflex; and some Mss. show this in practice, e.g. <span lang="grc">πὰγκρὰτής</span>. Later it was restricted to its use as a substitute for a final acute.

##### <a id="s156"></a> §156

The circumflex is formed from the union of the acute and the grave (<span lang="grc">́̀</span> = ^), never from <span lang="grc">̀́</span>. Thus, <span lang="grc">παῖς ῀ πάὶς, εὖ ῀ ἔὺ</span>. Similarly, since every long vowel may be resolved into two short units (_morae_), <span lang="grc">τῶν</span> may be regarded as = <span lang="grc">τόὸν</span>. The circumflex was thus spoken with a rising tone followed by one of lower pitch. <span lang="grc">μοῦσα, δῆμος</span> are thus = <span lang="grc">μόὺσα, δέὲμος; μούσης, δήμου</span> are = <span lang="grc">μὸύσης, δὲέμου</span>. In <span lang="grc">διδοῦσα</span> (i.e. <span lang="grc">διδόὺσα</span>) compared with <span lang="grc">διδούς</span> the accent has receded ([159](#s159)) one _mora_.

**a.** The whole vowel receives the acute when the second short unit of a vowel long by nature is accented: <span lang="grc">Δί̄ ῀ Δὶί</span>.

##### <a id="s157"></a> §157

Words are named according to their accent as follows:

Oxytone (acute on the ultima): <span lang="grc">θήρ, καλός, λελυκώς</span>.

Paroxytone (acute on the penult): <span lang="grc">λύ̄ω, λείπω, λελυκότος</span>.

Proparoxytone (acute on the antepenult): <span lang="grc">ἄνθρωπος, παιδεύομεν</span>.

Perispomenon (circumflex on the ultima): <span lang="grc">γῆ, θεοῦ</span>.

Properispomenon (circumflex on the penult): <span lang="grc">πρᾶξις, μοῦσα</span>.

Barytone (when the ultima is unaccented, [158](#s158)): <span lang="grc">μοῦσα, μήτηρ, πόλεμος</span>.

##### <a id="s158"></a> §158

A word is called _barytone_ (<span lang="grc">βαρύ-τονος</span> _deep-toned_, _low-toned_) when it has no accent on the ultima. All paroxytones, proparoxytones, and properispomena are also barytones.

##### <a id="s159"></a> §159

An accent is called _recessive_ when it moves back as far from the end of the word as the quantity of the ultima permits ([166](#s166)). The quantity of the _penult_ is here disregarded (<span lang="grc">τρέπωμεν</span>). Cp. [178](#s178).

##### <a id="s160"></a> §160

_Oxytone_ (<span lang="grc">ὀξύς</span>, _sharp_ + <span lang="grc">τόνος</span>) means ‘sharp-toned,’ _perispomenon_ (<span lang="grc">περισπώμενος</span>) ‘turned-around’ (_circumflectus_, [156](#s156)). _Paroxytone_ and _proparoxytone_ are derived from <span lang="grc">ὀξύτονος</span> with the prepositions <span lang="grc">παρά</span> and <span lang="grc">πρό</span> respectively. _Acute_ corresponds to Lat. _acutus_ (<span lang="grc">ὀξεῖα</span>, _scil_. <span lang="grc">προσῳδίᾱ</span>).

##### <a id="s161"></a> §161

The invention of the marks of accent is attributed to Aristophanes of Byzantium, librarian at Alexandria about 200 B.C. The use of signs served to fix the correct accentuation, which was becoming uncertain in the third century B.C.; marked the variation of dialect usage; and rendered the acquisition of Greek easier for foreigners. The signs for the accents (and the breathings) were not regularly employed in Mss. till after 600 A.D.

##### <a id="s162"></a> §162

The position of the accent has to be learned by observation. But the kind of accent is determined by the following rules.

##### <a id="s162D"></a> §162 D

\1. Aeolic has recessive ([159](#s159)) accent in all words except prepositions and conjunctions. Thus, <span lang="grc">σόφος, Ζεῦς</span>, i.e. <span lang="grc">Ζέὺς, αὖτος, λίπειν</span> (= <span lang="grc">λιπεῖν</span>), <span lang="grc">λίποντος</span> (= <span lang="grc">λιπόντος</span>), <span lang="grc">ἄμμες</span> (= <span lang="grc">ἡμεῖς</span>).

\2. Doric regarded final <span lang="grc">-οι</span> ([169](#s169)) as long (<span lang="grc">ἀνθρώποι</span>), and probably <span lang="grc">-αι</span> in nouns (<span lang="grc">χώραι</span>); made paroxytones the 3 pl. act. of the past tenses (<span lang="grc">ἐφέρον, ἐλύ̄σαν</span>) and such words as <span lang="grc">παίδες, γυναίκες, πτώκας</span>; made perispomena the gen. masc. pl. of pronouns (<span lang="grc">τουτῶν, ἀλλῶν</span>) and the gen. fem. pl. of adj. in <span lang="grc">-ος</span> (<span lang="grc">ἀμφοτερᾶν</span>). The substitution, in the accus. pl., of <span lang="grc">-ᾰς</span> and <span lang="grc">-ος</span> for <span lang="grc">-ᾱς</span> and <span lang="grc">-ους</span>, caused no change in the accent (<span lang="grc">πά̄σᾰς, ἀμπέλος</span>). <!-- page 39 -->

##### <a id="s163"></a> §163

The antepenult, if accented, can have the acute only (<span lang="grc">ἄνθρωπος, βασίλεια</span> _queen_, <span lang="grc">οἰκοφύλακος</span> _of a house-guard_). If the ultima is long, either by nature or by position ([144](06-syllables-and-their-quantity.md#s144)), the antepenult cannot take an accent: hence <span lang="grc">ἀνθρώπου</span> ([176 a](#s176)), <span lang="grc">βασιλείᾱ</span> _kingdom_, <span lang="grc">οίκοφύλαξ</span>.

**a.** Some nouns in <span lang="grc">-εως</span> and <span lang="grc">-εων</span> admit the acute on the antepenult. Thus, the genitive of nouns in <span lang="grc">-ις</span> and <span lang="grc">-υς</span> (<span lang="grc">πόλεως, πόλεων, ἄστεως</span>), the forms of the _Attic_ declension, as <span lang="grc">ἵ̄λεως</span> ([289](../part-2-inflection/04-substantives/07-adjectives.md#s289)). So the Ionic genitive in <span lang="grc">-εω</span> (<span lang="grc">πολί̄τεω</span>); also some compound adjectives in <span lang="grc">-ως</span>, as <span lang="grc">δύσερως</span> _unhappy in love_, <span lang="grc">ὑψίκερως</span> _lofty antlered_. On <span lang="grc">ὧντινων</span> see [186](#s186).

##### <a id="s164"></a> §164

The penult, if accented and long, takes the circumflex when the ultima is short by nature (<span lang="grc">νῆσος, ταῦτα</span>). In all other cases it has the acute (<span lang="grc">φόβος, λελυκότος, τούτου</span>).

**a.** Apparent exceptions are <span lang="grc">ὥστε, οὔτις, ἥδε</span> (properly <span lang="grc">ἧδε</span>). See [186](#s186).

**b.** A final syllable containing a vowel short by nature followed by <span lang="grc">ξ</span> or <span lang="grc">ψ</span> does not permit the acute to stand on the antepenult (<span lang="grc">οἰκοφύλαξ</span>); but the circumflex may stand on the penult (<span lang="grc">κῆρυξ</span>).

##### <a id="s165"></a> §165

The ultima, if accented and short, has the acute (<span lang="grc">ποταμός</span>); if accented and long, has either the acute (<span lang="grc">λελυκώς</span>), or the circumflex (<span lang="grc">Περικλῆς</span>).

##### <a id="s166"></a> §166

When the ultima is long, the acute cannot stand on the antepenult, nor the circumflex on the penult. Thus, <span lang="grc">ἄνθρωπου</span> and <span lang="grc">δῶρου</span> are impossible.

##### <a id="s167"></a> §167

When the ultima is short, a word, if accented

**a.** on the ultima, has the acute: <span lang="grc">σοφός</span>.

**b.** on a short penult, has the acute: <span lang="grc">νόμος</span>.

**c.** on a long penult, has the circumflex: <span lang="grc">δῶρον</span>.

**d.** on the antepenult, has the acute: <span lang="grc">ἄνθρωπος</span>.

##### <a id="s168"></a> §168

When the ultima is long, a word, if accented

**a.** on the ultima, has the acute or the circumflex: <span lang="grc">ἐγώ, σοφῶς</span>.

**b.** on the penult, has the acute: <span lang="grc">λέων, δαίμων</span>.

##### <a id="s169"></a> §169

Final <span lang="grc">-αι</span> and <span lang="grc">-οι</span> are regarded as short: <span lang="grc">μοῦσαι, βούλομαι, πρόπαλαι, ἄνθρωποι</span>. But in the optative <span lang="grc">-αι</span> and <span lang="grc">-οι</span> are long (<span lang="grc">λύ̄σαι, βουλεύοι</span>), as in contracted syllables. So also in the locative <span lang="grc">οἴκοι</span> _at home_ (but <span lang="grc">οἶκοι</span> _houses_).

**a.** The difference in the quantitative treatment of <span lang="grc">-αι</span> and <span lang="grc">-οι</span> depends on an original difference of accentuation that may have vanished in Greek. <span lang="grc">-αι</span> and <!-- page 40 --> <span lang="grc">-οι</span>, when short, were pronounced with a clipped, or simple, tone; when long, with a drawled, or compound, tone.

##### <a id="s170"></a> §170

The quantity of <span lang="grc">α, ι, υ</span> ([147](06-syllables-and-their-quantity.md#s147)) may often be learned from the accent. Thus, in <span lang="grc">θάλαττα, ἥμισυς, πῆχυς, δύναμις, μῆνις</span>, the vowel of the last syllable must be short; in <span lang="grc">φίλος</span> the <span lang="grc">ι</span> must be short (otherwise <span lang="grc">φῖλος</span>). Cp. [163](#s163).

## ACCENT AS AFFECTED BY CONTRACTION, CRASIS, AND ELISION

##### <a id="s171"></a> §171

**Contraction**.—If either of the syllables to be contracted had an accent, the contracted syllable has an accent. Thus:

**a.** A contracted antepenult has the acute: <span lang="grc">φιλεόμενος ῀ φιλούμενος</span>.

**b.** A contracted penult has the circumflex when the ultima is short; the acute, when the ultima is long: <span lang="grc">φιλέουσι ῀ φιλοῦσι, φιλεόντων ῀ φιλούντων</span>.

**c.** A contracted ultima has the acute when the uncontracted form was oxytone: <span lang="grc">ἑσταώς ῀ ἑστώς</span>; otherwise, the circumflex: <span lang="grc">φιλέω ῀ φιλῶ</span>.

N. 1.—A contracted syllable has the circumflex only when, in the uncontracted form, an acute was _followed_ by the (unwritten) grave ([155](#s155), [156](#s156)). Thus, <span lang="grc">Περικλέὴς ῀ Περικλῆς, τῑμάὼ ῀ τῑμῶ</span>. In all other cases we have the acute: <span lang="grc">φιλὲόντων ῀ φιλούντων, βεβὰώς ῀ βεβώς</span>.

N. 2.—Exceptions to 171 are often due to the analogy of other forms ([236 a](../part-2-inflection/04-substantives/03-contracted-substantives.md#s236), [264](../part-2-inflection/04-substantives/05-third-declension.md#s264) e, 279 a, 290 c, 309 a).

##### <a id="s172"></a> §172

If neither of the syllables to be contracted had an accent, the contracted syllable has no accent: <span lang="grc">φίλεε ῀ φίλει, γένεϊ ῀ γένει, περίπλοος ῀ περίπλους</span>. For exceptions, see [236 b](../part-2-inflection/04-substantives/03-contracted-substantives.md#s236).

##### <a id="s173"></a> §173

**Crasis**.—In crasis, the first word (as less important) loses its accent: <span lang="grc">τἀ̄γαθά</span> for <span lang="grc">τὰ ἀγαθά, τἀ̄ν</span> for <span lang="grc">τὰ ἐν, κἀ̄γώ</span> for <span lang="grc">καὶ ἐγώ</span>.

**a.** If the second word is a dissyllabic paroxytone with short ultima, it is uncertain whether, in crasis, the paroxytone remains or changes to properispomenon. In this book <span lang="grc">τοὔργον, τἄ̄λλα</span> are written for <span lang="grc">τὸ ἔργον, τὰ ἄλλα</span>; but many scholars write <span lang="grc">τοὖργον, τἆλλα</span>.

##### <a id="s174"></a> §174

**Elision**.—In elision, oxytone prepositions and conjunctions lose their accent: <span lang="grc">παρ᾽</span> (for <span lang="grc">παρὰ</span>) <span lang="grc">ἐμοῦ, ἀλλ᾽</span> (for <span lang="grc">ἀλλὰ</span>) <span lang="grc">ἐγώ</span>. In other oxytones the accent is thrown back to the penult: <span lang="grc">πόλλ᾽</span> (for <span lang="grc">πολλὰ</span>) <span lang="grc">ἔπαθον</span>.

**a.** Observe that in <span lang="grc">πόλλ᾽ ἔπαθον</span> the acute is not changed to the grave ([154 a](#s154), [3](01-the-letters.md#s3)). A circumflex does not result from the recession of the accent. Thus, <span lang="grc">φήμ᾽</span> (not <span lang="grc">φῆμ᾽</span>) <span lang="grc">ἐγώ</span> for <span lang="grc">φημὶ ἐγώ. τινά</span> and <span lang="grc">ποτέ</span>, after a word which cannot receive their accent ([183 d](#s183)), drop their accent: <span lang="grc">οὕτω ποτ᾽ ἦν</span>.

## ANASTROPHE

##### <a id="s175"></a> §175

Anastrophe (<span lang="grc">ἀναστροφή</span> _turning-back_) occurs in the case of oxytone prepositions of two syllables, which throw the accent back on the first syllable. <!-- page 41 -->

**a.** When the preposition follows its case: <span lang="grc">τούτων πἐρι</span> (for <span lang="grc">περὶ τούτων</span>) _about these things_. No other preposition than <span lang="grc">περί</span> follows its case in prose.

N. 1.—In poetry anastrophe occurs with the other dissyllabic prepositions (except <span lang="grc">ἀντί, ἀμφί, διά</span>). In Homer a preposition following its verb and separated from it by tmesis ([1650](../part-4-syntax/19-prepositions.md#s1650)) also admits anastrophe (<span lang="grc">λούσῃ ἄπο</span> for <span lang="grc">ἀπολούσῃ</span>).

N. 2.—When the final vowel of the preposition is elided, the accent is dropped if no mark of punctuation intervenes: <span lang="grc">χερσὶν ὑφ᾽ ἡμετέρῃσιν</span> B 374.

**b.** When a preposition stands for a compound formed of the preposition and <span lang="grc">ἐστί</span>. Thus, <span lang="grc">πάρα</span> for <span lang="grc">πάρεστι</span> _it is permitted_, <span lang="grc">ἔνι</span> for <span lang="grc">ἔνεστι</span> _it is possible_ (<span lang="grc">ἐνί</span> is a poetic form of <span lang="grc">ἐν</span>).

N.—In poetry, <span lang="grc">πάρα</span> may stand for <span lang="grc">πάρεισι</span> or <span lang="grc">πάρειμι</span>; and <span lang="grc">ἄνα</span> _arise! up!_ is used for <span lang="grc">ἀνάστηθι</span>. Hom. has <span lang="grc">ἔνι ῀ ἔνεισι</span>.

## CHANGE OF ACCENT IN DECLENSION, INFLECTION, AND COMPOSITION

##### <a id="s176"></a> §176

When a short ultima of the nominative is lengthened in an oblique case

**a.** a proparoxytone becomes paroxytone: <span lang="grc">θάλαττα θαλάττης, ἄνθρωπος ἀνθρώπου</span>.

**b.** a properispomenon becomes paroxytone: <span lang="grc">μοῦσα μούσης, δῶρον δώρον</span>.

**c.** an oxytone becomes perispomenon in the genitive and dative of the second declension: <span lang="grc">θεός θεοῦ θεῷ θεῶν θεοῖς</span>.

##### <a id="s177"></a> §177

When, for a long ultima, a short ultima is substituted in inflection

**a.** a dissyllabic paroxytone (with penult long by nature) becomes properispomenon: <span lang="grc">λύ̄ω λῦε</span>.

**b.** a polysyllabic paroxytone (with penult either long or short) becomes proparoxytone: <span lang="grc">παιδεύω παίδευε, πλέκω πλέκομεν</span>.

##### <a id="s178"></a> §178

In composition the accent is usually recessive ([159](#s159)) in the case of substantives and adjectives, regularly in the case of verbs: <span lang="grc">βάσις ἀνάβασις, θεός ἄθεος, λῦε ἀπόλῡε</span>.

**a.** Proper names having the form of a substantive, adjective, or participle, usually change the accent: <span lang="grc">Ἔλπις</span> (<span lang="grc">ἐλπίς</span>), <span lang="grc">Γλαῦκος</span> (<span lang="grc">γλαυκός</span>), <span lang="grc">Γέλων</span> (<span lang="grc">γελῶν</span>).

**b.** Special cases will be considered under Declension and Inflection.

## PROCLITICS

##### <a id="s179"></a> §179

Ten monosyllabic words have no accent and are closely connected with the following word. They are called _proclitics_ (from <span lang="grc">προκλί̄νω</span> _lean forward_). They are:

The forms of the article beginning with a vowel (<span lang="grc">ὁ, ἡ, οἱ, αἱ</span>); the prepositions <span lang="grc">ἐν, εἰς</span> (<span lang="grc">ἐς</span>), <span lang="grc">ἐξ</span> (<span lang="grc">ἐκ</span>); the conjunction <span lang="grc">εἰ</span> _if_; <span lang="grc">ὡς</span> _as_, _that_ (also a preposition _to_); the negative adverb <span lang="grc">οὐ</span> (<span lang="grc">οὐκ, οὐχ</span>, [137](05-movable-consonants.md#s137)). <!-- page 42 -->

##### <a id="s180"></a> §180

A proclitic sometimes takes an accent, thus:

**a.** <span lang="grc">οὐ</span> at the end of a sentence: <span lang="grc">φῄς, ἢ οὔ</span>; _do you say so or not?_ <span lang="grc">πῶς γὰρ οὔ</span>; _for why not?_ Also <span lang="grc">οὔ</span> _no_ standing alone.

**b.** <span lang="grc">ἐξ, ἐν</span>, and <span lang="grc">εἰς</span> receive an acute in poetry when they follow the word to which they belong and stand at the end of the verse: <span lang="grc">κακῶν ἔξ</span> _out of evils_ <cite data-ref="Hom. Il. 14.472"><span lang="grc">Ξ</span> 472</cite>.

**c.** <span lang="grc">ὡς</span> _as_ becomes <span lang="grc">ὥς</span> in poetry when it follows its noun: <span lang="grc">θεὸς ὥς</span> _as a god_. <span lang="grc">ὡς</span> standing for <span lang="grc">οὕτως</span> is written <span lang="grc">ὥς</span> even in prose (<span lang="grc">οὐδ᾽ ὥς</span> _not even thus_).

**d.** When the proclitic precedes an enclitic ([183 e](#s183)): <span lang="grc">ἔν τισι</span>.

N.—<span lang="grc">ὁ</span> used as a relative (for <span lang="grc">ὅς</span>, [1105](../part-4-syntax/16-the-article-origin-and-development.md#s1105)) is written <span lang="grc">ὅ</span>. On <span lang="grc">ὅ</span> demonstrative see [1114](../part-4-syntax/16-the-article-origin-and-development.md#s1114).

## ENCLITICS

##### <a id="s181"></a> §181

Enclitics (from <span lang="grc">ἐγκλί̄νω</span> _lean on, upon_) are words attaching themselves closely to the preceding word, after which they are pronounced rapidly. Enclitics usually lose their accent. They are:

**a.** The personal pronouns <span lang="grc">μοῦ, μοί, μέ; σοῦ, σοί, σέ; οὗ, οἷ, ἕ</span>, and (in poetry) <span lang="grc">σφίσι</span>.

**b.** The indefinite pronoun <span lang="grc">τὶς, τὶ</span> in all cases (including <span lang="grc">τοῦ, τῷ</span> for <span lang="grc">τινός, τινί</span>, but excluding <span lang="grc">ἄττα ῀ τινά</span>); the indefinite adverbs <span lang="grc">πού</span> (or <span lang="grc">ποθί</span>), <span lang="grc">πῄ, ποί, ποθέν, ποτέ, πώ, πώς</span>. When used as interrogatives these words are not enclitic (<span lang="grc">τίς, τί, ποῦ</span> (or <span lang="grc">πόθι</span>), <span lang="grc">πῇ, ποῖ, πόθεν, πότε, πῶ, πῶς</span>).

**c.** All dissyllabic forms of the present indicative of <span lang="grc">εἰμί</span> _am_ and <span lang="grc">φημί</span> _say_ (i.e. all except <span lang="grc">εἶ</span> and <span lang="grc">φῄς</span>).

**d.** The particles <span lang="grc">γέ, τέ, τοί, πέρ</span>; the inseparable <span lang="grc">-δε</span> in <span lang="grc">ὅδε, τοσόσδε</span>, etc.

N.—Enclitics, when they retain their accent, are called _orthotone_. See [187](#s187).

##### <a id="s181D"></a> §181 D

Also enclitic are the dialectic and poetical forms <span lang="grc">μεῦ, σέο, σεῦ, τοί, τέ</span>, and <span lang="grc">τύ</span> (accus. = <span lang="grc">σέ</span>), <span lang="grc">ἕο, εὗ, ἕθεν, μίν, νίν, σφί, σφίν, σφέ, σφωέ, σφωί̄ν, σφέων, σφέας, σφά̆ς</span> and <span lang="grc">σφᾶς, σφέα</span>; also the particles <span lang="grc">νύ</span> or <span lang="grc">νύν</span> (not <span lang="grc">νῦν</span>), Epic <span lang="grc">κέ</span> (<span lang="grc">κέν</span>), <span lang="grc">θήν, ῥά</span>; and Epic <span lang="grc">ἐσσί</span>, Ion. <span lang="grc">εἶς</span>, _thou art_.

##### <a id="s182"></a> §182

The accent of an enclitic, when it is thrown back upon the preceding word, always appears as an acute: <span lang="grc">θήρ τε</span> (not <span lang="grc">θῆρ τε</span>) from <span lang="grc">θήρ ¨ τέ</span>.

##### <a id="s183"></a> §183

The word preceding an enclitic is treated as follows:

**a.** An oxytone keeps its accent, and does not change an acute to a grave ([154 a](#s154)): <span lang="grc">δός μοι, καλόν ἐστι</span>.

**b.** A perispomenon keeps its accent: <span lang="grc">φιλῶ σε, τῑμῶν τινων</span>.

**c.** A proparoxytone or properispomenon receives, as an additional accent, the acute on the ultima: <span lang="grc">ἄνθρωπός τις, ἄνθρωποί τινες, ἤκουσά τινων; σῶσόν με, παῖδές τινες</span>.

**d.** A paroxytone receives no additional accent: a monosyllabic enclitic loses its accent (<span lang="grc">χώρᾱ τις, φίλος μου</span>), a dissyllabic enclitic retains its accent (<span lang="grc">χώρᾱς τινός, φίλοι τινές</span>) except when its final vowel is elided ([174 a](#s174)). <!-- page 43 -->

N.—Like paroxytones are treated properispomena ending in <span lang="grc">ξ</span> or <span lang="grc">ψ</span> when followed by a dissyllabic enclitic: <span lang="grc">κῆρυξ ἐστί</span>; and so probably <span lang="grc">κῆρυξ τις</span>.

**e.** A proclitic ([179](#s179)) takes an acute: <span lang="grc">ἔν τινι, εἴ τινες</span>.

##### <a id="s184"></a> §184

Since an enclitic, on losing its accent, forms a part of the preceding word, the writing <span lang="grc">ἄνθρωπος τις</span> would violate the rule ([149](#s149)) that no word can be accented on a syllable before the antepenult. A paroxytone receives no additional accent in order that two successive syllables may not have the acute (not <span lang="grc">φίλός ἐστιν</span>).

##### <a id="s185"></a> §185

When several enclitics occur in succession, each receives an accent from the following, only the last having no accent: <span lang="grc">εἴ πού τίς τινα ἴδοι ἐχθρόν</span> _if ever any one saw an enemy anywhere_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 4.47">T. 4.47</cite>.

##### <a id="s186"></a> §186

Sometimes an enclitic unites with a preceding word to form a compound (cp. Lat. _-que_, _-ve_), which is accented as if the enclitic were still a separate word. Thus, <span lang="grc">οὔτε</span> (not <span lang="grc">οὖτε</span>), <span lang="grc">ὥστε, εἴτε, καίτοι, οὗτινος, ᾧτινι, ὧντινων</span>; usually <span lang="grc">περ</span> (<span lang="grc">ἕσπερ</span>); and the inseparable <span lang="grc">-δε</span> in <span lang="grc">ὅδε, τούσδε, οἴκαδε</span>; and <span lang="grc">-θε</span> and <span lang="grc">-χι</span> in <span lang="grc">εἴθε</span> (poetic <span lang="grc">αἴθε</span>), <span lang="grc">ναίχι. οὔτε, ᾧτινι</span>, etc., are not real exceptions to the rules of accent ([163](#s163), [164](#s164)).

**a.** <span lang="grc">οἷός τε</span> _able_ is sometimes written <span lang="grc">οἷόστε. οὐκ οὖν</span> is usually written <span lang="grc">οὔκουν</span> _not therefore_ , and _not therefore?_ in distinction from <span lang="grc">οὐκοῦν</span> _therefore_. <span lang="grc">ἐγώ γε</span> and <span lang="grc">ἐμοί γε</span> may become <span lang="grc">ἔγωγε, ἔμοιγε</span>.

##### <a id="s187"></a> §187

An enclitic retains its accent (is orthotone, cp. [181](#s181) N.):

**a.** When it is emphatic, as in contrasts: <span lang="grc">ἢ σοὶ ἢ τῷ πατρί σου</span> _either to you or to your father_ (<span lang="grc">ἐμοῦ, ἐμοί, ἐμέ</span> are emphatic: <span lang="grc">εἰπὲ καὶ ἐμοί</span> _tell me too_), and at the beginning of a sentence or clause: <span lang="grc">φημὶ γάρ</span> _I say in fact_.

**b.** <span lang="grc">ἐστί</span> is written <span lang="grc">ἔστι</span> at the beginning of a sentence; when it expresses existence or possibility; when it follows <span lang="grc">οὐκ, μή, εἰ, ὡς, καί, ἀλλά</span> (or <span lang="grc">ἀλλ᾽</span>), <span lang="grc">τοῦτο</span> (or <span lang="grc">τοῦτ᾽</span>); and in <span lang="grc">ἔστιν οἵ</span> _some_, <span lang="grc">ἔστιν ὅτε</span> _sometimes_. Thus, <span lang="grc">εἰ ἔστιν οὕτως</span> _if it is so_, <span lang="grc">τοῦτο δ ἔστι</span> _that which exists_.

**c.** In the phrases <span lang="grc">ποτὲ μὲν . . . ποτὲ δέ, τινὲς μὲν . . . τινὲς δέ</span>.

**d.** After a word suffering elision: <span lang="grc">πολλοὶ δ᾽ εἰσίν</span> (for <span lang="grc">δέ εἰσιν</span>), <span lang="grc">ταῦτ᾽ ἐστί</span>.

**e.** When a dissyllabic enclitic follows a paroxytone ([183 d](#s183)).

N. 1.—When they are used as indirect reflexives in Attic prose ([1228](../part-4-syntax/17-pronouns.md#s1228)), the pronouns of the third person <span lang="grc">οὗ</span> and <span lang="grc">σφίσι</span> are orthotone, <span lang="grc">οἷ</span> is generally enclitic, while <span lang="grc">ἕ</span> is generally orthotone.

N. 2.—After oxytone prepositions and <span lang="grc">ἕνεκα</span> enclitic pronouns (except <span lang="grc">τὶς</span>) usually keep their accent (<span lang="grc">ἐπὶ σοί</span>, not <span lang="grc">ἐπί σοι; ἕνεκα σοῦ</span>, not <span lang="grc">ἕνεκά σου; ἕνεκά του</span>, not <span lang="grc">ἕνεκα τοῦ</span>). <span lang="grc">ἐμοῦ, ἐμοί, ἐμέ</span> are used after prepositions (except <span lang="grc">πρός με</span>; and in the drama <span lang="grc">ἀμφί μοι</span>).
