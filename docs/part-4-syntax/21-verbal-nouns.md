# VERBAL NOUNS

\1. The Infinitive. 2. The Participle. 3. The Verbal Adjectives in <span lang="grc">-τός</span> and <span lang="grc">-τέος</span>.

## THE INFINITIVE

##### <a id="s1966"></a> §1966

The infinitive is in part a verb, in part a substantive.

**a.** Many substantives are closely related to verbs, but not all verbs can form substantives. All verbs can, however, form infinitives.

**b.** The word _infinitive_ denotes a verbal form without any limitations (_finis_) of number and person.

##### <a id="s1967"></a> §1967

The infinitive is like a verb herein: <!-- page 438 -->

**a.** It shows the distinctions of voice and tense (but not those of number and person). Having tenses, it can express different stages of action (action simply occurring, continuing, or finished); whereas the corresponding substantive sets forth the abstract idea without these distinctions. Contrast <span lang="grc">ποιεῖν, ποιήσειν, ποιῆσαι, πεποιηκέναι</span> with <span lang="grc">ποίησις</span> _making_.

**b.** It can have a subject before it and a predicate after it, and it can have an object in the genitive, dative, or accusative like the corresponding finite verb. Infinitives scarcely ever stand in the subjective genitive; and the object of an infinitive never stands in the objective genitive.

**c.** It is modified by adverbs, not by adjectives.

**d.** It may take <span lang="grc">ἄν</span> and with that particle represent <span lang="grc">ἄν</span> with the indicative ([1784 f](20-the-verb-voices/07-the-moods-in-simple-sentences.md#s1784)f.) or <span lang="grc">ἄν</span> with the optative ([1824](20-the-verb-voices/07-the-moods-in-simple-sentences.md#s1824)).

**e.** It forms clauses of result with <span lang="grc">ὥστε</span>, and temporal clauses with <span lang="grc">πρίν</span>, etc.

##### <a id="s1968"></a> §1968

The infinitive is like a substantive herein:

**a.** It may be the subject or object of a verb.

**b.** With the (neuter) article it shows all the case forms (except the vocative): <span lang="grc">τὸ</span> (<span lang="grc">τοῦ, τῷ, τὸ</span>) <span lang="grc">λύ̄ειν, λύ̄σειν</span>, etc.

**c.** It may be governed by prepositions: <span lang="grc">πρὸ τοῦ λύ̄ειν</span>.

##### <a id="s1969"></a> §1969

The infinitive was originally a verbal noun in the dative (in part possibly also in the locative) case. The use to express purpose ([2008](#s2008)) is a survival of the primitive meaning, from which all the other widely diverging uses were developed in a manner no longer always clear to us. But the _to_ or _for_ meaning seen in <span lang="grc">μανθάνειν ἥκομεν</span> _we have come to learn_ (_for learning_) can also be discerned in <span lang="grc">δύναμαι ἰδεῖν</span> _I have power for seeing_, then _I can see_. Cp. [2000](#s2000), [2006](#s2006) **a.** As early as Homer, when the datival meaning had been in part obscured, the infinitive was employed as nominative (as subject) and accusative (as object). After Homer, the infinitive came to be used with the neuter article, the substantive idea thus gaining in definiteness. The article must be used when the infinitive stands as an object in the genitive or dative, and when it depends on prepositions.

##### <a id="s1970"></a> §1970

The infinitive is used as subject, as predicate, and to supplement the meaning of words and clauses.

##### <a id="s1971"></a> §1971

The negative of the infinitive is <span lang="grc">μή</span>; but <span lang="grc">οὐ</span>, used with a finite mood in direct discourse, is retained when that mood becomes infinitive in indirect discourse. Sometimes, however, <span lang="grc">μή</span> is used in place of this <span lang="grc">οὐ</span> ([2723 f](35-negative-sentences.md#s2723)f.).

### SUBJECT AND PREDICATE NOUN WITH THE INFINITIVE

##### <a id="s1972"></a> §1972

In general the subject of the infinitive, if expressed at all, stands in the accusative; when the subject of the infinitive is the same as the subject or object of the governing verb, or when it has already been made known in the sentence, it is not repeated with the infinitive.

##### <a id="s1973"></a> §1973

When the subject of the infinitive is the same as that of the governing verb, it is omitted, and a predicate noun stands in the nominative case. <!-- page 439 -->

<span lang="grc">οἶμαι εἰδέναι</span> _I think that I know_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Prot. 312e">P. Pr. 312e</cite>, <span lang="grc">Πέρσης ἔφη εἶναι</span> _he said he was a Persian_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 4.4.17">X. A. 4.4.17</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἐγὼ οὐχ ὁμολογήσω ἄκλητος ἥκειν</span> _I shall not admit that I have come uninvited_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Sym. 174d">P. S. 174d</cite>, <span lang="grc">ὁμολογεῖς περὶ ἐμὲ ἄδικος γεγενῆσθαι</span>; _do you admit that you have been guilty as regards me?_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 1.6.8">X. A. 1.6.8</cite> (cp. [4](../part-1-letters-sounds-syllables-accent/01-the-letters.md#s4).2.27 in [2263](29-adverbial-complex-sentences-2193-2487/06-omegasigmatauepsilon-rarely-omegasigma-with-the-infinitive.md#s2263)).

**a.** The nominative is used when the infinitive, expressing some action or state of the subject of the main verb, has the article in an oblique case. Thus, <span lang="grc">τούτων ἀξιωθεὶς διὰ τὸ πατρικὸς αὐτῷ φίλος εί̂ναι</span> _justifying these_ requests _on the ground that he was his hereditary friend_ <cite data-ref="Aeschin. 3.52">Aes. 3.52</cite>, <span lang="grc">τοῦτο δ᾽ ἐποίει ἐκ τοῦ χαλεπὸς εἶναι</span> _this he effected by reason of his being severe_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 2.6.9">X. A. 2.6.9</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἐπὶ τῷ ὁμοῖοι τοῖς λειπομένοις εἶναι ἐκπέμπονται</span> (colonists) _are sent out to be the equals of those who stay at home_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 1.34">T. 1.34</cite>.

**b.** The nominative stands usually in sentences with <span lang="grc">δεῖν, χρῆναι</span> etc., dependent on a _verb of saying_ or _thinking_. Thus, <span lang="grc">ἡγούμην . . . περιεῖναι δεῖν αὐτῶν καὶ μεγαλοψῡχότερος φαίνεσθαι</span> _I thought I ought to surpass them and to show myself more magnificent_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 19.235">D. 19.235</cite>. Here <span lang="grc">ἡγούμην δεῖν</span> is equivalent to _I thought it proper_.

**c.** When the governing verb is a participle in an oblique case, a predicate noun usually agrees with the participle, and rarely stands in the nominative. Thus, <span lang="grc">ἀπαλλαγεὶς τούτων τῶν φασκόντων δικαστῶν εἶναι</span> _being rid of those who profess to be judges_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Apol. 41a">P. A. 41a</cite>, <span lang="grc">τὰ̄ς ἀρχὰ̄ς δίδωσι . . . τοῖς ἀεὶ δόξᾱσιν ἀρίστοις εἶναι</span> _it dispenses the offices to those who always seem to be the most deserving_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Menex. 238d">P. Menex. 238d</cite>.

##### <a id="s1974"></a> §1974

A pronoun subject of the infinitive, if (wholly or partially) identical with the subject of the main verb, is generally expressed when emphatic, and stands in the accusative (cases of the nominative are rare and suspected); but the indirect reflexive <span lang="grc">σφεῖς</span> stands in the nominative or accusative.

<span lang="grc">οἶμαι ἐμὲ πλείω χρήματα εἰργάσθαι ἢ ἄλλους σύνδυο</span> _I think I have made more money than any two others together_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Hipp. Maj. 282e">P. Hipp. M. 282e</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἡγησάμενος ἐμαυτὸν ἐπιεικέστερον εἶναι</span> (emphatic for <span lang="grc">ἡγησάμενος ἐπιεικέστερος εἶναι</span>) _deeming myself to be too honest_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Apol. 36b">P. A. 36b</cite>, <span lang="grc">τοὺς δὲ Θηβαίους ἡγεῖτο . . . ἐά̄σειν ὅπως βούλεται πρά̄ττειν ἑαυτόν</span> _he thought the Thebans would let him have his own way_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 6.9">D. 6.9</cite>, <span lang="grc">οὐ σφεῖς ἀδικεῖσθαι, ἀλλ᾽ ἐκείνους μᾶλλον</span> he said _that not they_ (the speaker and the other Lacedaemonians), _but they_ (the Toroneans) _rather had been wronged_ 4. 114 (but <span lang="grc">σφᾶς</span> in 1228 b).

**a.** After a preceding accusative with the infinitive, a second pronoun referring to a different person, and also subject of an infinitive, must also stand in the accusative whether or not it denotes the same person as the subject of the governing verb. Thus, <span lang="grc">ἀλλὰ νομίζεις ἡμᾶς μὲν ἀνέξεσθαί σου, αὐτὸς</span> (see below) <span lang="grc">δὲ τυπήσειν; καὶ ἡμᾶς μὲν ἀποψηφιεῖσθαί σου, δὲ</span> (_not_ <span lang="grc">σὺ</span>) <span lang="grc">δ᾽ ου᾽ παύσεσθαι</span> _but do you think that we are going to put up with you, while you strike us yourself? and that we are going to acquit you, while you will not cease_ your outrageous conduct? <cite data-ref="Dem. 21.204">D. 21.204</cite>. <span lang="grc">αὐτός</span>, above and in <span lang="grc">Κλέων οὐκ ἔφη αὐτός, ἀλλ᾽ ἐκεῖνον στρατηγεῖν</span> _Cleon said that not he himself, but that_ Nicias _was in command_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 4.28">T. 4.28</cite>, is not the expressed subject of the infinitive, but <span lang="grc">αὐτός</span> of direct discourse (<span lang="grc">αὐτὸς τυπήσεις, αὐτὸς οὐ στρατηγῶ</span>); hence <span lang="grc">αὐτός</span> is _not_ used here for <span lang="grc">σεαυτόν</span> (<span lang="grc">ἑαυτόν</span>).

##### <a id="s1975"></a> §1975

When the subject of the infinitive is different from that of the governing verb, it stands in the accusative; and a predicate noun stands also in the accusative. <!-- page 440 -->

<span lang="grc">νομίζω γὰρ ὑ̄μᾶς ἐμοὶ εἶναι καὶ πατρίδα καὶ φίλους</span> _for I think you are to me both fatherland and friends_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 1.3.6">X. A. 1.3.6</cite>, <span lang="grc">τὸν γὰρ καλὸν κἀ̄γαθὸν ἄνδρα εὐδαίμονα εἶναί φημι</span> _for I maintain that the noble and good man is happy_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Gorg. 470e">P. G. 470e</cite>.

##### <a id="s1976"></a> §1976

A predicate noun takes the case of the subject of an infinitive itself dependent on a subjectless infinitive. Thus, <span lang="grc">ἡμῖν δὲ ποιοῦσι δοκεῖν σφᾶς παντοδαποὺς φαίνεσθαι</span> _they manage it so that they seem to us to appear in various forms_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Rep. 381e">P. R. 381e</cite>.

##### <a id="s1977"></a> §1977

Several infinitives may be used in succession, one infinitive being the subject of another: <span lang="grc">περὶ πολλοῦ ποιούμενος μηδενὶ δόξαι ὑβρίζειν βούλεσθαι</span> _regarding it of great importance not to seem to any one to wish to behave outrageously_ <cite data-ref="Lys. 23.5">L. 23.5</cite>.

##### <a id="s1978"></a> §1978

When the subject of the infinitive is the same as the _object_ (in the genitive or dative) of the governing verb, it is often omitted, and a predicate noun is either attracted into the genitive or dative, or stands in the accusative in agreement with the omitted subject of the infinitive. See [1060](14-adjectives.md#s1060)-1062.

<span lang="grc">ἔξεστιν ἡμῖν ἀγαθοῖς εἶναι</span> or <span lang="grc">ἔξεστιν ἡμῖν ἀγαθοὺς εἶναι</span> _it is in our power to be good_ (lit. _to be good is possible for us_). Thus, <span lang="grc">δεόμεθ᾽ οὖν ὑ̄μῶν . . . ἀκροά̄σασθαι τῶν λεγομένων, ἐνθῡμηθέντας ὅτι κτλ</span>. _we ask you therefore to listen to what is said_, _considering that_, etc. 1. 14. 6. Cp. <span lang="grc">νῦν σοι ἔξεστιν ἀνδρὶ γενέσθαι</span> quoted in 1062 with <span lang="grc">Λακεδαιμονίοις ἔξεστιν ὑ̄μῖν φίλους γενέσθαι</span> _it is in your power to become friends to the Lacedaemonians_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 4.29">T. 4.29</cite>. The latter construction may be explained as abbreviated for <span lang="grc">ἔξεστιν ὑ̄μῖν</span> (<span lang="grc">ὑ̄μᾶς</span>) <span lang="grc">φίλους γενέσθαι</span>.

##### <a id="s1979"></a> §1979

The subject of the infinitive is often retained when it is the same as the (omitted) oblique object of the governing verb. Thus, <span lang="grc">παρήγγειλε τὰ ὅπλα τίθεσθαι τοὺς Ἕλληνας</span> _he issued orders that the Greeks should get under arms_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 2.2.21">X. A. 2.2.21</cite>.

##### <a id="s1980"></a> §1980

An indefinite or general subject of the infinitive (<span lang="grc">τινά, τινάς, ἀνθρώπους</span>) is commonly omitted; and a predicate noun stands in the accusative. Thus, <span lang="grc">φιλάνθρωπον εἶναι δεῖ</span> _one_ (<span lang="grc">τινά</span>) _must be humane_ <cite data-ref="Isoc. 2.15">I. 2.15</cite> (cp. [1984](#s1984)), <span lang="grc">ῥᾷον παραινεῖν ἢ παθόντα καρτερεῖν</span> _it is easier_ for a man _to give advice than to endure suffering_ Men. Sent. 471, <span lang="grc">δρῶντας γὰρ ἢ μὴ δρῶντας ἥδῑον θανεῖν</span> _for it is preferable to die in action rather than doing nothing_ <cite data-ref="Eur. Hel. 814">E. Hel. 814</cite>.

##### <a id="s1981"></a> §1981

The construction of the accusative with the infinitive seems to have originated from the employment of the infinitive to complement the meaning of transitive verbs; as in <span lang="grc">κελεύω σε ἀπελθεῖν</span> _I command you to depart_. Here the accusative was separated from the transitive verb and felt to be the independent subject of the infinitive (_I command that you depart_). Gradually the accusative with the infinitive was used even after verbs incapable of taking an object-accusative.

### PERSONAL AND IMPERSONAL CONSTRUCTION

##### <a id="s1982"></a> §1982

Instead of an impersonal passive verb with the accusative and infinitive as subject, Greek often uses the personal passive construction, the accusative becoming the nominative, subject to the leading verb.

Thus, <span lang="grc">Κῦρος ἠγγέλθη νῑκῆσαι</span> _Cyrus was reported to have conquered_ instead of <!-- page 441 --> <span lang="grc">ἠγγέλθη Κῦρον νῑκῆσαι</span> _it was reported that Cyrus had conquered_, and <span lang="grc">δίκαιός εἰμι ἀπελθεῖν</span> _I am justified in going away_ instead of <span lang="grc">δίκαιόν ἐστιν ἐμὲ ἀπελθεῖν</span> _it is right for me to go away_. English sometimes has to use the impersonal construction in place of the Greek personal construction (cp. [2107](22-the-participle.md#s2107)).

**a.** The personal construction is more common with <span lang="grc">λέγεται, ἀγγέλλεται, ὁμολογεῖται</span> and other passive _verbs of saying_ (regular with passive verbs of _thinking_); with <span lang="grc">συμβαίνει</span> _it happens;_ with <span lang="grc">ἀναγκαῖος</span> _necessary_, <span lang="grc">ἄξιος</span> _worthy_, <span lang="grc">δίκαιος</span> _just_, <span lang="grc">δυνατός</span> _possible_, <span lang="grc">ἐπιτήδειος</span> _fit_, etc., followed by a form of <span lang="grc">εἶναι</span>, instead of <span lang="grc">ἀναγκαῖον, ἄξιον</span>, etc. Thus, <span lang="grc">ὁ Ἀσσύριος εἰς τὴν χώρᾱν αὐτοῦ ἐμβαλεῖν ἀγγέλλεται</span> _the Assyrian is reported to be about to make an incursion into his country_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 5.3.30">X. C. 5.3.30</cite>, <span lang="grc">πολλή τις ἀλογίᾱ ξυμβαίνει γίγνεσθαι</span> _much absurdity would result_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Phileb. 55a">P. Phil. 55a</cite>, <span lang="grc">δίκαιος εἶ εἰπεῖν</span> _it is right for you to speak_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Sym. 214c">P. S. 214c</cite>, <span lang="grc">τὴν αἰτίᾱν οὗτός ἐστι δίκαιος ἔχειν</span> _it is right for him to bear the blame_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 18.4">D. 18.4</cite>. Both constructions together: <span lang="grc">σοὶ γὰρ δὴ λέγεται πάνυ γε τεθεραπεῦσθαι ὁ Ἀπόλλων, καί σε πάντα ἐκείνῳ πειθόμενον πρἀ̄ττειν</span> _for Apollo is said to have been greatly served by you, and_ (it is said) _that you do everything in obedience to him_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 7.2.15">X. C. 7.2.15</cite>. Cp. [2104](22-the-participle.md#s2104).

N.—<span lang="grc">δῆλός ἐστι</span> and <span lang="grc">φανερός ἐστι</span> take <span lang="grc">ὅτι</span> or the participle ([2107](22-the-participle.md#s2107)); <span lang="grc">δῆλόν ἐστι</span> and <span lang="grc">φανερόν ἐστι</span> take <span lang="grc">ὅτι</span>, not the infinitive.

##### <a id="s1983"></a> §1983

The personal constructions <span lang="grc">δοκῶ, ἔοικα</span> ([2089 c](22-the-participle.md#s2089)), <span lang="grc">δέω</span> are regular instead of <span lang="grc">δοκεῖ, ἔοικε</span> _it seems_, <span lang="grc">δεῖ</span> _it lacks_ (much or little). So with <span lang="grc">φαίνομαι</span> for <span lang="grc">φαίνεται</span>.

<span lang="grc">δοκῶ γάρ μοι ἄδυνατος εἶναι</span> _for I seem to be unable_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Rep. 368b">P. R. 368b</cite>, <span lang="grc">δοκοῦμέν μοι καθῆσθαι</span> _it seems to me that we are encamped_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 1.3.12">X. A. 1.3.12</cite>, <span lang="grc">νῦν γε ἡμῶν ἔοικας βασιλεὺς εἶναι</span> _now at least you seem to be our king_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 1.4.6">X. C. 1.4.6</cite>, <span lang="grc">πολλοῦ δέω ἐγὼ ὑπὲρ ἐμαυτοῦ ἀπολογεῖσθαι</span> _I am far from speaking in my own defence_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Apol. 30d">P. A. 30d</cite>, <span lang="grc">μῑκροῦ ἐδέησεν Κύπρον ἅπᾱσαν κατασχεῖν</span> _he almost_ (lacked a little) _occupied the whole of Cyprus_ <cite data-ref="Isoc. 9.62">I. 9.62</cite>, <span lang="grc">εὖ σὺ λέγειν φαίνει</span> _you seem to speak well_ <cite data-ref="Aristoph. Cl. 403">Ar. Nub. 403</cite>.

**a.** <span lang="grc">δοκεῖ μοί τινα ἐλθεῖν</span> for <span lang="grc">δοκεῖ τίς μοι ἐλθεῖν</span> _it seems to me that some one came_ is very rare. <span lang="grc">δοκεῖ</span> meaning _it seems good, it is decreed_ always takes the infinitive ([1984](#s1984), [1991](#s1991)). <span lang="grc">δοκῶ</span> _believe_ has the construction of 1992 c. Cp. [1998](#s1998).

## THE INFINITIVE WITHOUT THE ARTICLE: AS SUBJECT, PREDICATE, AND APPOSITIVE

##### <a id="s1984"></a> §1984

**As Subject**.—The infinitive may be used as subject, especially with quasi-impersonal verbs and expressions ([933 a](07-omission-of-the-subject.md#s933)).

<span lang="grc">γράμματα μαθεῖν δεῖ</span> _to learn to read is necessary_ Men. Sent. 96, <span lang="grc">τί χρὴ ποιεῖν</span>; _what must be done?_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 2.1.16">X. A. 2.1.16</cite>, <span lang="grc">κόσμος</span> (<span lang="grc">ἐστὶ</span>) <span lang="grc">καλῶς τοῦτο δρᾶν</span> _to perform this well is a credit_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 1.5">T. 1.5</cite>, <span lang="grc">πᾶσιν ἁδεῖν χαλεπόν</span> (<span lang="grc">ἐστι</span>) _to please everybody is difficult_ Solon 7, <span lang="grc">ἔδοξεν αὐτοῖς προϊέναι</span> _it seemed best to them to proceed_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 2.1.2">X. A. 2.1.2</cite>, <span lang="grc">συμφέρει αὐτοῖς φίλους εἶναι</span> _it is for their interest to be friends_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Ec. 11.23">X. O. 11.23</cite>. Cp. [1062](14-adjectives.md#s1062), [1978](#s1978).

##### <a id="s1985"></a> §1985

Such quasi-impersonal verbs and expressions are <span lang="grc">δεῖ</span> _it is necessary_, <span lang="grc">χρή</span> (properly a substantive with <span lang="grc">ἐστί</span> omitted, [793](../part-2-inflection/10-verb-stem-and-present-stem/16-irregular-mi-verbs.md#s793)) _it is necessary_, <span lang="grc">δοκεῖ</span> _it seems good_, <span lang="grc">ἔστι</span> _it is possible_, <span lang="grc">ἔξεστι</span> _it is in one's power_, <span lang="grc">οἷόν τέ ἐστι</span> _it is possible_, <span lang="grc">πρέπει</span> and <span lang="grc">προσήκει</span> _it is fitting_, <span lang="grc">συμβαίνει</span> _it happens;_ and many expressions formed by <span lang="grc">ἐστί</span> and a predicate noun, as <span lang="grc">ἄξιον</span> _it is right_, <span lang="grc">δίκαιον</span> _it is just_, <span lang="grc">ἀναγ-</span> <!-- page 442 --> <span lang="grc">καῖον</span> _it is necessary_, <span lang="grc">δυνατόν</span> _it is possible_, <span lang="grc">ἀδύνατον</span> (or <span lang="grc">ἀδύνατα</span>) _it is impossible_, <span lang="grc">αἰσχρόν</span> _it is disgraceful_, <span lang="grc">καλόν</span> _it is honourable_, <span lang="grc">ὥρᾱ</span> and <span lang="grc">καιρός</span> _it is time_. With the last two expressions the old dative use of the infinitive is clear: <span lang="grc">ὥρᾱ βουλεύεσθαι</span> _it is time for considering_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Soph. 241b">P. Soph. 241b</cite>.

**a.** On the personal <span lang="grc">ἄξιός εἰμι, δίκαιός εἰμι, δοκῶ</span>, see [1982](#s1982). For <span lang="grc">δεῖ με τοῦτο λέγειν</span> we find the personal <span lang="grc">δέομαι τοῦτο λέγειν</span>. Note the attraction in <span lang="grc">τὸ πλῆθος τῶν ἐνόντων εἰπεῖν</span> _the number of the things it is possible to mention_ <cite data-ref="Isoc. 5.110">I. 5.110</cite> (for <span lang="grc">τούτων ἃ ἔνεστιν</span>).

**b.** <span lang="grc">δεῖ</span> and <span lang="grc">χρή</span> regularly take the accusative and infinitive (cp. [1562](18-the-cases/07-accusative.md#s1562)); <span lang="grc">ἀνάγκη</span> _it is necessary_ takes the accusative or dative with the infinitive.

**c.** The subject of the infinitive is expressed or omitted according to the sense.

**d.** Homer shows only the beginnings of the use of the infinitive as a real subject, i.e. not a _grammatical_ subject, as in 1984.

##### <a id="s1986"></a> §1986

**As Predicate**.—In definitions the infinitive may be used as a predicate noun with <span lang="grc">ἐστί</span>.

<span lang="grc">τὸ γὰρ γνῶναι ἐπιστήμην λαβεῖν ἐστιν</span> _for to learn is to get knowledge_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Theaet. 209e">P. Th. 209e</cite>.

##### <a id="s1987"></a> §1987

**As an Appositive**.—The infinitive may stand in apposition to a preceding substantive, pronoun, or adverb.

<span lang="grc">εἷς οἰωνὸς ἄριστος, ἀμύ̄νεσθαι περὶ πάτρης</span> _one omen is best, to fight for our country_ M 243, <span lang="grc">εἶπον . . . τοῦτο μόνον ὁρᾶν πάντας, τῷ πρόσθεν ἕπεσθαι</span> _I told all to pay heed to this only_, viz., _to follow their leader_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 2.2.8">X. C. 2.2.8</cite>, <span lang="grc">καὶ ὑ̄μᾶς δὲ οὕτως, ὦ παῖδες, . . . ἐπαίδευον, τοὺς μὲν γεραιτέρους προτῑμᾶν, τῶν δὲ νεωτέρων προτετῑμῆσθαι</span> _and I have instructed you, too_, _my children_ (_to this effect_) _to honour your elders in preference to yourselves and to receive honour from the younger in preference to them_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 8.7.10">X. C. 8.7.10</cite>.

##### <a id="s1988"></a> §1988

The infinitive not in indirect discourse, and in indirect discourse, is often used as the object of a verb.

### THE INFINITIVE NOT IN INDIRECT DISCOURSE

##### <a id="s1989"></a> §1989

The infinitive as object not in indirect discourse is used after almost any verb that requires another verb to complete its meaning. The tenses of this infinitive are timeless, and denote only stage of action.

##### <a id="s1990"></a> §1990

The infinitive may be the only expressed object, or it may be one of two expressed objects, of the leading verb.

<span lang="grc">παίδευσις καλὴ διδάσκει χρῆσθαι νόμοις</span> _a good education teaches obedience to the laws_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Hunt. 12.14">X. Ven. 12.14</cite>, <span lang="grc">διαγιγνώσκειν σε τοὺς ἀγαθοὺς καὶ τοὺς κακοὺς ἐδίδαξεν</span> _he taught you to distinguish the good and the bad_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Mem. 3.1.9">X. M. 3.1.9</cite>.

**a.** Verbs signifying _to ask, bid_, _forbid_, _permit_, _teach_, etc., allow an infinitive as one of two objects.

**b.** Many verbal expressions, formed by a substantive and a verb, take the infinitive. Thus, <span lang="grc">τοὺς ἄλλους διδάσκειν τέχνην ἔχουσιν</span> _they possess the skill to teach_ (_the_) _others_ <cite data-ref="Isoc. 16.11">I. 16.11</cite>. Cp. [2000](#s2000).

#### A. _Object Infinitive after Verbs of Will or Desire_

<!-- page 443 -->

##### <a id="s1991"></a> §1991

_Verbs of will_ or _desire_ (and their opposites) are often followed by an infinitive. The infinitive with a subject accusative denotes that something _should_ (_may_) _be_ or _be done_. The negative is <span lang="grc">μή</span> (see [2719](35-negative-sentences.md#s2719)-[2721](35-negative-sentences.md#s2721)).

<span lang="grc">ἤθελον αὐτοῦ ἀκούειν</span> _they were willing to listen to him_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 2.6.11">X. A. 2.6.11</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἐβουλεύοντο ἐκλιπεῖν τὴν πόλιν</span> _they planned to leave the city_ <cite data-ref="Hdt. 6.100">Hdt. 6.100</cite>, <span lang="grc">τὰ ἥδιστα . . . ζητεῖ ποιεῖν</span> _he seeks to do what he likes best_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Mem. 4.5.11">X. M. 4.5.11</cite>, <span lang="grc">βασιλεὺς ἀξιοῖ σὲ ἀποπλεῖν</span> _the king asks that you sail away_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Hell. 3.4.25">X. H. 3.4.25</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἱ̄κέτευε μὴ ἀποκτεῖναι</span> _he entreated that_ they _should not put_ him (self) _to death_ <cite data-ref="Lys. 1.25">L. 1.25</cite>, <span lang="grc">πέμπουσιν . . . στρατεύεσθαι ἐπὶ Κᾱρίᾱν</span> _they send_ orders _that he shall march upon Caria_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Hell. 3.1.7">X. H. 3.1.7</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἔδοξε πλεῖν τὸν Ἀλκιβιάδην</span> _it was decided that Alcibiades should sail_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 6.29">T. 6.29</cite>.

**a.** _Verbs of will_ or _desire_ with an accusative subject of the infinitive form one of the classes of substantive clauses introduced in English by _that_, though the infinitive in English is often more idiomatic.

##### <a id="s1992"></a> §1992

Of _verbs of will_ or _desire_ that take the infinitive some have an object

**a.** In the accusative (or are intransitive), _e.g.:_ <span lang="grc"> **αἱροῦμαι** </span> _choose_, <span lang="grc"> **αἰτῶ, αἰτοῦμαι** </span> _ask_, <span lang="grc"> **ἀξιῶ** </span> _claim_, _ask_, <span lang="grc"> **βουλεύομαι** </span> _resolve_, <span lang="grc"> **βούλομαι** </span> _wish_, _will_, <span lang="grc"> **δικαιῶ** </span> _deem right_, <span lang="grc"> **διανοοῦμαι** </span> _intend_, <span lang="grc"> **ἐθέλω** </span> (poet. <span lang="grc"> **θέλω** </span>), _wish_, _will_, <span lang="grc"> **εἴωθα** </span> _am wont to_, <span lang="grc"> **ἐπιχειρῶ** </span> _attempt_, <span lang="grc"> **ἐῶ** </span> _permit_, <span lang="grc"> **ζητῶ** </span> _seek_, <span lang="grc"> **κελεύω** </span> _command_, _suggest_, _invite_, <span lang="grc"> **μέλλω** </span> _delay_, <span lang="grc"> **πειρῶμαι** </span> _try_, <span lang="grc"> **πέμπω** </span> _send_, <span lang="grc"> **προθῡμοῦμαι** </span> _am zealous_, <span lang="grc"> **προκαλοῦμαι** </span> _invite_, <span lang="grc"> **προτρέπω** </span> _urge_, <span lang="grc"> **σπεύδω** </span> _hasten_, _am eager_, <span lang="grc"> **σπουδάζω** </span> _am eager_, <span lang="grc"> **τολμῶ** </span> _dare_, <span lang="grc"> **φιλῶ** </span> _am wont to_, <span lang="grc"> **ψηφίζομαι** </span> _vote_.

**b.** In the genitive, _e.g.:_ <span lang="grc"> **δέομαι** </span> _ask_, <span lang="grc"> **ἐπιθῡμῶ** </span> and <span lang="grc"> **ὀρέγομαι** </span> _desire_.

**c.** In the dative, _e.g.:_ <span lang="grc"> **εὔχομαι** </span> _pray_, <span lang="grc"> **παραγγέλλω** </span> and <span lang="grc"> **προστάττω** </span> _command_, <span lang="grc"> **ἐπιβουλεύω** </span> _purpose_, <span lang="grc"> **συμβουλεύω** </span> _advise_, <span lang="grc"> **ἐπιτρέπω** </span> and <span lang="grc"> **συγχωρῶ** </span> _permit_, <span lang="grc"> **παραινῶ** </span> _exhort_, <span lang="grc"> **δοκῶ μοι** </span> _I have a mind to;_ and <span lang="grc">**λέγω**, **εἶπον**, **φωνῶ**, **φράζω**</span> _tell_ (and <span lang="grc"> **βοῶ** </span> _shout_) in the sense of _command_.

N.—<span lang="grc">πείθω</span> _urge_ to a course of action, takes the infinitive, <span lang="grc">πείθω</span> _convince_ generally has <span lang="grc">ὡς</span>, rarely the accusative with the infinitive. Thus, <span lang="grc">ἔπειθεν αὐτὸν καθ᾽ αὑτὸν πορεύεσθαι</span> _he urged him to go by himself_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 6.2.13">X. A. 6.2.13</cite>, <span lang="grc">οὐ γὰρ πείσονται οἱ πολλοί, ὡς σὺ αὐτὸς οὐκ ἠθέλησας ἀπιέναι</span> _for most people will not be convinced that of your own free will you did not desire to go away_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Crito 44c">P. Cr. 44c</cite> (infinitive <cite data-ref="Xen. Mem. 1.1.20">X. M. 1.1.20</cite>).

##### <a id="s1993"></a> §1993

Verbs of will or desire _not to do anything_ are _e.g.:_ <span lang="grc">δέδοικα, φοβοῦμαι</span> _fear_, <span lang="grc">φεύγω</span> _avoid_, <span lang="grc">ὀκνῶ</span> _scruple_, <span lang="grc">αἰσχύ̄νομαι, αἰδοῦμαι</span> ([2126](22-the-participle.md#s2126)) _feel shame to_, <span lang="grc">ἀπαγορεύω</span> _forbid_, <span lang="grc">κωλύ̄ω</span> _hinder_, <span lang="grc">ἀπέχομαι</span> _abstain from_, <span lang="grc">εὐλαβοῦμαι, φυλάττομαι</span> _beware of_. Thus, <span lang="grc">φοβοῦμαι διελέγχειν σε</span> _I fear to refute you_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Gorg. 457e">P. G. 457e</cite>, <span lang="grc">αἰσχύ̄νομαι ὑ̄μῖν εἰπεῖν τἀ̄ληθῆ</span> _I am ashamed to tell you the truth_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Apol. 22b">P. A. 22b</cite>.

##### <a id="s1994"></a> §1994

Under _verbs of will_ or _desire_ are included verbs expressing an activity to the end that something _shall_ or _shall not be done_. Thus, <span lang="grc">δίδωμι</span> _offer_, _give_, <span lang="grc">διαμάχομαι</span> _struggle against_, <span lang="grc">ποιῶ, διαπρά̄ττομαι, κατεργάζομαι</span> _manage_, _effect_, <span lang="grc">παρέχω</span> _offer_ (others in 1992, [1993](#s1993)). <!-- page 444 -->

##### <a id="s1995"></a> §1995

Several _verbs of will_ or _desire_ take <span lang="grc">ὅπως</span> with the future or the subjunctive (_verbs of effort_, 2211, [2214](29-adverbial-complex-sentences-2193-2487/03-object-clauses.md#s2214)); or <span lang="grc">μή</span> with the subjunctive (_verbs of fear_, [2225](29-adverbial-complex-sentences-2193-2487/03-object-clauses.md#s2225)); some take the participle ([2123 f](22-the-participle.md#s2123)f.).

##### <a id="s1996"></a> §1996

The infinitive may be used with the

**a.** Genitive or dative when the expression of desire is addressed _to_ a person and the genitive or dative depends on the leading verb. Here the sentence is simple. Thus, <span lang="grc">δέομαι ὑ̄μῶν . . . τὰ δίκαια ψηφίσασθαι</span> _I ask you to render a just verdict_ <cite data-ref="Isoc. 19.51">I. 19.51</cite>, <span lang="grc">τοῖς ἄλλοις πᾶσι παρήγγελλεν ἐξοπλίζεσθαι</span> _he ordered all the rest to arm themselves_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 1.8.3">X. A. 1.8.3</cite>.

**b.** Accusative when the action _of_ a person is desired (example in [1979](#s1979)). Such sentences are complex.

N.—_Verbs of commanding_ allow either **a** or **b**; but only <span lang="grc">κελεύω</span> with the accusative permits either meaning: <span lang="grc">κελεύω σὲ ταῦτα μὴ ποιεῑν</span> _I tell you not to do this_ and _I command that you shall not do this_. Cp. [1981](#s1981).

##### <a id="s1997"></a> §1997

Several verbs signifying _to say_ are also used as _verbs of will_ and then mean _command_. The agent commanded usually stands in the accusative subject of the infinitive. So with <span lang="grc">λέγω, εἶπον, φράζω, φωνῶ</span>. Thus <span lang="grc">λέγω σ᾽ ἐγὼ δόλῳ Φιλοκτήτην λαβεῖν</span> _I say that thou shalt take Philoctetes by craft_ <cite data-ref="Soph. Phil. 101">S. Ph. 101</cite>, <span lang="grc">τούτοις ἔλεγον πλεῖν</span> _I told them that they should sail_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 19.150">D. 19.150</cite>, <span lang="grc">πάντες ἔλεγον τοὺς τούτων ἄρξαντας δοῦναι δίκην</span> _all said that the ringleaders should suffer punishment_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 5.7.34">X. A. 5.7.34</cite>, <span lang="grc">εἶπον τὴν θύρᾱν κεκλεῖσθαι</span> _they commanded that the door should be shut_ (and stay shut) <cite data-ref="Xen. Hell. 5.4.7">X. H. 5.4.7</cite>, <span lang="grc">βασιλεὺς ἔγραψε πά̄σᾱς τὰ̄ς ἐν τῇ Ἑλλάδι πόλεις αὐτονόμους εἶναι</span> _the king issued a written order that all the cities in Greece should be independent_ (not: _wrote that they were independent_) <cite data-ref="Xen. Hell. 6.3.12">X. H. 6.3.12</cite>.

**a.** The agent may stand in the dative as <span lang="grc">χαλᾶν λέγω σοι</span> _I bid thee let go_ <cite data-ref="Soph. OC 840">S. O. C. 840</cite>.

##### <a id="s1998"></a> §1998

The present and aorist infinitive (both timeless) are the usual tenses of the infinitive after _verbs of will_ or _desire_ (see [1869](20-the-verb-voices/10-the-tenses.md#s1869)). The perfect is rare; as <span lang="grc">εἶπον τὴν θύρᾱν κεκλεῖσθαι</span> ([1997](#s1997)). <span lang="grc">δοκῶ</span> and <span lang="grc">δοκῶ μοι</span> signifying _I have a mind to_ or _I am determined to_ take the present or aorist like <span lang="grc">δοκεῖ· τὸν ὄνον ἐξάγειν δοκῶ</span> _I have a mind to bring out the ass_ <cite data-ref="Aristoph. Wasps 177">Ar. Vesp. 177</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἐγὼ οὖν μοι δοκῶ . . . ὑφηγήσασθαι κτλ</span>. _now I have a mind to show_, etc. <cite data-ref="Plat. Euthyd. 288c">P. Eu. 288c</cite>. Cp. [1983 a](#s1983). When it is clearly denoted that the action resolved on is to follow without delay the future is used; as in <span lang="grc">ἀλλά μοι δοκῶ . . . οὐ πείσεσθαι αὐτῷ</span> _but I am determined that I will not accept his opinion_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Theaet. 183d">P. Th. 183d</cite>.

**a.** Some verbs, as <span lang="grc">κελεύω</span>, which might be held to introduce indirect discourse, are classed under ver&lt;\*&gt;s of _will_ or _desire_, because, like these verbs, they do not regularly take the future infinitive; and because, unlike _verbs of saying_ and _thinking_ (which admit _all_ the tenses of the infinitive) they introduce infinitives which do not show differences of time. The future infinitive does not express a command. For a few cases of the future after _verbs of will_ or _desire_, see [1869](20-the-verb-voices/10-the-tenses.md#s1869).

##### <a id="s1999"></a> §1999

Verbs signifying _to hope, expect_, _promise_, _threaten_ , and _swear_, when followed by the aorist (less often the present) infinitive ([1868](20-the-verb-voices/10-the-tenses.md#s1868)), have the construction of _verbs of will_ or _desire_. When such verbs take the future infinitive they have the construction of indirect discourse.

#### B. _Infinitive after Other Verbs_

<!-- page 445 -->

##### <a id="s2000"></a> §2000

The infinitive follows many verbs, especially such as denote _ability_, _fitness_, _necessity_, etc. (and their opposites).

<span lang="grc">οὐκέτι ἐδύνατο . . . βιοτεύειν</span> _he was no longer able to live_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 1.130">T. 1.130</cite>, <span lang="grc">νεῖν ἐπιστάμενος</span> _knowing how to swim_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 5.7.25">X. A. 5.7.25</cite>, <span lang="grc">πεφύ̄κᾱσί τε ἅπαντες . . . ἁμαρτάνειν</span> _and all men are by nature prone to err_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 3.45">T. 3.45</cite>, <span lang="grc">μανθάνουσιν ἄρχειν τε καὶ ἄρχεσθαι</span> _they learn how to govern and be governed_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 1.9.4">X. A. 1.9.4</cite>; also after the impersonals of 1985.

**a.** <span lang="grc">ἔχω</span> _I can_ is derived from the meaning _I have_ especially with a _verb of saying_. Thus, <span lang="grc">Διὸς πλᾱγὰ̄ν ἔχουσιν εἰπεῖν</span> _they can proclaim a stroke of Zeus_ <cite data-ref="Aesch. Ag. 367">A. Ag. 367</cite>.

#### C. _Infinitive after Adjectives_, _Adverbs_, _and Substantives_

##### <a id="s2001"></a> §2001

The infinitive serves to define the meaning of adjectives, adverbs, and substantives, especially those denoting _ability_, _fitness_, _capacity_, etc. (and their opposites), and generally those analogous in meaning to verbs which take the infinitive ([2000](#s2000)). Here the datival meaning (_purpose_, _destination_) is often apparent. Cp. [1969](#s1969).

##### <a id="s2002"></a> §2002

**Adjectives and Adverbs.**—<span lang="grc">ἱκανοὶ ἡμᾶς ὠφελεῖν</span> _able to assist us_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 3.3.18">X. A. 3.3.18</cite>, <span lang="grc">δεινὸς λέγειν, κακὸς βιῶναι</span> _skilled in speaking, evil in life_ <cite data-ref="Aeschin. 3.174">Aes. 3.174</cite>, <span lang="grc">οἷοι φιλεῖν</span> _able to love_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 25.2">D. 25.2</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἕτοιμοί εἰσι μάχεσθαι</span> _they are ready to fight_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 4.1.1">X. C. 4.1.1</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἄρχειν ἀξιώτατος</span> _most worthy to govern_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 1.9.1">X. A. 1.9.1</cite>, <span lang="grc">ὁδὸς . . . ἀμήχανος εἰσελθεῖν στρατεύματι</span> _a road impracticable for an army to enter_ 1. 2. 21, <span lang="grc">χαλεπὸν διαβαίνειν</span> _hard to cross_ 5. 6. 9, <span lang="grc">ἐπινοῆσαι ὀξεῖς</span> _quick to conceive_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 1.70">T. 1.70</cite>. So also after <span lang="grc">ῥᾴδιος</span> _easy_, <span lang="grc">ἡδύς</span> _pleasant_, <span lang="grc">δίκαιος</span> _just_, <span lang="grc">ἀναγκαῖος</span> _necessary_, <span lang="grc">ἐπιτήδειος</span> _suitable_, <span lang="grc">ἀγαθός</span> _good_, <span lang="grc">αἴτιος</span> _responsible for_, <span lang="grc">μαλακός</span> _incapable of;_ cp. <span lang="grc">ὀλίγος</span> 1063. After adverbs: <span lang="grc">κάλλιστα ἰδεῖν</span> _most splendid to behold_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 8.3.5">X. C. 8.3.5</cite>.

**a.** Some of these adjectives take the infinitive by analogy to the related verbs, as <span lang="grc">πρόθῡμος</span> _zealous_ (<span lang="grc">προθῡμοῦμαι</span>), <span lang="grc">ἐπιστήμων</span> _knowing how_ (<span lang="grc">ἐπίσταμαι</span>).

##### <a id="s2003"></a> §2003

<span lang="grc"> **οἷος** </span> _fit_, <span lang="grc"> **ὅσος** </span> _sufficient_ take the infinitive like the fuller expressions <span lang="grc">τοιοῦτος οἷος, τοσοῦτος ὅσος</span>. Thus, <span lang="grc">οὐ γὰρ ἦν ὥρᾱ οἵᾱ τὸ πεδίον ἄρδειν</span> _for it was not the proper season to irrigate the plain_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 2.3.13">X. A. 2.3.13</cite>, <span lang="grc">ὅσον ἀποζῆν</span> _sufficient to live off of_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 1.2">T. 1.2</cite>, <span lang="grc">τοιοῦτος οἷος . . . πείθεσθαι</span> _the kind of a man to be convinced_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Crito 46b">P. Cr. 46b</cite>. On <span lang="grc">τοσοῦτος ὥστε</span> (<span lang="grc">ὡς</span>) see [2263](29-adverbial-complex-sentences-2193-2487/06-omegasigmatauepsilon-rarely-omegasigma-with-the-infinitive.md#s2263). Hom. has the infinitive after <span lang="grc">τοῖος, τόσος</span>, etc.

##### <a id="s2004"></a> §2004

**Substantives**.—As, <span lang="grc">οἱ παῖδες ὑ̄μῖν ὀλίγου ἡλικίᾱν ἔχουσι παιδεύεσθαι</span> _your children are almost of an age to be educated_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Lach. 187c">P. Lach. 187c</cite>. With <span lang="grc">ἐστί</span> omitted: <span lang="grc">σχολή γε ἡμῖν μανθάνειν</span> _we have leisure to learn_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 4.3.12">X. C. 4.3.12</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἀνάγκη πείθεσθαι</span> _there is need to obey_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Hell. 1.6.8">X. H. 1.6.8</cite>, <span lang="grc">περαίνειν ἤδη ὥρᾱ</span> _it is high time to finish_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 3.2.32">X. A. 3.2.32</cite>. Cp. [1985](#s1985).

##### <a id="s2005"></a> §2005

The infinitive is added, like an accusative of respect ([1601](18-the-cases/07-accusative.md#s1601), [1602](18-the-cases/07-accusative.md#s1602)), to intransitive verbs (especially in poetry), to adjectives (more frequently in poetry), and to substantives (rarely). Thus, <span lang="grc">τοῖος ἰδεῖν</span> _such in aspect_ (lit. _to look on_) Theognis 216, <span lang="grc">ὁρᾶν στυγνός</span> _of a repulsive expression_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 2.6.9">X. A. 2.6.9</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἀκοῦ-</span> <!-- page 446 --> <span lang="grc">σαι παγκάλως ἔχει</span> _it is very fine to hear_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 19.47">D. 19.47</cite>, <span lang="grc">θαῦμα καὶ ἀκοῦσαι</span> _a marvel even to hear of_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Laws 656d">P. L. 656d</cite>.

##### <a id="s2006"></a> §2006

The infinitive limiting the meaning of an adjective is commonly active (or middle) in cases where the passive is more natural in English. Thus, <span lang="grc">λόγος δυνατὸς κατανοῆσαι</span> _a speech capable of being understood_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Phaedo 90c">P. Ph. 90c</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἄξιος θαυμάσαι</span> _worthy to be admired_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 1.138">T. 1.138</cite> (but <span lang="grc">ἄξιος θαυμάζεσθαι</span> <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 5.1.6">X. C. 5.1.6</cite>).

**a.** The active use is due to the old datival function of the infinitive: <span lang="grc">δυνατὸς κατανοῆσαι</span> _capable for understanding_.

##### <a id="s2007"></a> §2007

The infinitive, with or without <span lang="grc">ὥστε</span> or <span lang="grc">ὡς</span>, may be used with <span lang="grc">ἤ</span> _than_ after comparatives, depending on an (implied) idea of _ability_ or _inability_. <span lang="grc">ἢ ὥστε</span> is more common than <span lang="grc">ἤ</span> or <span lang="grc">ἢ ὡς</span>. Cp. [2264](29-adverbial-complex-sentences-2193-2487/06-omegasigmatauepsilon-rarely-omegasigma-with-the-infinitive.md#s2264).

<span lang="grc">τὸ γὰρ νόσημα μεῖζον ἢ φέρειν</span> _for the disease is too great to be borne_ <cite data-ref="Soph. OT 1293">S. O. T. 1293</cite>, <span lang="grc">φοβοῦμαι μή τι μεῖζον ἢ ὥστε φέρειν δύνασθαι κακὸν τῇ πόλει συμβῇ</span> _I fear lest some calamity befall the State greater than it can bear_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Mem. 3.5.17">X. M. 3.5.17</cite>, <span lang="grc">βραχύτερα ἢ ὡς ἐξικνεῖσθαι</span> _too short to reach_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 3.3.7">X. A. 3.3.7</cite>.

**a.** The force of <span lang="grc">ἢ ὥστε</span> may be expressed by the genitive; as, <span lang="grc">κρεῖσσον λόγου</span> (<cite data-ref="Thuc. 2.50">T. 2.50</cite>) = <span lang="grc">κρεῖσσον ἢ ὥστε λέγεσθαι</span>. Cp. [1077](14-adjectives.md#s1077).

**b.** Words implying a comparison may take the infinitive with <span lang="grc">ὥστε</span> or <span lang="grc">ὡς</span> ([1063](14-adjectives.md#s1063)).

#### D. _Infinitive of Purpose and Result_

##### <a id="s2008"></a> §2008

**Infinitive of Purpose**.—The infinitive may express purpose (usually only with verbs taking the accusative).

<span lang="grc">ταύτην τὴν χώρᾱν ἐπέτρεψε διαρπάσαι τοῖς Ἕλλησιν</span> _he gave this land over to the Greeks to plunder_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 1.2.19">X. A. 1.2.19</cite>, <span lang="grc">τὸ ἥμισυ</span> (<span lang="grc">τοῦ στρατεύματος</span>) <span lang="grc">κατέλιπε φυλάττειν τὸ στρατόπεδον</span> _he left half_ (_of the army_) _behind to guard the camp_ 5. 2. 1, <span lang="grc">ἰέναι ἐπὶ βασιλέᾱ οὐκ ἐγίγνετο τὰ ἱερά</span> _the sacrifices did not turn out_ (favourable) _for going against the king_ 2. 2. 3, <span lang="grc">Ἀριστάρχῳ . . . ἔδοτε ἡμέρᾱν ἀπολογήσασθαι</span> _you granted a day to Aristarchus to make his defence_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Hell. 1.7.28">X. H. 1.7.28</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἡ θύρᾱ ἡ ἐμὴ ἀνέῳκτο . . . εἰσιέναι τῷ δεομένῳ τι ἐμοῦ</span> _my door stood open for any petitioner of mine to enter_ 5. 1. 14, <span lang="grc">παρέχω ἐμαυτὸν ἐρωτᾶν</span> _I offer myself to be questioned_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Apol. 33b">P. A. 33b</cite>, <span lang="grc">τὰ̄ς γυναῖκας πιεῖν φερούσᾱς</span> _the women bringing_ (something) _to drink_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Hell. 7.2.9">X. H. 7.2.9</cite>. Cp. also 2032 e.

##### <a id="s2009"></a> §2009

The infinitive of purpose is used in prose especially after verbs meaning _to give, entrust_, _choose_, _appoint_, _take_, _receive_. Verbs signifying _to send_, _go_, _come_ usually take the future active participle ([2065](22-the-participle.md#s2065)); but <cite data-ref="Thuc. 6.50">T. 6.50</cite> has <span lang="grc">δέκα τῶν νεῶν προύπεμψαν ἐς τὸν μέγαν λιμένα πλεῦσαι</span> _they sent ahead ten ships to sail into the great harbour;_ and in poetry the infinitive often denotes purpose after these verbs, and after <span lang="grc">εἶναι</span> in Homer (<cite data-ref="Hom. Il. 11.20"><span lang="grc">Λ</span> 20</cite>) and Hdt. (5. [25](../part-1-letters-sounds-syllables-accent/01-the-letters.md#s25)).

##### <a id="s2010"></a> §2010

After verbs meaning _to have_ (or _be_) _at one's disposition:_ <span lang="grc">οἱ στρατιῶται ἀργύριον οὐκ εἶχον ἐπισῑτίζεσθαι</span> _the soldiers did not have money by means of which they could provision themselves_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 7.1.7">X. A. 7.1.7</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἐκεῖ σκιά̄ τ᾽ ἐστὶ καὶ πόᾱ καθίζεσθαι</span> _there is shade and grass to sit down in_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Phaedrus 229b">P. Phae. 229b</cite>.

##### <a id="s2011"></a> §2011

**Infinitive of Result**.—The infinitive may be used with <span lang="grc">ὥστε</span> <!-- page 447 --> (sometimes with <span lang="grc">ὡς</span>) to denote a result, often an intended result. See [2260](29-adverbial-complex-sentences-2193-2487/06-omegasigmatauepsilon-rarely-omegasigma-with-the-infinitive.md#s2260) ff.

**a.** Several verbs, substantives, and adjectives usually taking the infinitive also admit <span lang="grc">ὥστε</span> with the infinitive ([2271](29-adverbial-complex-sentences-2193-2487/06-omegasigmatauepsilon-rarely-omegasigma-with-the-infinitive.md#s2271)); and the infinitive is found where <span lang="grc">ὥστε</span> with the infinitive might be expected: <span lang="grc">μνημονεύουσιν ἀφεθέντα τοῦτον ἐλεύθερον εἶναι</span> _they recall that he was emancipated_ (lit. _released so as to be free_) <cite data-ref="Dem. 29.25">D. 29.25</cite>. Here the redundant infinitive expresses an intended result.

N.—This redundant use of <span lang="grc">εἶναι</span> is common in Hom. and Hdt.

#### E. _Absolute Infinitive_

##### <a id="s2012"></a> §2012

Certain idiomatic infinitives are used absolutely in parenthetical phrases to limit the application of a single expression or of the entire sentence.

**a. Verbs of Saying**.—<span lang="grc">ὡς ἔπος εἰπεῖν, ὡς εἰπεῖν</span> _so to speak, almost;_ (<span lang="grc">ὡς</span>) <span lang="grc">ἁπλῶς εἰπεῖν, ὡς συνελόντι</span> ([1497](18-the-cases/06-dative.md#s1497)) <span lang="grc">εἰπεῖν, ὡς</span> (<span lang="grc">ἐν βραχεῖ</span> or) <span lang="grc">συντόμως εἰπεῖν</span> _to speak briefly_, _concisely;_ <span lang="grc">ὡς ἐπὶ πᾶν εἰπεῖν, τὸ σύμπαν εἰπεῖν</span> _speaking generally;_ <span lang="grc">σχεδὸν εἰπεῖν</span> _so to say, almost_ (_paene dixerim_); <span lang="grc">σὺν θεῷ εἰπεῖν</span> _in God's name;_ and so <span lang="grc">ὡς</span> with <span lang="grc">λέγειν, φράζειν, εἰρῆσθαι</span>, as <span lang="grc">ὡς ἐν τύπῳ εἰρῆσθαι</span> _in general_. Examples: <span lang="grc">ἀληθές γε ὡς ἔπος εἰπεῖν οὐδὲν εἰρήκᾱσιν</span> _not one word of truth_, _I may say, did they utter_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Apol. 17a">P. A. 17a</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἀγαθὸν μὲν ἁπλῶς εἰπεῖν οὐδὲν γέγονε τῇ πόλει</span> _in a word the State gained no advantage_ Dinarchus 1.33.

**b.** <span lang="grc">ὡς</span> (<span lang="grc">ἔπος</span>) <span lang="grc">εἰπεῖν</span> is often used to limit too strict an application of a general statement, especially <span lang="grc">πᾶς</span> or <span lang="grc">οὐδείς</span>. Thus, <span lang="grc">πάντες ὡς ἔπος εἰπεῖν</span> _nearly every one_, <span lang="grc">οὐδεὶς ὡς ἔπος εἰπεῖν</span> _almost no one_. It is thus used like _paene dixerim;_ rarely, like _ut ita dicam_, to soften the strength of a metaphor.

**c.** Especially common is the absolute <span lang="grc">εἶναι</span> in <span lang="grc">ἑκὼν</span> _willingly_, _intentionally_, _if you can help it_, usually in negative or quasi-negative statements (<span lang="grc">ἑκών</span> may be inflected). Also in <span lang="grc">τὸ κατὰ τοῦτον</span> (<span lang="grc">ἐπὶ τούτῳ</span>) <span lang="grc">εἶναι</span> _as far as he is concerned_, <span lang="grc">ὡς . . . εἶναι</span> _as far as . . . is concerned_, <span lang="grc">τὸ νῦν εἶναι</span> _at present_. Examples: <span lang="grc">οὐδὲ ξένοις ἑκὼν εἶναι γέλωτα παρέχεις</span> _nor do you intentionally cause strangers to laugh_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 2.2.15">X. C. 2.2.15</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἑκοῦσα εἶναι οὐκ ἀπολείπεται</span> _it is not willingly separated_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Phaedrus 252a">P. Phae. 252a</cite>, <span lang="grc">τό γε ἐπ᾽ ἐκεῖνον εἶναι ἐσώθης</span> (<span lang="grc">ἄν</span>) _so far, at least_, _as it depended on him you would have been saved_ <cite data-ref="Lys. 13.58">L. 13.58</cite>.

**d. Other expressions**: <span lang="grc">ἐμοὶ δοκεῖν, ὡς ἐμοὶ δοκεῖν, ὡς ἐμοὶ κρῖναι</span> _as it seems to me_, _in my opinion_, (<span lang="grc">ὡς</span>) <span lang="grc">εἰκάσαι</span> _to make a guess_, (<span lang="grc">ὡς</span>) <span lang="grc">συμβάλλειν</span> _to compare_, (<span lang="grc">ὡς</span>) <span lang="grc">ἀκοῦσαι</span> _to the ear_, <span lang="grc">ὡς ὑμομνῆσαι</span> _to recall the matter_, <span lang="grc">ὅσον γέ μ᾽ εἰδέναι</span> _as far as I know_, etc.; <span lang="grc">ὀλίγου δεῖν, μῑκροῦ δεῖν</span> _almost_, _all but_ (<span lang="grc">δεῖν</span> may be omitted, [1399](18-the-cases/05-free-uses-of-the-genitive.md#s1399)). Examples: <span lang="grc">ὁ γὰρ Κτήσιππος ἔτυχε πόρρω καθεζόμενος τοῦ Κλεινίου, ἐμοὶ δοκεῖν</span> _for Ctesippus_, _it seems to me, happened to be sitting at a distance from Clinias_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Euthyd. 274b">P. Eu. 274b</cite>, <span lang="grc">μῑκροῦ δεῖν τρία τάλαντα</span> _almost three talents_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 27.29">D. 27.29</cite>.

**e.** Some of these absolute infinitives may be explained by reference to the idea of purpose ([2008](#s2008)) or result. Thus, <span lang="grc">συνελόντι εἰπεῖν</span> _for one compressing_ the matter _to speak_ (cp. _ut paucis dicam_), <span lang="grc">μῑκροῦ δεῖν</span> _so as to lack little_. Others recall the adverbial accusative ([1606](18-the-cases/07-accusative.md#s1606)); cp. <span lang="grc">ἐμοὶ δοκεῖν</span> with <span lang="grc">γνώμην ἐμήν</span>.

#### F. _Infinitive in Commands_, _Wishes_, _and Exclamations_

<!-- page 448 -->

##### <a id="s2013"></a> §2013

**Infinitive in Commands**.—The infinitive may be used for the second person of the imperative. The person addressed is regarded as the subject. This infinitive is commoner in poetry than in prose (where it has a solemn or formal force).

<span lang="grc">θαρσῶν νῦν, Διόμηδες, ἐπὶ Τρώεσσι μάχεσθαι</span> _with good courage now_, _Diomed_, _fight against the Trojans_ E 124, <span lang="grc">σὺ δέ, Κλεαρίδᾱ . . . τὰ̄ς πύλᾱς ἀνοίξᾱς ἐπεκθεῖν</span> _but do you_, _Clearidas_, _open the gates and sally forth_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 5.9">T. 5.9</cite>.

**a.** This infinitive may be used in conjunction with an imperative: <span lang="grc">ἀκούετε λεῴ· κατὰ τὰ πάτρια τοὺς χόας πί̄νειν</span> _hear ye, good people! drink the Pitchers as our sires drank!_ <cite data-ref="Aristoph. Ach. 1000">Ar. Ach. 1000</cite>.

**b.** The infinitive for the third person of the imperative often occurs in legal language (laws, treaties, etc.), and does not necessarily depend on the principal verb. Thus, <span lang="grc">ἔτη δὲ εἶναι τὰ̄ς σπονδὰ̄ς πεντήκοντα</span> _and the treaty shall continue for fifty years_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 5.18">T. 5.18</cite>. In this construction the infinitive has the force of an infinitive dependent on <span lang="grc">ἔδοξε</span> (_it was voted that_) or the like. So in medical language, as <span lang="grc">πί̄νειν δὲ ὕδωρ</span> it is well for the patient _to drink water_ Hippocrates 1. 151.

**c.** The infinitive (with subject accusative) is rarely used for the third person of the imperative when there is an unconscious ellipsis of a word like <span lang="grc">δός</span> _grant_, or <span lang="grc">εὔχομαι</span> _I pray_. Thus, <span lang="grc">τεύχεα σῡλήσᾱς φερέτω κοίλᾱς ἐπὶ νῆας, σῶμα δὲ οἴκαδ᾽ ἐμὸν δόμεναι πάλιν</span> _let him strip off my arms and carry them to the hollow ships_, _but let him give back my body to my home_ H 78.

**d.** In negative commands (prohibitions) <span lang="grc">μή</span> with the infinitive is poetic and Ionic: <span lang="grc">οἷς μὴ πελάζειν</span> _do not approach these_ ( = <span lang="grc">μὴ πέλαζε</span>) <cite data-ref="Aesch. PB 712">A. Pr. 712</cite>, <span lang="grc">μηδὲ καλεῖν πω ὄλβιον</span> _and do not call him happy yet_ <cite data-ref="Hdt. 1.32">Hdt. 1.32</cite>.

##### <a id="s2014"></a> §2014

**Infinitive in Wishes**.—The infinitive with a subject accusative may be used in the sense of the optative of wish, usually with the same ellipsis as in 2013 c.

<span lang="grc">θεοὶ πολῖται, μή με δουλείᾱς τυχεῖν</span> _ye gods of my country, may bondage not be my lot!_ <cite data-ref="Aesch. Seven 253">A. Sept. 253</cite>, <span lang="grc">ὦ Ζεῦ, ἐκγενέσθαι μοι Ἀθηναίους τείσασθαι</span> _oh Zeus_, _that it be granted to me to punish the Athenians!_ <cite data-ref="Hdt. 5.105">Hdt. 5.105</cite> (cp. <span lang="grc">ὦ Ζεῦ, δός με τείσασθαι μόρον πατρός</span> _oh Zeus_, _grant that I may avenge my father's murder!_ <cite data-ref="Aesch. Lib. 18">A. Ch. 18</cite>). This construction is very rare in Attic prose: <span lang="grc">τὸν κυνηγέτην ἔχοντα ἐξιέναι . . . ἐλαφρὰ̄ν ἐσθῆτα</span> _the hunter should go forth in a light dress_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Hunt. 6.11">X. Ven. 6.11</cite>. Here no definite verb can be supplied.

**a.** The nominative with the infinitive (instead of the optative) after <span lang="grc">αι᾽ γάρ</span> occurs in Homer (<cite data-ref="Hom. Od. 7.311"><span lang="grc">η</span> 311</cite>, <cite data-ref="Hom. Od. 24.376"><span lang="grc">ω</span> 376</cite>).

##### <a id="s2015"></a> §2015

**Infinitive in Exclamations**.—The infinitive is often used in exclamations of surprise or indignation. The subject stands in the accusative.

<span lang="grc">ἐμὲ παθεῖν τάδε</span> _that I should suffer this!_ <cite data-ref="Aesch. Eum. 837">A. Eum. 837</cite>, <span lang="grc">τοιουτονὶ̄ τρέφειν κύνα</span> _to keep a dog like that!_ <cite data-ref="Aristoph. Wasps 835">Ar. Vesp. 835</cite>.

On the infinitive with <span lang="grc">ἐφ᾽ ᾧ</span> (<span lang="grc">ἐφ᾽ ᾧτε</span>) see [2279](29-adverbial-complex-sentences-2193-2487/08-clauses-with-epsilonphi-omega-and-epsilonphi-omegatauepsilon.md#s2279); with <span lang="grc">πρίν</span>, see [2453](29-adverbial-complex-sentences-2193-2487/12-temporal-clauses.md#s2453).

### INFINITIVE AS OBJECT IN INDIRECT DISCOURSE

<!-- page 449 -->

##### <a id="s2016"></a> §2016

The infinitive is used as the object of _verbs of saying_ and _thinking_. Such infinitives denote both time and stage of action (cp. [1866](20-the-verb-voices/10-the-tenses.md#s1866)).

**a.** The finite verb of a sentence placed in dependence on a _verb of saying_ or _thinking_ that requires the infinitive, becomes infinitive, which infinitive stands in the relation of a substantive as subject or object of the leading verb. Commonly as _object:_ thus, <span lang="grc">Κῦρος νῑκᾷ</span> _Cyrus is victorious_, when made the object of <span lang="grc">φησί</span> _he says_, becomes a part of a new sentence <span lang="grc">φησὶ Κῦρον νῑκᾶν</span>, in which <span lang="grc">Κῦρον νικᾶν</span> is the object of <span lang="grc">φησί</span>. As _subject_, when the _verb of saying_ is passive: thus, in <span lang="grc">λέγεται Κῦρον νῑκᾶν</span>, the last two words form the subject of <span lang="grc">λέγεται</span>.

##### <a id="s2017"></a> §2017

_Verbs of saying_ are _e.g.: say_ <span lang="grc">φημί, φάσκω, λέγω</span>; _confess_ <span lang="grc">ὁμολογῶ</span>; _promise_ <span lang="grc">ὑπισχνοῦμαι, ὑποδέχομαι, ἐπαγγέλλομαι, ὑφίσταμαι</span>; _pretend_ <span lang="grc">προσποιοῦμαι</span>; _swear_ <span lang="grc">ὄμνῡμι</span>; _deny_ <span lang="grc">ἀπαρνοῦμαι</span>; _gainsay_ <span lang="grc">ἀντιλέγω</span>; _dispute_ <span lang="grc">ἀμφισβητῶ</span>, etc.

Some _verbs of saying_ admit other constructions than the infinitive, and especially <span lang="grc">ὅτι</span> or <span lang="grc">ὡς</span> ([2579](31-dependent-substantive-clauses-2574-2635.md#s2579)). <span lang="grc">λέγω, εἶπον, φράζω, φωνῶ</span> with <span lang="grc">ὅτι</span> or <span lang="grc">ὡς</span> mean _say_, with the infinitive _command_ ([1997](#s1997)).

**a.** <span lang="grc">φημί</span> _say_, _assert_, _express the opinion that_ in classical Greek is almost always followed by the infinitive, but by <span lang="grc">ὅτι</span> very often in the later language. <span lang="grc">φημὶ ὅτι</span> occurs in <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 7.1.5">X. A. 7.1.5</cite> (<span lang="grc">φημὶ ὡς</span> in <cite data-ref="Lys. 7.19">L. 7.19</cite>, <cite data-ref="Xen. Hell. 6.3.7">X. H. 6.3.7</cite>; <cite data-ref="Dem. 4.48">D. 4.48</cite>, 27. 19 by anacoluthon).

**b.** <span lang="grc">λέγω</span> _state_ (impart a fact) takes either the infinitive or <span lang="grc">ὅτι</span> or <span lang="grc">ὡς</span>. The infinitive occurs usually with the passive (<span lang="grc">λέγεται</span>, etc.) either in the personal or impersonal construction ([1982 a](#s1982)). The active forms of <span lang="grc">λέγω</span> with the infinitive mean _command_ ([1997](#s1997)).

**c.** <span lang="grc">εἶπον</span> _said_ usually takes <span lang="grc">ὅτι</span> or <span lang="grc">ὡς</span>; with the infinitive, it commonly means _commanded_ ([1997](#s1997)). Cp. the double use of _told_.

N.—<span lang="grc">εἶπον</span> meaning _said_ with the infinitive is rare, but occurs in good Attic prose: <cite data-ref="Andoc. 1.57">And. 1.57</cite>, 80; Thuc. 7. 35; Lys. 10. 6, 10. 9, 10. 12; Xen. H. 1. 6. 7, 2. 2. 15, C. 5. 5. 24, S. 2. 13; <cite data-ref="Isaeus 2.29">Is. 2.29</cite>; Lyc. 50; <cite data-ref="Aeschin. 3.37">Aes. 3.37</cite>, 3. 59; Dem. 15. 18; Plato, G. 473 a, 503 d, Lach. 192 b, Charm. 174 a, Hipp. Maj. 291 b, Pol. 263 c, 290 b, L. 654 a, Clitoph. 409 a, 410 b. In poetry this use is frequent.

##### <a id="s2018"></a> §2018

_Verbs of thinking_ almost always take the infinitive. Such are: _think_ <span lang="grc">ἡγοῦμαι, οἴομαι, δοκῶ, νομίζω</span>; _hope_ <span lang="grc">ἐλπίζω</span>; _suppose_ <span lang="grc">ὑπολαμβάνω</span>; _suspect_ <span lang="grc">ὑποπτεύω</span>; _guess_ <span lang="grc">εἰκάζω</span>; _feel confident_ <span lang="grc">πιστεύω</span>; _disbelieve_ <span lang="grc">ἀπιστῶ</span>. The use of <span lang="grc">ὡς</span> is rare, while <span lang="grc">ὅτι</span> is very rare ([2580](31-dependent-substantive-clauses-2574-2635.md#s2580)).

**a.** _Verbs of perceiving_ sometimes take the infinitive by analogy to _verbs of thinking;_ as <span lang="grc">ἀκούω, αἰσθάνομαι, πυνθάνομαι</span> ([2144](22-the-participle.md#s2144)).

##### <a id="s2019"></a> §2019

Each tense of direct discourse is retained (with its proper meaning as regards stage of action) when it becomes infinitive in indirect discourse; but an imperfect is represented by the present infinitive; a pluperfect, by the perfect infinitive. See [1866](20-the-verb-voices/10-the-tenses.md#s1866), [1867](20-the-verb-voices/10-the-tenses.md#s1867).

##### <a id="s2020"></a> §2020

An original <span lang="grc">οὐ</span> of direct discourse is generally, an original <span lang="grc">μή</span> is always, retained in indirect discourse. But in some cases <span lang="grc">οὐ</span> becomes <span lang="grc">μή</span> ([2723 f](35-negative-sentences.md#s2723)f.). <!-- page 450 -->

##### <a id="s2021"></a> §2021

The infinitive is the subject of the passive of _verbs of saying_ and _thinking_ ([1982 a](#s1982)). So with <span lang="grc">δοκεῖ</span> _it seems_, <span lang="grc">φαίνεται</span> _it is plain_, etc.

##### <a id="s2022"></a> §2022

The infinitive represents a finite verb after _verbs of saying_ and _thinking_.

**a.** <span lang="grc">εὖνοί φᾱσιν εἶναι</span> _they assert that they are loyal_ <cite data-ref="Lys. 12.49">L. 12.49</cite>, <span lang="grc">οὐδεὶς ἔφασκεν γιγνώσκειν αὐτόν</span> _nobody said that he knew him_ 23. 3, <span lang="grc">οἱ ἡγεμόνες οὔ φᾱσιν</span> ([2692](35-negative-sentences.md#s2692)) <span lang="grc">εἶναι ἄλλην ὁδόν</span> _the guides say there is no other road_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 4.1.21">X. A. 4.1.21</cite>, <span lang="grc">πάντες ἐροῦσι τὸ λοιπὸν μηδὲν εἶναι κερδαλεώτερον τῆς ἀρετῆς</span> _everybody in time to come will say that there is nothing more profitable than bravery_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 7.1.18">X. C. 7.1.18</cite>. Other examples 1867.

**b.** <span lang="grc">βασιλεὺς νῑκᾶν ἡγεῖται</span> _the king thinks he is victorious_ ( = <span lang="grc">νῑκῶ</span>, cp. [1887](20-the-verb-voices/10-the-tenses.md#s1887)) <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 2.1.11">X. A. 2.1.11</cite>, <span lang="grc">οἴομαι βέλτιστον εἶναι</span> _I think it is best_ 5. 1. 8, <span lang="grc">ὑπώπτευον ἐπὶ βασιλέᾱ ἰέναι</span> _they suspected that they were to go against the king_ 1. 3. 1, (<span lang="grc">Σωκράτης</span>) <span lang="grc">τὸ ἀγνοεῖν ἑαυτὸν ἐγγυτάτω . . . μανίᾱς ἐλογίζετο εἶναι</span> _Socrates was of the opinion that for a man not to know himself was very near to madness_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Mem. 3.9.6">X. M. 3.9.6</cite>.

**c.** When a _word of saying_ is expressed or implied in what precedes, several infinitives may be used where the indicative is employed in translation. So in the narration in <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 1.3.5">X. C. 1.3.5</cite>-6.

##### <a id="s2023"></a> §2023

The infinitive with <span lang="grc">ἄν</span> represents an indicative with <span lang="grc">ἄν</span> or a potential optative with <span lang="grc">ἄν</span>. See [1846](20-the-verb-voices/09-infinitive-and-participle-with-alphanu.md#s1846), [1848](20-the-verb-voices/09-infinitive-and-participle-with-alphanu.md#s1848), [1849](20-the-verb-voices/09-infinitive-and-participle-with-alphanu.md#s1849), [2270](29-adverbial-complex-sentences-2193-2487/06-omegasigmatauepsilon-rarely-omegasigma-with-the-infinitive.md#s2270).

##### <a id="s2024"></a> §2024

Verbs signifying _to hope, expect_, _promise_, _threaten_ , and _swear_ take the future infinitive in indirect discourse, and the aorist (less often the present) infinitive not in indirect discourse (like _verbs of will_ or _desire_, 1868, [1999](#s1999)). <span lang="grc">ἐλπίζω ταῦτα ποιήσειν</span> _I hope that I shall do this_, <span lang="grc">ἐλπίζω ταῦτα ποιῆσαι</span> or <span lang="grc">ποιεῖν</span> _I hope to do this_.

## THE INFINITIVE WITH THE ARTICLE (ARTICULAR INFINITIVE)

##### <a id="s2025"></a> §2025

The articular infinitive, while having the character of a substantive, retains the functions of a verb. In its older use the articular infinitive is a subject or object; the nearest approach to this use in Homer is <span lang="grc">ἀνί̄η καὶ τὸ φυλάσσειν</span> _to watch is also trouble_ <cite data-ref="Hom. Od. 20.52"><span lang="grc">υ</span> 52</cite>. In the tragic poets the genitive and dative are rarely used; in the speeches in Thucydides and in Demosthenes all of its four cases appear with great frequency. The articular infinitive may take dependent clauses.

##### <a id="s2026"></a> §2026

The articular infinitive admits the constructions of an ordinary substantive.

Nom. <span lang="grc"> **τὸ ποιεῖν** </span> _making_ or _to make_, <span lang="grc"> **τὸ ποιήσειν, τὸ ποιῆσαι, τὸ πεποιηκέναι** </span>

Gen. <span lang="grc"> **τοῦ ποιεῖν** </span> _of making_, <span lang="grc"> **τοῦ ποιήσειν, τοῦ ποιῆσαι** </span>, etc.

Dat. <span lang="grc"> **τῷ ποιεῖν** </span> _for making, by making_, <span lang="grc"> **τῷ ποιήσειν, τῷ ποιῆσαι** </span>, etc.

Acc. <span lang="grc"> **τὸ ποιεῖν, τὸ ποιήσειν, τὸ ποιῆσαι** </span>, etc.

##### <a id="s2027"></a> §2027

The articular infinitive is treated as subject, predicate noun, and object like the simple infinitive ([1984](#s1984)-[1986](#s1986)). <!-- page 451 -->

##### <a id="s2028"></a> §2028

The negative of the articular infinitive is <span lang="grc">μή</span>.

##### <a id="s2029"></a> §2029

The articular infinitive may indicate time (after _verbs of saying_ or _thinking_, 2034 g), or may be timeless.

##### <a id="s2030"></a> §2030

The articular infinitive is in general used like the infinitive without the article, and may take <span lang="grc">ἄν</span>; as regards its constructions it has the value of a substantive. The article is regularly used when the connection uniting the infinitive to another word has to be expressed by the genitive, the dative, or a preposition.

**a.** The articular infinitive is rarely used, like a true substantive, with the subjective genitive: <span lang="grc">τό γ᾽ εὖ φρονεῖν αὐτῶν μῑμεῖσθε</span> _imitate at least their wisdom_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 19.269">D. 19.269</cite>.

##### <a id="s2031"></a> §2031

**NOMINATIVE OF THE ARTICULAR INFINITIVE**

Subject ([1984](#s1984)): <span lang="grc">νέοις τὸ σῑγᾶν κρεῖττόν ἐστι τοῦ λαλεῖν</span> _in the young silence is better than speech_ Men. Sent. 387, <span lang="grc">τὸ Πελοποννησίους αὐτοῖς μὴ βοηθῆσαι παρέσχεν ὑ̄μῖν . . . Σαμίων κόλασιν</span> _the fact that the Peloponnesians did not come to their assistance enabled you to punish the Samians_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 1.41">T. 1.41</cite>.

##### <a id="s2032"></a> §2032

**GENITIVE OF THE ARTICULAR INFINITIVE**

**a.** The genitive of the articular infinitive is used to limit the meaning of substantives, adjectives, and verbs.

**b.** Adnominal ([1290](18-the-cases/03-genitive.md#s1290)): <span lang="grc">τοῦ πιεῖν ἐπιθῡμίᾳ</span> _from desire to drink_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 7.84">T. 7.84</cite>, <span lang="grc">πρὸς τὴν πόλιν προσβαλόντες ἐς ἐλπίδα ἦλθον τοῦ ἑλεῖν</span> _they attacked the city and entertained hopes of taking it_ 2.56.

**c.** Partitive ([1306](18-the-cases/03-genitive.md#s1306)): <span lang="grc">τοῦ θαρσεῖν τὸ πλεῖστον εἰληφότες</span> _having gained the greatest amount of courage_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 4.34">T. 4.34</cite>. After comparatives ([1431](18-the-cases/05-free-uses-of-the-genitive.md#s1431)): <span lang="grc">τί οὖν ἐστιν . . . τοῦ τοῖς φίλοις ἀρήγειν κάλλῑον</span>; _what then is nobler than to help one's friends?_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 1.5.13">X. C. 1.5.13</cite>.

**d.** After verbs: <span lang="grc">ἐπέσχομεν τοῦ δακρύ̄ειν</span> _we desisted from weeping_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Phaedo 117e">P. Ph. 117e</cite> (cp. [1392](18-the-cases/05-free-uses-of-the-genitive.md#s1392)).

**e.** Purpose (cp. [1408](18-the-cases/05-free-uses-of-the-genitive.md#s1408)), often a negative purpose: <span lang="grc">τοῦ μὴ τὰ δίκαια ποιεῖν</span> _in order not to do what was just_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 18.107">D. 18.107</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἐτειχίσθη Ἀταλάντη . . . τοῦ μὴ λῃστὰ̄ς . . . κακουργεῖν τὴν Εὔβοιαν</span> _Atalante was fortified to prevent pirates from ravaging Euboea_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 2.32">T. 2.32</cite>. More common is the use with <span lang="grc">ὑπέρ</span> ([2032 g](#s2032)) or <span lang="grc">ἕνεκα</span>.

**f.** Genitive Absolute ([2070](22-the-participle.md#s2070)): <span lang="grc">ἐπ᾽ ἐκείνοις δὲ ὄντος αἰεὶ τοῦ ἐπιχειρεῖν καὶ ἐφ᾽ ἡμῖν εἶναι δεῖ τὸ προαμύ̄νασθαι</span> _since_ the power of _attack is always in their hands, so in our hands should lie_ the power of _repelling it in advance_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 3.12">T. 3.12</cite>.

**g.** After prepositions, e.g. <span lang="grc">ἀντὶ τοῦ ἐπὶ Κᾱρίᾱν ἰέναι . . . ἐπὶ Φρυγίᾱς ἐπορεύετο</span> _instead of going against Caria_, _he marched toward Phrygia_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Hell. 3.4.12">X. H. 3.4.12</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἄνευ τοῦ σωφρονεῖν</span> _without exercising self-control_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Mem. 4.3.1">X. M. 4.3.1</cite>. To express purpose the genitive with <span lang="grc">ὑπέρ</span> is very common: <span lang="grc">ὑπὲρ τοῦ τούτων γενέσθαι κύ̄ριος . . . πάντα πρᾱγματεύεται</span> _he devotes his every effort that he may become master of these_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 8.45">D. 8.45</cite>, <span lang="grc">ὑπὲρ τοῦ μὴ τὸ κελευόμενον ποιῆσαι</span> _in order not to do what was commanded_ 18. 204. Furthermore, after <span lang="grc">ἀπό, πρό, διά, μετά, περί, ὑπό, ἕνεκα, χάριν, χωρίς, πλήν, μέχρι</span>; and after adverbs. In Hdt. <span lang="grc">τοῦ</span> may be omitted after <span lang="grc">ἀντί</span>. <!-- page 452 -->

##### <a id="s2033"></a> §2033

**DATIVE OF THE ARTICULAR INFINITIVE**

**a.** With verbs, adjectives, and adverbs: thus, <span lang="grc">ἵνα . . . ἀπιστῶσι τῷ ἐμὲ τετῑμῆσθαι ὑπὸ δαιμόνων</span> _that they may distrust my having been honoured by divine powers_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Apol. 14">X. Ap. 14</cite>, <span lang="grc">τῷ ζῆν ἐστί τι ἐναντίον, ὥσπερ τῷ ἐγρηγορέναι τὸ καθεύδειν</span>; _is it something opposed to living, as sleeping to waking?_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Phaedo 71c">P. Ph. 71c</cite>, <span lang="grc">οὐδενὶ τῶν πάντων πλέον κεκράτηκε Φίλιππος ἢ τῷ πρότερος πρὸς τοῖς πρά̄γμασι γίγνεσθαι</span> _Philip has conquered us by nothing so much as by being beforehand in his operations_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 8.11">D. 8.11</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἅμα τῷ τῑμᾶν</span> _at the same time that_ we _honour_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Rep. 468e">P. R. 468e</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἴσον δὲ τῷ προστένειν</span> _equal to sorrowing beforehand_ <cite data-ref="Aesch. Ag. 252">A. Ag. 252</cite>.

**b.** After prepositions: e.g. <span lang="grc">οὐ γὰρ ἐπὶ τῷ δοῦλοι, ἀλλ᾽ ἐπὶ τῷ ὁμοῖοι τοῖς λειπομένοις εἶναι ἐκπέμπονται</span> (<span lang="grc">ἄποικοι</span>) _for colonists are not sent out on the basis of being inferiors, but on the basis of being the equals of those who are left at home_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 1.34">T. 1.34</cite>, <span lang="grc">ὁ μὲν πρὸς τῷ μηδὲν ἐκ τῆς πρεσβείᾱς λαβεῖν, τοὺς αἰχμαλώτους . . . ἐλύ̄σατο</span> _the one, in addition to gaining nothing from the embassy, ransomed the prisoners of war_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 19.229">D. 19.229</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἐν τῷ φρονεῖν γὰρ μηδὲν ἥδιστος βίος</span> _for life is sweetest in being conscious of nothing_ <cite data-ref="Soph. Aj. 553">S. Aj. 553</cite>.

##### <a id="s2034"></a> §2034

**ACCUSATIVE OF THE ARTICULAR INFINITIVE**

**a.** Object (cp. [1989](#s1989)): <span lang="grc">δείσᾱς τὸ ζῆν</span> _fearing to live_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Apol. 28d">P. A. 28d</cite>, <span lang="grc">μεῖζον μέν φαμεν κακὸν τὸ ἀδικεῖν, ἔλᾱττον δὲ τὸ ἀδικεῖσθαι</span> _we call doing wrong a greater evil, being wronged a lesser_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Gorg. 509c">P. G. 509c</cite>.

**b.** After prepositions: e.g. <span lang="grc">μέγιστον ἀγαθὸν τὸ πειθαρχεῖν φαίνεται εἰς τὸ καταπρά̄ττειν τἀ̄γαθά</span> _obedience appears to be an advantage of the greatest importance with regard to the successful accomplishment of excellent objects_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 8.1.3">X. C. 8.1.3</cite>, <span lang="grc">τῶν ἁπάντων ἀπερίοπτοί εἰσι παρὰ τὸ νῑκᾶν</span> _they are indifferent to everything in comparison with victory_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 1.41">T. 1.41</cite>, <span lang="grc">πρὸς τὸ μετρίων δεῖσθαι πεπαιδευμένος</span> _schooled to moderate needs_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Mem. 1.2.1">X. M. 1.2.1</cite>, <span lang="grc">πῶς ἔχεις πρὸς τὸ ἐθέλειν ἂν ἰέναι ἄκλητος ἐπὶ δεῖπνον</span>; _how do you feel about being willing to go uninvited to supper?_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Sym. 174a">P. S. 174a</cite> (cp. <span lang="grc">ἐθέλοις ἂν ἰέναι</span>). Furthermore, after <span lang="grc">διά, ἐπί, κατά, μετά, περί</span>.

**c.** The accusative of the infinitive with <span lang="grc">τό</span> appears after many verbs and verbal expressions which usually take only the simple infinitive. Such verbal expressions may be followed also by a genitive of a noun. Thus, <span lang="grc">τὸ σπεύδειν δέ σοι παραινῶ</span> _I commend speed to thee_ <cite data-ref="Soph. Phil. 620">S. Ph. 620</cite>, <span lang="grc">καρδίᾱς δ᾽ ἐξίσταμαι τὸ δρᾶν</span> _I withdraw from my resolution so as to_ ( = _and_) _do this thing_ <cite data-ref="Soph. Ant. 1105">S. Ant. 1105</cite>, <span lang="grc">μαθὼν γὰρ οὐκ ἂν ἀρνοίμην τὸ δρᾶν</span> _when I am informed_, _I will not refuse the deed_ <cite data-ref="Soph. Phil. 118">S. Ph. 118</cite>, <span lang="grc">τὸ προθῡμεῖσθαι δὲ συναύξειν τὸν οἶκον ἐπαιδεύομεν αὐτήν</span> _we trained her to show zeal in assisting to increase our estate_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Ec. 9.12">X. O. 9.12</cite> (cp. [1628](18-the-cases/07-accusative.md#s1628)), <span lang="grc">τὸ ἐρᾶν ἔξαρνος εἶ</span> _you refuse to love_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Lysis 205a">P. Lys. 205a</cite>.

**d.** So after adjectives. Thus, <span lang="grc">μακρὸς τὸ κρῖναι ταῦτα χὡ λοιπὸς χρόνος</span> _the future is long_ (i.e. time enough) _to decide this_ <cite data-ref="Soph. El. 1030">S. El. 1030</cite>.

**e.** This object infinitive after verbs is often an internal accusative. The accusative after verbs and nouns is, in many cases, like an accusative of respect ([1600](18-the-cases/07-accusative.md#s1600)); as <span lang="grc">τὸ δρᾶν οὐκ ἠθέλησαν</span> _they refused to do it_ <cite data-ref="Soph. OC 442">S. O. C. 442</cite>, <span lang="grc">αἰσχύ̄νονται τὸ τολμᾶν</span> _they are ashamed to dare_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Soph. 247b">P. Soph. 247b</cite>, <span lang="grc">οὐδ᾽ ἐμοί τοι τοὐξανιστάναι ἐστὶ θάρσος</span> _nor have I courage to remove thee_ <cite data-ref="Soph. OC 47">S. O. C. 47</cite>, <span lang="grc">τὸ μὲν ἐς τὴν γῆν ἡμῶν</span> <!-- page 453 --> <span lang="grc">ἐσβάλλειν . . . ἱκανοί εἰσι</span> _they are able to make an inroad into our country_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 6.17">T. 6.17</cite>. This infinitive after adjectives (and sometimes after verbs) occurs when the simple infinitive expresses purpose or result, as in <span lang="grc">τίς Μήδων . . . σοῦ ἀπελείφθη τὸ μή σοι ἀκολουθεῖν</span>; _what one of the Medes remained away from you so as not to attend you?_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 5.1.25">X. C. 5.1.25</cite>.

**f.** Some verbs take the articular infinitive as an object when the simple infinitive could not be used: <span lang="grc">μόνον ὁρῶν τὸ παίειν τὸν ἁλισκόμενον</span> _taking heed only to strike any one he caught_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 1.4.21">X. C. 1.4.21</cite>.

**g.** _Verbs of saying_ and _thinking_ rarely take the articular infinitive (also with <span lang="grc">ἄν</span>): <span lang="grc">ἐξομεῖ τὸ μὴ εἰδέναι</span>; _wilt thou swear thou didst not know?_ <cite data-ref="Soph. Ant. 535">S. Ant. 535</cite>, <span lang="grc">τῆς ἐλπίδος γὰρ ἔρχομαι δεδραγμένος, τὸ μὴ παθεῖν ἂν ἄλλο πλὴν τὸ μόρσιμον</span> _for I come with good grip on the hope that I can suffer nothing save what is my fate_ <cite data-ref="Soph. Ant. 235">S. Ant. 235</cite>.

**h.** On the use of the object infinitive with <span lang="grc">τὸ μή</span> and <span lang="grc">τὸ μὴ οὐ</span>, see [2744](35-negative-sentences.md#s2744) and 2749.

**i.** The accusative with the infinitive may stand in the absolute construction: <span lang="grc">ἐπεί γε τὸ ἐλθεῖν τοῦτον, οἶμαι θεόν τινα αὐτὸν ἐπ᾽ αὐτὴν ἀγαγεῖν τὴν τῑμωρίᾱν</span> _as for his coming_, _I believe that some god brought him to his very punishment_ Lyc. 91.

### OTHER USES OF THE ARTICULAR INFINITIVE

##### <a id="s2035"></a> §2035

**Apposition** (cp. [1987](#s1987)). The articular infinitive, in any case, is often used in apposition to a preceding word, especially a demonstrative.

<span lang="grc">τοῦτό ἐστι τὸ ἀδικεῖν, τὸ πλέον τῶν ἄλλων ζητεῖν ἔχειν</span> _injustice is this: to seek to have more than other people_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Gorg. 483c">P. G. 483c</cite>, <span lang="grc">τί γὰρ τούτου μακαριώτερον, τοῦ γῆ μιχθῆναι κτλ</span>. _for what is more blessed than this: to be commingled with the earth_, etc. <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 8.7.25">X. C. 8.7.25</cite>, <span lang="grc">δοκεῖ τούτῳ διαφέρειν ἀνὴρ τῶν ἄλλων ζῴων, τῷ τῑμῆς ὀρέγεσθαι</span> _man differs herein from other creatures that he aspires after honour_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Hiero 7.3">X. Hi. 7.3</cite>.

##### <a id="s2036"></a> §2036

**In Exclamation** (cp. [2015](#s2015)).—Thus, <span lang="grc">τῆς τύχης· τὸ ἐμὲ νῦν κληθέντα δεῦρο τυχεῖν</span> _my ill-luck! that I should happen now to have been summoned hither!_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 2.2.3">X. C. 2.2.3</cite>.

##### <a id="s2037"></a> §2037

**With Adjuncts**.—The articular infinitive may take various adjuncts including dependent clauses, the whole forming one large substantival idea.

<span lang="grc">τὸ μὲν γὰρ πόλλ᾽ ἀπολωλεκέναι κατὰ τὸν πόλεμον</span> _the fact that we have lost much in the war_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 1.10">D. 1.10</cite>, <span lang="grc">πέπεισμαι . . . τὰ πλείω τῶν πρᾱγμάτων ἡμᾶς ἐκπεφευγέναι τῷ μὴ βούλεσθαι τὰ δέοντα ποιεῖν, ἢ τῷ μὴ συνῑέναι</span> _I am persuaded that more of your advantages have escaped you from your not being willing to do your duty than from your ignorance_ 3. 3, <span lang="grc">καὶ γὰρ πάνυ μοι δοκεῖ ἄφρονος ἀνθρώπου εἶναι τὸ</span> (<span lang="grc">μεγάλου ἔργου ὄντος τοῦ ἑαυτῷ τὰ δέοντα παρασκευάζειν</span>) <span lang="grc">μὴ ἀρκεῖν τοῦτο, ἀλλὰ προσαναθέσθαι τὸ καὶ τοῖς ἄλλοις πολί̄ταις ὧν δέονται πορίζειν</span> _and in fact, since it is a serious business to provide for one's own necessities, it seems to me to be the part of an utter fool not to rest content with that, but in addition to take upon himself the burden of providing for the needs of the rest of the community_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Mem. 2.1.8">X. M. 2.1.8</cite>.

## CONSTRUCTIONS OF THE INFINITIVE WITH VERBS OF _hindering_

<!-- page 454 -->

##### <a id="s2038"></a> §2038

Verbs signifying (or suggesting) _to hinder_ take both the simple infinitive and the articular infinitive. Such verbs may take the strengthening but redundant negative <span lang="grc">μή</span> ([2739](35-negative-sentences.md#s2739)); and some, when themselves negatived or appearing in a question expecting a negative answer, admit the addition of the sympathetic <span lang="grc">οὐ</span> ([2742](35-negative-sentences.md#s2742)). Hence we have a variety of constructions (described in 2744 ff.)
