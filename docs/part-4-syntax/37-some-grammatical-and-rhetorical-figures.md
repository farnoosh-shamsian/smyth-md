# SOME GRAMMATICAL AND RHETORICAL FIGURES

##### <a id="s3004"></a> §3004

**Anacolūthon** (<span lang="grc">ἀνακόλουθον</span> _inconsequent_), or grammatical inconsistency, is inadvertent or purposed deviation in the structure of a sentence by which a construction started at the beginning is not followed out consistently. Anacoluthon is sometimes real, sometimes <!-- page 672 --> only slight or apparent. It is natural to Greek by reason of the mobility and elasticity of that language; but in English it could not be tolerated to an equal extent because our tongue—a speech of few inflected forms—is much more rigid than Greek.

##### <a id="s3005"></a> §3005

Anacoluthon is, in general, caused either (**a**) by the choice of some form of expression more convenient or more effective than that for which the sentence was grammatically planned; at times, too, the disturbing influence is the insertion of a brief expression of an additional thought not foreseen at the start. Or (**b**) by the intrusion of some explanation requiring a parenthesis of such an extent that the connection is obscured or the continuation of the original structure made difficult. In this case the beginning may be repeated, or what has already been said may be summed up in a different grammatical form and sometimes with the addition of a resumptive particle, such as <span lang="grc">δή, οὖν</span> _well then_, _then_, _as I was saying_ (<cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 1.8.13">X. A. 1.8.13</cite>, 3. 1. 20, <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 3.3.9">X. C. 3.3.9</cite>). So with <span lang="grc">δέ</span> (<cite data-ref="Thuc. 8.29">T. 8.29</cite>. 2).

##### <a id="s3006"></a> §3006

Anacoluthon usually produces the effect of naturalness and liveliness, sometimes of greater clearness (as after long parentheses), or of brevity, force, or concentration.

##### <a id="s3007"></a> §3007

Anacoluthon is either natural or artificial. Natural anacoluthon is seen in the loose and discursive style of Herodotus; in the closely packed sentences of Thucydides, who hurries from one thought to another with the least expenditure of words; and in the slovenliness of Andocides. Artificial or rhetorical anacoluthon is the result of a deliberate purpose to give to written language the vividness, naturalness, and unaffected freedom of the easy flow of conversation, and is best seen in the dialogues of Plato. Such anacoluthon is usually graceful and free from obscurity.

##### <a id="s3008"></a> §3008

There are very many forms of anacoluthon, e.g.

**a.** Many cases are due to the fact that a writer conforms his construction, not to the words which he has just used, but to another way in which the antecedent thought might have been expressed: the construction <span lang="grc">πρὸς τὸ νοούμενον</span> (or <span lang="grc">σημαινόμενον</span>) _according to what is thought_. Cp. [2148](22-the-participle.md#s2148) and <cite data-ref="Xen. Hell. 2.2.3">X. H. 2.2.3</cite>, <cite data-ref="Soph. OT 353">S. O. T. 353</cite>, <cite data-ref="Eur. Hec. 970">E. Hec. 970</cite>.

**b.** Some cases are due to changes in the subject, as <cite data-ref="Thuc. 1.18">T. 1.18</cite>. 2.

**c.** Many cases occur in connection with the use of a participle ([2147](22-the-participle.md#s2147), [2148](22-the-participle.md#s2148)).

**d.** Coördinate clauses connected by <span lang="grc">τὲ . . . καί, καὶ . . . καί, οὔτε . . . οὔτε, ἢ . . . ἤ</span> often show anacoluthon, especially when a finite verb takes the place of a participle. Cp. [2147 c](22-the-participle.md#s2147), and <cite data-ref="Thuc. 5.61">T. 5.61</cite>. 4, 6. 32. 3, 7. 47. 1-2.

**e.** The nominative “in suspense” may stand at the head of a sentence instead of another case required by the following construction. This involves a redundant pronoun. Thus, <span lang="grc">Πρόξενος δὲ καὶ Μένων, ἐπείπερ εἰσὶν ὑ̄μέτεροι εὐεργέται . . . πέμψατε αὐτοὺς δεῦρο</span> (for <span lang="grc">Πρόξενον καὶ Μένωνα . . . πέμψατε δεῦρο</span>) <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 2.5.41">X. A. 2.5.41</cite>. Cp. “The prince that feeds great natures, they will slay him:” Ben Jonson.

**f.** The accusative often stands absolutely when at the head of a sentence. Thus, <span lang="grc">ἀλλὰ μὴν καὶ τῑμά̄ς γε . . ., τῶν μὲν μεθέξει καὶ γεύσεται ἑκών, ᾱ&lt;\*&gt;`ς ἂν ἡγῆται ἀμείνω αὑτὸν ποιήσειν, ἃ̄ς δ᾽ . . . φεύξεται</span> _but furthermore as regards honours_, _those he will partake of and be glad to taste which he thinks will make him a_ <!-- page 673 --> _better man, but others he will shun_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Rep. 591e">P. R. 591e</cite>, <span lang="grc">Ἕλληνας τοὺς ἐν τῇ Ἀσίᾳ οἰκοῦντας οὐδέν πω σαφὲς λέγεται εἰ ἕπονται</span> (for <span lang="grc">λέγουσιν εἰ ἕπονται</span> or <span lang="grc">λέγεται ἕπεσθαι</span>) _as to the Greeks who dwell in Asia there is as yet no certain intelligence whether they are to accompany_ the expedition <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 2.1.5">X. C. 2.1.5</cite>.

**g.** A main clause may take the construction of a parenthetical clause (<cite data-ref="Thuc. 4.93">T. 4.93</cite>. 2). Here belongs the attraction into the relative clause of a verb that should have been principal. So after <span lang="grc">ὡς ἤκουσα, ὡς οἶμαι, ὡς λέγουσι</span>, etc. Thus, <span lang="grc">τόδε γε μήν, ὡς οἶμαι, περὶ αὐτοῦ ἀναγκαιότατον εἶναι</span> (for <span lang="grc">ἐστὶ</span>) <span lang="grc">λέγειν</span> _this indeed is_, _as I think_, _most necessary to state about it_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Phileb. 20d">P. Phil. 20d</cite>. Often in Hdt., as <span lang="grc">ὡς δ᾽ ἐγὼ ἤκουσα . . . εἶναι αὐτὸν Ἰδανθύρσου . . . πάτρων</span> _but as I have heard he was the uncle of Idanthyrsus on the father's side_ 4. 76. A construction may be introduced by <span lang="grc">ὅτι</span> or <span lang="grc">ὡς</span> and then pass to the infinitive, or the infinitive may precede and a finite verb follow ([2628](31-dependent-substantive-clauses-2574-2635.md#s2628)).

**h.** After a subordinate clause with parentheses the main clause sometimes follows in the form of an independent sentence (<cite data-ref="Plat. Apol. 28c">P. A. 28c</cite>, cp. [36](../part-1-letters-sounds-syllables-accent/01-the-letters.md#s36) a).

**i.** An infinitive may resume the idea set forth by the principal verb; as <span lang="grc">τοῦ δὲ θεοῦ τάττοντος, ὡς ἐγὼ ᾠήθην τε καὶ ὑπέλαβον, φιλοσοφοῦντά με δεῖν ζῆν κτλ</span>. _whereas when God orders me, as I think and believe, to pass my life in the pursuit of wisdom_, etc. <cite data-ref="Plat. Apol. 28e">P. A. 28e</cite>. Cp. <cite data-ref="Xen. Hell. 7.4.35">X. H. 7.4.35</cite>.

**j.** Anacoluthon is sometimes due to the desire to maintain similarity of form between contrasted expressions; as <span lang="grc">τοὺς μὲν γὰρ ἱπποκενταύρους οἶμαι ἔγωγε πολλοῖς μὲν ἀπορεῖν τῶν ἀνθρώποις ηὑρημένων ἀγαθῶν ὅπως δεῖ χρῆσθαι, πολλοῖς δὲ τῶν ἵπποις πεφῡκότων ἡδέων πῶς αὐτῶν χρὴ ἀπολαύειν</span> _for I think that the horse-centaurs were at a loss how to make use of many conveniences devised for men and how to enjoy many of the pleasures natural to horses_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 4.3.19">X. C. 4.3.19</cite>. Here <span lang="grc">πολλοῖς δέ</span> is used as if it were to be governed by <span lang="grc">χρῆσθαι</span>, instead of which <span lang="grc">αὐτῶν ἀπολαύειν</span> is substituted.

##### <a id="s3009"></a> §3009

**Anadiplōsis** (<span lang="grc">ἀναδίπλωσις</span> _doubling_) is the rhetorical repetition of one or several words. Cp. “The Isles of Greece, the Isles of Greece, where burning Sappho loved and sung:” Byron.

<span lang="grc">Θῆβαι δέ, Θῆβαι πόλις ἀστυγείτων, μεθ᾽ ἡμέρᾱν μίαν ἐκ μέσης τῆς Ἑλλάδος ἀνήρπασται</span> _Thebes_, _Thebes_, _a neighbouring city, in the course of one day has been extirpated from the midst of Greece_ <cite data-ref="Aeschin. 3.133">Aes. 3.133</cite>.

##### <a id="s3010"></a> §3010

**Anaphora** (<span lang="grc">ἀναφορά̄</span> _carrying back_) is the repetition, with emphasis, of the same word or phrase at the beginning of several successive clauses. This figure is also called _epanaphora_ or _epanalepsis_. Cp. “Strike as I would Have struck those tyrants! Strike deep as my curse! Strike! and but once:” Byron.

<span lang="grc">οὗτοι γὰρ πολλοὺς μὲν τῶν πολῑτῶν εἰς τοὺς πολεμίους ἐξήλασαν, πολλοὺς δ᾽ ἀδίκως ἀποκτείναντες ἀτάφους ἐποίησαν, πολλοὺς δ᾽ ἐπιτἱ̄μους ὄντας ἀτί̄μους κατέστησαν</span> _many of the citizens they drove out to the enemy; many they slew unjustly and left unburied; many who were in possession of their civic rights they deprived of them_ <cite data-ref="Lys. 12.21">L. 12.21</cite>. Cp. <cite data-ref="Dem. 18.48">D. 18.48</cite>, 75, 121, 310.

##### <a id="s3011"></a> §3011

**Anastrophe** (<span lang="grc">ἀναστροφή</span> _return_) is the use, at the beginning of one clause, of the same word that concluded the preceding clause. <!-- page 674 --> Also called _epanastrophe_. Cp. “Has he a gust for blood? Blood shall fill his cup.”

<span lang="grc">οὐ δήπου Κτησιφῶντα δύναται διώκειν δι᾽ ἐμέ, ἐμὲ δ᾽ εἴπερ ἐξελέγξειν ἐνόμιζεν, αὐτὸν οὐκ ἂν ἐγράψατο</span> _for surely it cannot be that he is prosecuting Ctesiphon on my account_, _and yet would not have indicted me myself, if he had thought that he could convict me_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 18.13">D. 18.13</cite>.

##### <a id="s3012"></a> §3012

**Antistrophe** (<span lang="grc">ἀντιστροφή</span> _turning about_) is the repetition of the same word or phrase at the end of successive clauses.

<span lang="grc">ὅστις δ᾽ ἐν τῷ πρώτῳ λόγῳ τὴν ψῆφον αἰτεῖ ὅρκον αἰτεῖ, νόμον αἰτεῖ, δημοκρατίᾱν αἰτεῖ</span> _whoever in his first speech asks for your vote_ as a favour, _asks the surrender of your oath, asks the surrender of the law, asks the surrender of the democratic constitution_ <cite data-ref="Aeschin. 3.198">Aes. 3.198</cite>.

##### <a id="s3013"></a> §3013

**Antithesis** (<span lang="grc">ἀντίθεσις</span> _opposition_) is the contrast of ideas expressed by words which are the opposite of, or are closely contrasted with, each other. Cp. “Wit is negative, analytical, destructive; Humor is creative:” Whipple.

<span lang="grc">δι᾽ ὧν ἐκ χρηστῶν φαῦλα τὰ πρά̄γματα τῆς πόλεως γέγονε, διὰ τούτων ἐλπίζετε τῶν αὐτῶν πρά̄ξεων ἐκ φαύλων αὐτὰ χρηστὰ γενήσεσθαι</span>; _do you expect that the affairs of state will become prosperous instead of bad by the same measures by which they have become bad instead of prosperous?_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 2.26">D. 2.26</cite>.

**a.** Antithesis is sometimes extended to a parallelism in sense effected (1) by the use of two words of opposite meaning in the expression of one idea, (2) by the opposition of ideas which are not specifically contrasted in words.

##### <a id="s3014"></a> §3014

**Aporia** (<span lang="grc">ἀπορίᾱ</span> _doubt_) is an artifice by which a speaker feigns doubt as to where he shall begin or end or what he shall do or say, etc. Cp. “Then the steward said within himself, What shall I do?” St. Luke 16. 3.

<span lang="grc">ἀπορῶ τοῦ πρώτου μνησθῶ</span> _I am uncertain what I shall recall first_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 18.129">D. 18.129</cite>. When the doubt is between _two_ courses it is often called _diaporēsis_.

##### <a id="s3015"></a> §3015

**Aposiopēsis** (<span lang="grc">ἀποσιώπησις</span> _becoming silent_) is a form of ellipse by which, under the influence of passionate feeling or of modesty, a speaker comes to an abrupt halt. Examples 2352 d, <cite data-ref="Dem. 18.3">D. 18.3</cite>, 22, 195, <cite data-ref="Soph. OT 1289">S. O. T. 1289</cite>, <cite data-ref="Aristoph. Wasps 1178">Ar. Vesp. 1178</cite>. Cp. “Massachusetts and her people . . . hold him, and his love . . . and his principles, and his standard of truth in utter—what shall I say? —anything but respect:” Webster.

##### <a id="s3016"></a> §3016

**Asyndeton** (<span lang="grc">ἀσύνδετον</span> _not bound together_) is the absence of conjunctions in a series of coördinate words or phrases. See [2165](26-syntax-of-the-compound-sentence.md#s2165) ff.

**a.** Here is sometimes placed the omission of the verb after <span lang="grc">μή</span> (<span lang="grc">μὴ σύ γε, μὴ γάρ</span>, etc.); as <span lang="grc">μὴ τριβὰ̄ς ἔτι</span> (<span lang="grc">ποιεῖσθε</span>) _no more delays!_ <cite data-ref="Soph. Ant. 577">S. Ant. 577</cite>, <span lang="grc">τι´ς οὐχὶ κατέπτυσεν ἂν σοῦ; μὴ γὰρ</span> (<span lang="grc">εἰπὲ</span>) <span lang="grc">τῆς πόλεως γε, μηδ᾽ ἐμοῦ</span> _who would not have reviled_ you? _Do not say the State_, _nor me_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 18.200">D. 18.200</cite>. Cp. [946](09-the-predicate.md#s946), [1599](18-the-cases/07-accusative.md#s1599).

##### <a id="s3017"></a> §3017

**Brachylogy** (<span lang="grc">βραχυλογίᾱ</span> _brevity of diction, abbreviated expres. sion_ or _construction_) is a concise form of expression by which an <!-- page 675 --> element is not repeated or is omitted when its repetition or use would make the thought or the grammatical construction complete. The suppressed element must be supplied from some corresponding word in the context, in which case it often appears with some change of form or construction; or it must be taken from the connection of the thought.

**a.** _Brachylogy_ and _ellipse_ cannot always be distinguished sharply. In ellipse the suppressed word is not to be supplied from a corresponding word in the context; and, in general, ellipse is less artificial and less dependent on the momentary and arbitrary will of the speaker or writer. Compendious Comparison ([1501](18-the-cases/06-dative.md#s1501)), Praegnans Constructio ([3044](#s3044)), and Zeugma ([3048](#s3048)) are forms of brachylogy.

##### <a id="s3018"></a> §3018

There are many forms of brachylogy; for example:

**a.** One verbal form must often be supplied from another; e.g. a passive from an active, an infinitive from a finite verb, a participle from an infinitive. Thus, <span lang="grc">τὴν τῶν πέλας δῃοῦν μᾶλλον ἢ τὴν ἑαυτῶν ὁρᾶν</span> (<span lang="grc">δῃουμένην</span>) _rather to ravage the territory of their neighbours than to see their own_ (_being ravaged_) <cite data-ref="Thuc. 2.11">T. 2.11</cite>, <span lang="grc">ταῦτα ἐγώ σοι οὐ πείθομαι . . ., οἶμαι δὲ οὐδὲ ἄλλον ἀνθρώπων οὐδένα</span> (<span lang="grc">πείθεσθαί σοι</span>) _of this I am not persuaded by you and I do not believe that any other human being is either_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Apol. 25e">P. A. 25e</cite>, <span lang="grc">οὔτε πάσχοντες κακὸν οὐδὲν οὔτε μέλλοντες</span> (<span lang="grc">πάσχειν</span>) _neither suffering_, _nor being likely_ (_to suffer_), _any evil_ <cite data-ref="Isoc. 12.103">I. 12.103</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἀνεχώρησαν δὲ καὶ οἱ Αθηναῖοι . . . , ἐπειδὴ καὶ ἐκείνους εἶδον</span> (<span lang="grc">ἀναχωρήσαντας</span>) _and the Athenians too withdrew when they saw that they_ (the Lacedaemonians) _had done so_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 3.16">T. 3.16</cite>.

**b.** A verb must often be supplied from a coördinate or subordinate clause either preceding or following. Thus, <span lang="grc">ἔγειρε καὶ σὺ τήνδ᾽, ἐγὼ δὲ σέ</span> _do you wake her_, _as I_ (_wake_) _you_ <cite data-ref="Aesch. Eum. 140">A. Eum. 140</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἐὰ̄ν δὲ αὐτόχειρ μὲν μή</span> (<span lang="grc">ᾖ</span>), <span lang="grc">βουλεύσῃ δὲ θάνατόν τις ἄλλος ἑτέρῳ</span> _if a person shall not kill with his own hand, but if some one shall suggest murder to another_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Laws 872a">P. L. 872a</cite>; <span lang="grc">φίλους νομίζουσ᾽ οὕσπερ ἂν πόσις σέθεν</span> (<span lang="grc">νομίζῃ</span>) _regarding as friends even those whom thy husband_ (_so regards_) <cite data-ref="Eur. Med. 1153">E. Med. 1153</cite>. A verb is rarely supplied from the subordinate to the main construction.

**c.** In clauses with <span lang="grc">δεῖ, χρή</span> etc.: <span lang="grc">ἵνα φαίνησθε ἀμύ̄νοντες οἷς δεῖ</span> (<span lang="grc">ἀμύ̄νειν</span>) _that you may seem to assist those you ought_ (_to assist_) <cite data-ref="Thuc. 3.13">T. 3.13</cite>. When a form of <span lang="grc">τυγχάνω</span> stands in the subordinate clause; <span lang="grc">ἀπέπλευσαν ὡς ἕκαστοι ἔτυχον</span> (<span lang="grc">ἀποπλέοντες</span>) _they sailed away as each best could_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 4.25">T. 4.25</cite>. In conditional clauses when the protasis indicates that the assertion made in the apodosis holds true of a person or a thing more than of any other person or thing (<span lang="grc">εἴπερ τις καὶ ἄλλος, εἴπερ που, εἴπερ ποτέ, ὥς τις καὶ ἄλλος</span>, etc.); as <span lang="grc">συμφέρει δ᾽ ὑ̄μῖν, εἴπερ τῳ καὶ ἄλλῳ, τὸ νῑκᾶν</span> _victory is of advantage to you, if it_ (_is of advantage_) _to any_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 3.3.42">X. C. 3.3.42</cite>. Hence <span lang="grc">εἴ τις</span> (<span lang="grc">που, ποθεν</span>) is almost = <span lang="grc">τὶς</span>, etc. (<cite data-ref="Thuc. 7.21">T. 7.21</cite>. 5).

**d.** Compound verbs (especially those compounded with <span lang="grc">μετά</span> and <span lang="grc">ἐξ</span>) are often so used that the force both of the compound and of the simple verb is requisite to the meaning. Thus, (<span lang="grc">οἱ \*᾿αθηναῖοι</span>) <span lang="grc">μετέγνωσαν Κερκῡραίοις ξυμμαχίᾱν μὴ ποιήσασθαι</span> _the Athenians changed their minds_ and decided _not to make an alliance with the Corcyraeans_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 1.44">T. 1.44</cite>.

**e.** A compound verb on its second occurrence often omits the preposition (rarely _vice versa_); as <span lang="grc">ἀπεργάζηται . . . εἰργάζετο</span> <cite data-ref="Plat. Phaedo 104d">P. Ph. 104d</cite>. Euripides is <!-- page 676 --> fond of such collocations as <span lang="grc">ὑπάκουσον ἄκουσον</span> Alc. 400. Cp. the difference in metrical value of repeated words in Shakespeare, as “These víolént desires have víolent ends.”

N.—In <span lang="grc">καὶ ξυμμετίσχω καὶ φέρω τῆς αἰτίᾱς</span> _I share and bear alike the guilt_ (<cite data-ref="Soph. Ant. 537">S. Ant. 537</cite>) <span lang="grc">φέρω</span>, though capable of taking the partitive genitive, is influenced by <span lang="grc">ξυμμετίσχω</span> and has the force of <span lang="grc">ξυμφέρω</span>.

**f.** From a following verb of special meaning a verb of more general meaning, such as <span lang="grc">ποιεῖν, γίγνεσθαι, εἶναι</span>, must be supplied with the phrases <span lang="grc">οὐδὲν ἄλλο ἤ, ἄλλο τι ἤ, τί ἄλλο ἤ</span>. Examples in 946, 2652, 2778.

**g.** A _verb of saying_ or _thinking_ must often be supplied from a foregoing _verb of exhorting_, _commanding_, _announcing_, or from any other verb that implies _saying_ or _thinking_. Thus, <span lang="grc">Κριτόβουλος καὶ Ἀπολλόδωρος κελεύουσί με τριά̄κοντα μνῶν τῑμήσασθαι, αὐτοὶ δὲ ἐγγυᾶσθαι</span> _Critobulus and Apollodorus urge me to set a penalty of thirty minae, and_ (_say_) _that they themselves are sureties_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Apol. 38b">P. A. 38b</cite>.

**h.** When two verbs taking the same or different cases have an object in common, that object is expressed only once, and usually is dependent on the nearer verb. See [1634](18-the-cases/07-accusative.md#s1634), [1635](18-the-cases/07-accusative.md#s1635).

**i.** A substantive or a verb is often to be supplied from a substantive or a verb related in meaning: <span lang="grc">ναυμαχήσαντας μίαν</span> (<span lang="grc">ναυμαχίᾱν</span>) _having fought one_ (_sea-fight_) <cite data-ref="Aristoph. Frogs 693">Ar. Ran. 693</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἡ μὲν ἔπειτα εἰς ἅλα ἆλτο . . ., Ζεὺς δὲ ἑὸν πρὸς δῶμα</span> (<span lang="grc">ἔβη</span>) _she then sprang into the sea, but Zeus_ (_went_) _to his abode_ A 532.

**j.** The subject of a sentence is often taken from a preceding object or from some other preceding noun in an oblique case without a pronoun of reference to aid the transition. Thus, <span lang="grc">ἐξεφόβησαν μὲν τοὺς πολλοὺς οὐκ εἰδότας τὰ πρᾱσσόμενα, καὶ ἔφευγον</span> (<span lang="grc">οἱ πολλοί</span>) _they frightened away most_ of the citizens, _who were in ignorance of the plot and began to fly_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 8.44">T. 8.44</cite>. Cp. [943](08-case-of-the-subject-the-nominative.md#s943).

**k.** In general an object is frequently omitted when it can readily be supplied from the context. Thus, <span lang="grc">ἐγχεῖν</span> (<span lang="grc">τὸν οἶνον</span>) <span lang="grc">ἐκέλενε</span> _he gave orders to pour in_ (_the wine_) <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 4.3.13">X. A. 4.3.13</cite>. An unemphatic pronoun in an oblique case is often omitted when it can be supplied from a preceding noun. Cp. [1214](17-pronouns.md#s1214).

**l.** A dependent noun must often be supplied, in a different construction, from one coördinate clause to another. Thus, <span lang="grc">ὅρκους ἔλαβον καὶ ἔδοσαν παρὰ Φαρναβάζου</span> _they received oaths from Pharnabazus and gave_ him theirs <cite data-ref="Xen. Hell. 1.3.9">X. H. 1.3.9</cite>. So in contrasts where one member is to be supplied from the other, as <span lang="grc">οὐκ ἐκεῖνος</span> (<span lang="grc">ἐκείνην</span>), <span lang="grc">ἀλλ᾽ ἐκείνη κεῖνον ἐνθάδ᾽ ἤγαγεν</span> _he did not bring_ (_her_) _here_, _but she brought him_ <cite data-ref="Eur. Orest. 742">E. Or. 742</cite>.

**m.** From a preceding word its opposite must often be supplied, especially an affirmative after a negative. Thus, <span lang="grc">ἀμελήσᾱς ὧνπερ οἱ πολλοὶ</span> (<span lang="grc">ἐπιμελοῦνται</span>) _neglecting the very things which most people_ (_care for_) <cite data-ref="Plat. Apol. 36b">P. A. 36b</cite>. This laxity of expression is especially frequent in the case of <span lang="grc">ἕκαστος, τὶς</span>, or <span lang="grc">πάντες</span>, to be supplied after <span lang="grc">οὐδείς</span> (<span lang="grc">μηδείς</span>), as <span lang="grc">μηδεὶς τὴν ὑπερβολὴν θαυμάσῃ, ἀλλὰ μετ᾽ εὐνοίᾱς δ̀ λέγω θεωρησάτω</span> _let no one wonder at the extravagance of my statement, but let_ (_every one_) _consider kindly what I say_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 18.199">D. 18.199</cite>. Cp. “No person held to service or labor in one state . . ., escaping into another, shall . . . be discharged from said service or labor, but shall be delivered up, etc.”: U. S. Constitution.

**n.** The same word though placed only once may stand in two different constructions; as <span lang="grc">αἰνέω δὲ καὶ τόνδε</span> (<span lang="grc">νόμον</span>) . . . <span lang="grc">μήτε τῶν ἄλλων Περσέων μηδένα τῶν</span> <!-- page 677 --> <span lang="grc">ἑωυτοῦ οἰκετέων . . . ἀνήκεστον πάθος ἔρδειν</span> _and I approve also this custom that no one of the other Persians shall do irremediable hurt to any one of his own servants_ <cite data-ref="Hdt. 1.137">Hdt. 1.137</cite>. Here <span lang="grc">μηδένα</span> is both subject and object of <span lang="grc">ἔρδειν</span>.

**o.** An assertion may be made concerning an action or a thing when the absence of that action or thing is meant (_res pro rei defectu_). Thus, <span lang="grc">εἴ τ᾽ ἄρ᾽ ὁγ᾽ εὐχωλῆς ἐπιμέμφεται</span> _whether then he blames us on account of an_ (_unfulfilled_) _vow_ A 65, <span lang="grc">ἐν ᾗ καὶ περὶ χρημάτων καὶ περὶ ἀτῑμίᾱς ἄνθρωποι κινδῡνεύουσιν</span> _on which_ charge _men run the risk both of_ (_loss of_) _money and civil degradation_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 29.16">D. 29.16</cite>. So <span lang="grc">δύναμις</span> _powerlessness_, <span lang="grc">φυλακή</span> _neglect of the watch_, <span lang="grc">μελέτημα</span> _lack of liberal exercise_.

##### <a id="s3019"></a> §3019

**Catachrēsis** (<span lang="grc">κατάχρησις</span> _misuse of a word_) is the extension of the meaning of a word beyond its proper sphere; especially a violent metaphor. In English: “a palatable tone,” “to take arms against a sea of troubles.”

<span lang="grc">δαιμόνιος</span> _extraordinary_, <span lang="grc">θαυμάσιος</span> _decided_, _strange_, _capital_, <span lang="grc">ἀμηχάνως</span> and <span lang="grc">ὑπερφυῶς</span> _decidedly_, <span lang="grc">ὑποπτεύω</span> _expect_, <span lang="grc">ναυστολεῖν χθόνα</span> <cite data-ref="Eur. Med. 682">E. Med. 682</cite>. Such usages are less often occasioned by the poverty of the language than by the caprice of the writer.

##### <a id="s3020"></a> §3020

**Chiasmus** (<span lang="grc">χῑασμός</span> _marking with diagonal lines like a_ X) is the crosswise arrangement of contrasted pairs to give alternate stress. By this figure both the extremes and the means are correlated. Cp. “Sweet is the breath of morn, her rising sweet”: Milton. <!-- figure: no image in source -->

<span lang="grc">ἓν σῶμ᾽ ἔχων καὶ ψῡχὴν μίαν</span> _having one body and one soul_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 19.227">D. 19.227</cite>.

So <span lang="grc">τοσοῦτον σὺ ἐμοῦ σοφώτερος εἶ τηλικούτου ὄντος τηλικόσδ᾽ ὤν</span>; _are you at your age so much wiser than I at mine?_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Apol. 25d">P. A. 25d</cite>, <span lang="grc">πᾶν μὲν ἔργον πᾶν δ᾽ ἔπος λέγοντάς τε καὶ πρά̄ττοντας</span> lit. _doing every deed and uttering every word_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Rep. 494e">P. R. 494e</cite>, <span lang="grc">δουλεύειν καὶ ἄρχεσθαι . . . ἄρχειν καὶ δεσπόζειν</span> _to be a slave and be ruled . . . to rule and be a master_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Phaedo 80a">P. Ph. 80a</cite>.

##### <a id="s3021"></a> §3021

**Climax** (<span lang="grc">κλῖμαξ</span> _ladder_) is an arrangement of clauses in succession whereby the last important word of one is repeated as the first important word of the next, each clause in turn surpassing its predecessor in the importance of the thought. Cp. “But we glory in tribulations also: knowing that tribulation worketh patience . . . and experience, hope; and hope maketh not ashamed”: Romans v. 3-5.

<span lang="grc">οὐκ εἶπον μὲν ταῦτα, οὐκ ἔγραψα δέ, οὐδ᾽ ἔγραψα μέν, οὐκ ἐπρέσβευσα δέ, οὐδ᾽ ἐπρέσβευσα μέν, οὐκ ἔπεισα δὲ Θηβαίους</span> _I did not utter these words without proposing a motion; nor did I propose a motion without becoming ambassador; nor did I become ambassador without convincing the Thebans_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 18.179">D. 18.179</cite>; cp. [4](../part-1-letters-sounds-syllables-accent/01-the-letters.md#s4). 19. This figure is very rare in Greek.

##### <a id="s3022"></a> §3022

**Ellipse** (<span lang="grc">ἔλλειψις</span> _leaving out, defect_) is the suppression of a word or of several words of minor importance to the logical expres<!-- page 678 --> sion of the thought, but necessary to the construction. Ellipse gives brevity, force, and liveliness; it is usually readily to be supplied, often unconscious, and appears especially in common phrases, constructions, and expressions of popular speech (such as <span lang="grc">ἐξ ὀνύχων λέοντα</span> to judge _a lion from his claws_).

**a.** Ellipse occurs in the case of substantives and pronouns, subject, object, finite verbs, main clauses, and (less often) subordinate clauses. See the Index under _Ellipse_.

##### <a id="s3023"></a> §3023

**Enallage** (<span lang="grc">ἐναλλαγή</span> _interchange_) is the substitution of one grammatical form for another, as plural for singular ([1006](11-peculiarities-in-the-use-of-number.md#s1006)-[1008](11-peculiarities-in-the-use-of-number.md#s1008)). Thus: “They fall successive, and successive rise”: Pope.

##### <a id="s3024"></a> §3024

**Euphemism** (<span lang="grc">εὐφημισμός</span> lit. _speaking favourably_) is the substitution of a less direct expression in place of one whose plainer meaning might be unpleasant or offensive. Thus: “The merchant prince had stopped payment” (for “became bankrupt”).

<span lang="grc">συμφορά̄</span> _occurrence_ for <span lang="grc">ἀτύχημα</span> _misfortune_, <span lang="grc">ἑτέρως</span> _otherwise_ = _not well_, <span lang="grc">εὐφρόνη</span> ‘the kindly time’ for <span lang="grc">νύξ</span> _night_, <span lang="grc">εὐώνυμος</span> _left_ (lit. _of good omen_, whereas the left was the unlucky side), <span lang="grc">εἴ τι πάθοι</span> _if anything should happen to him_ = _if he should die_.

##### <a id="s3025"></a> §3025

**Hendiadys** (<span lang="grc">ἓν διὰ δυοῖν</span> _one by two_) is the use of two words connected by a copulative conjunction to express a single complex idea; especially two substantives instead of one substantive and an adjective or attributive genitive.

<span lang="grc">χρόνῳ καὶ πολιορκίᾳ</span> _by length of time and siege_ = _by a long siege_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 19.123">D. 19.123</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἐν ἁλὶ κύ̄μασί τε</span> _in the waves of the sea_ <cite data-ref="Eur. Hel. 226">E. Hel. 226</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἀσπίδων τε καὶ στρατοῦ ῀ ὡπλισμένου στρατοῦ</span> _armed force_ <cite data-ref="Soph. El. 36">S. El. 36</cite>.

##### <a id="s3026"></a> §3026

**Homoioteleuton** (<span lang="grc">ὁμοιοτέλευτος</span> _ending alike_) is end-rhyme in clauses or verses.

<span lang="grc">τὴν μὲν ἀρχὴν εἰς τὸν πόλεμον κατέστησαν ὡς ἐλευθερώσοντες τοὺς Ἕλληνας, ἐπὶ δὲ τελευτῆς οὕτω πολλοὺς αὐτῶν ἐκδότους ἐποίησαν, καὶ τῆς μὲν ἡμετέρᾱς πόλεως τοὺς Ἴωνας ἀπέστησαν, ἐξ ἧς ἀπῴκησαν καὶ δι᾽ ἣν πολλάκις ἐσώθησαν</span> _in the beginning they entered upon the war with the avowed object of liberating the Greeks_, _at the end they have betrayed so many of them, and have caused the Ionians to revolt from our State_, _from which they emigrated and thanks to which they were often saved_ <cite data-ref="Isoc. 4.122">I. 4.122</cite>. Cp. <cite data-ref="Soph. Aj. 62">S. Aj. 62</cite>-65. _Homoioteleuton_ is most marked in _paromoiosis_.

##### <a id="s3027"></a> §3027

**Hypallage** (<span lang="grc">ὑπαλλαγή</span> _exchange_) is a change in the relation of words by which a word, instead of agreeing with the case it logically qualifies, is made to agree grammatically with another case. Hypallage is almost always confined to poetry.

<span lang="grc">ἐμὰ κήδεα θῡμοῦ</span> _the troubles of my spirit_ <cite data-ref="Hom. Od. 14.197"><span lang="grc">ξ</span> 197</cite>, <span lang="grc">νεῖκος ἀνδρῶν ξύναιμον</span> _kindred strife of men_ for _strife of kindred men_ <cite data-ref="Soph. Ant. 794">S. Ant. 794</cite>. Here the adjective virtually agrees with the rest of the phrase taken as a compound. <!-- page 679 -->

##### <a id="s3028"></a> §3028

**Hyperbaton** (<span lang="grc">ὑπέρβατον</span> _transposition_) is the separation of words naturally belonging together. Such displacement usually gives prominence to the first of two words thus separated, but sometimes to the second also. In prose hyperbaton is less common than in poetry, but even in prose it is frequent, especially when it secures emphasis on an important idea by placing it at the beginning or end of a sentence. At times hyperbaton may mark passionate excitement. Sometimes it was adopted to gain rhythmical effect. Thus: “Such resting found the sole of unblest feet”: Milton.

<span lang="grc">σὺ δὲ αὐτός, ὦ πρὸς θεῶν, Μένων, τί φῂς ἀρετὴν εἶναι</span>; _but what do you yourself_, _in heaven's name_, _Meno_, _say virtue is?_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Meno 71d">P. Men. 71d</cite>, <span lang="grc">ὦ πρός σε γονάτων</span> ([946](09-the-predicate.md#s946)) _by thy knees_ (_I entreat_) _thee_ <cite data-ref="Eur. Med. 324">E. Med. 324</cite>, <span lang="grc">ὑφ᾽ ἑνὸς τοιαῦτα πέπονθεν ἡ Ἑλλὰς ἀνθρώπου</span> _from one man Greece endured such sufferings_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 18.158">D. 18.158</cite>, <span lang="grc">κρατῶν τοὺς ὁποιουσδήποθ᾽ ὑ̄μεῖς ἐξεπέμπετε στρατηγούς</span> _conquering the generals you kept sending out—such as they were_ 18. 146.

**a.** The displacement is often caused by the intrusion of a clause of contrast or explanation. Thus <span lang="grc">τοὺς περὶ Ἀρχίᾱν . . . οὐ ψῆφον ἀνεμείνατε ἀλλ᾽ . . . ἐτῑμωρήσασθε</span> _you did not postpone your vote but took vengeance upon Archias and his company_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Hell. 7.3.7">X. H. 7.3.7</cite>.

**b.** Adverbs and particles may be displaced. Thus, <span lang="grc">οὕτω τις ἔρως δεινός</span> _a passion so terrible_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Theaet. 169c">P. Th. 169c</cite>, <span lang="grc">πολὺ γὰρ τῶν ἵππων ἔτρεχον θᾶττον</span> _for they ran much faster than the horses_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 1.5.2">X. A. 1.5.2</cite>; so <span lang="grc">εὖ, μάλα</span>; on <span lang="grc">ἄν</span> see [1764](20-the-verb-voices/06-the-particle-alphanu.md#s1764).

**c.** Prepositions often cause the displacement ([1663](19-prepositions.md#s1663), [2690](35-negative-sentences.md#s2690)). On displacement in connection with participles see [1166](16-the-article-origin-and-development.md#s1166), [1167](16-the-article-origin-and-development.md#s1167); with the negatives, see [2690](35-negative-sentences.md#s2690) ff.

**d.** Similar or contrasted words are often brought into juxtaposition. Here a nominative precedes an oblique case. Thus, <span lang="grc">ἀπὸ τῶν ὑ̄μετέρων ὑ̄μῖν πολεμεῖ συμμάχων</span> _he wages war on you from_ the resources of _your allies_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 4.34">D. 4.34</cite>, <span lang="grc">οὐ γάρ τίς με βίῃ γε ἑκὼν ἀέκοντα δίηται</span> _for no one shall chase me by force, he willing me unwilling_ H 197. Note <span lang="grc">ἄλλος ἄλλο</span> (<span lang="grc">ἄλλοθεν, ἄλλοτε</span>, etc.), <span lang="grc">αὐτὸς αὑτοῦ</span>.

**e.** **Construction <span lang="grc">ἀπὸ κοινοῦ</span>**.—In poetry an attributive genitive or an object, common to two coördinate words, is often placed with the second only, as <span lang="grc">φράζων ἅλωσιν Ἰ̄λίου τ᾽ ἀνάστασιν</span> _telling of the capture and overthrow of Ilium_ <cite data-ref="Aesch. Ag. 587">A. Ag. 587</cite>.

##### <a id="s3029"></a> §3029

**Hypophora** (<span lang="grc">ὑποφορἁ̄</span> _putting under_) is the statement of an objection (together with its refutation) which a speaker supposes to be made by an opponent or makes himself. Both objection and reply often take the form of questions ([2654](32-interrogative-sentences-questions.md#s2654), [2785](36-particles/02-alphalambdalambdaalpha.md#s2785), [2819](36-particles/07-gammaalpharho.md#s2819)). Cp. “But I hear it continually rung in my ears . . . ‘what will become of the preamble, if you repeal this tax?’”: Burke.

<span lang="grc">τί οὖν, ἄν τις εἴποι, ταῦτα λέγεις ἡμῖν νῦν; ἵνα γνῶτ᾽ κτλ</span>. _why then, some one will say, do you tell us this now? In order that you may know_, etc. <cite data-ref="Dem. 1.14">D. 1.14</cite>.

##### <a id="s3030"></a> §3030

**Hysteron Proteron** (<span lang="grc">ὕστερον πρότερον</span> _later earlier_) is an arrangement reversing the natural order of time in which events occur. It is used when an event, later in time, is regarded as more important than one earlier in time. <!-- page 680 -->

<span lang="grc">τράφεν ἠδὲ γένοντο</span> _were bred and born_ A 251 (so <span lang="grc">τροφὴ καὶ γένεσις</span> <cite data-ref="Xen. Mem. 3.5.10">X. M. 3.5.10</cite>; cp. “for I was bred and born”: Shakespeare), <span lang="grc">εἵματά τ᾽ ἀμφιέσᾱσα θυώδεα καὶ λούσᾱσα</span> _having put on fragrant robes and washed_ <cite data-ref="Hom. Od. 5.264"><span lang="grc">ε</span> 264</cite>.

##### <a id="s3031"></a> §3031

**Isocōlon** (<span lang="grc">ἰσόκωλον</span> _having equal members_) is the use of two or more sequent cola (clauses) containing an equal number of syllables.

<span lang="grc">τοῦ μὲν ἐπίπονον καὶ φιλοκίνδῡνον τὸν βίον κατέστησεν, τῆς δὲ περίβλεπτον καὶ περιμάχητον τὴν φύσιν ἐποίησεν</span> _the life of the one he rendered full of toil and peril_, _the beauty of the other he made the object of universal admiration and of universal contention_ <cite data-ref="Isoc. 10.16">I. 10.16</cite>.

##### <a id="s3032"></a> §3032

**Litotes** (<span lang="grc">λῑτότης</span> _plainness_, _simplicity_) is understatement so as to intensify, affirmation expressed by the negative of the contrary. Cp. [2694](35-negative-sentences.md#s2694). _Meiōsis_ (<span lang="grc">μείωσις</span> _lessening_) is ordinarily the same as _litotes_. Thus: “One of the few immortal names That were not born to die”: Halleck.

##### <a id="s3033"></a> §3033

**Metonymy** (<span lang="grc">μετωνυμίᾱ</span> _change of name_) is the substitution of one word for another to which it stands in some close relation. Thus: “We wish that infancy may learn the purpose of its creation from maternal lips”: Webster.

<span lang="grc">μῖσος</span> _loathed object_, <span lang="grc">ὦ κάθαρμα</span> _you scum!_ <span lang="grc">συμμαχίᾱ</span> _allies_, <span lang="grc">ἐν Βοιωτοῖς</span> _in Boeotia_, <span lang="grc">θέᾱτρον</span> _spectators_, <span lang="grc">μάχη</span> _battlefield_, <span lang="grc">ἵππος</span> _cavalry_, <span lang="grc">ἰχθύες</span> _fish-market_.

##### <a id="s3034"></a> §3034

**Onomatopoeia** (<span lang="grc">ὀνοματοποιίᾱ</span> _making of a name_ or _word_) is the formation of names to express natural sounds.

<span lang="grc">βληχῶμαι</span> _bleat_, <span lang="grc">βομβῶ</span> _buzz_, <span lang="grc">βρῡχῶμαι</span> _roar_, <span lang="grc">κοάξ</span> _quack_, <span lang="grc">κακκαβίζω</span> _cackle_, <span lang="grc">κόκκυξ</span> _cuckoo_, <span lang="grc">κράζω</span> _croak_, <span lang="grc">τῑτίζω</span> _cheep_, <span lang="grc">πιππίζω</span> _chirp_. Sometimes the sound of a whole verse imitates an action; as <span lang="grc">αὖτις ἔπειτα πέδονδε κυλίνδετο λᾶας ἀναιδής</span> _down again to the plain rolled the shameless stone_ <cite data-ref="Hom. Od. 11.598"><span lang="grc">λ</span> 598</cite> (of the stone of Sisyphus).

##### <a id="s3035"></a> §3035

**Oxymōron** (<span lang="grc">ὀξύμωρον</span> _pointedly_ or _cleverly foolish_) is the juxtaposition of words apparently contradictory of each other.

<span lang="grc">νόμος ἄνομος</span> _a law that is no law_ <cite data-ref="Aesch. Ag. 1142">A. Ag. 1142</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἄχαρις χάρις</span> _a graceless grace_ <cite data-ref="Aesch. PB 545">A. Pr. 545</cite>, <span lang="grc">πίστις ἀπιστοτάτη</span> _most faithless faith_ <cite data-ref="Andoc. 1.67">And. 1.67</cite>, <span lang="grc">αὐτοὶ φεύγοντας φεύγουσι</span> _they themselves are flying from those who fly_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 7.70">T. 7.70</cite>.

##### <a id="s3036"></a> §3036

**Paraleipsis** (<span lang="grc">παράλειψις</span> _passing over_) is pretended omission for rhetorical effect.

<span lang="grc">τὰ̄ς δ᾽ ἐπ᾽ Ἰλλυρίους καὶ Παίονας αὐτοῦ καὶ πρὸς Ἀρύββᾱν καὶ ὅποι τις ἂν εἴποι παραλείπω στρατείᾱς</span> _I omit his expeditions to Illyria and Paeonia and against Arybbas and many others that one might mention_ (lit. _whithersoever one might speak of_) <cite data-ref="Dem. 1.13">D. 1.13</cite>.

##### <a id="s3037"></a> §3037

**Parechēsis** (<span lang="grc">παρήχησις</span> _likeness of sound_) is the repetition of the same sound in words in close or immediate succession. _Alliteration_ is initial rhyme.

<span lang="grc">ἄγαμος, ἄτεκνος, ἄπολις, ἄφιλος</span> <cite data-ref="Eur. IT 220">E. I. T. 220</cite> (cp. “unwept, unhonoured, and unsung”), <span lang="grc">πόνος πόνῳ πόνον φέρει</span> _toil upon toil brings_ only _toil_ <cite data-ref="Soph. Aj. 866">S. Aj. 866</cite>, <span lang="grc">τυφλὸς</span> <!-- page 681 --> <span lang="grc">τά τ᾽ ὦτα τόν τε νοῦν τά τ᾽ ὄμματ᾽ εἶ</span> _blind art thou in thy ears, thy reason, and thy eyes_ <cite data-ref="Soph. OT 371">S. O. T. 371</cite>, <span lang="grc">οἱ οὐδὲ . . . δὶς ἀποθανόντες δίκην δοῦναι δύναιντ᾽ ἄν</span> _who would not be able to give satisfaction even by dying twice_ <cite data-ref="Lys. 12.37">L. 12.37</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἔσωσά σ᾽· ὡς ἴσᾱσιν Ἑλλήνων ὅσοι κτλ</span>. _I saved thee; as all of the Greeks know who_, etc. <cite data-ref="Eur. Med. 476">E. Med. 476</cite>, <span lang="grc">θανάτου θᾶττον θεῖ</span> wickedness ‘_fleeth faster than fate_’ <cite data-ref="Plat. Apol. 39a">P. A. 39a</cite>.

##### <a id="s3038"></a> §3038

**Parisōsis** (<span lang="grc">παρίσωσις</span> _almost equal_) is approximate equality of clauses as measured by syllables. _Parisōsis_ is sometimes regarded as synonymous with _isocōlon_.

##### <a id="s3039"></a> §3039

**Paromoiōsis** (<span lang="grc">παρομοίωσις</span> _assimilation_) is parallelism of sound between the words of two clauses either approximately or exactly equal in size. This similarity in sound may appear at the beginning, at the end (_homoioteleuton_), in the interior, or it may pervade the whole.

<span lang="grc">μαχομένους μὲν κρείττους εἶναι τῶν πολεμίων, ψηφιζομένους δὲ ἥττους τῶν ἐχθρῶν</span> _by fighting to be superior to our public enemies, and by voting to be weaker than our private enemies_ <cite data-ref="Lys. 12.79">L. 12.79</cite>.

##### <a id="s3040"></a> §3040

**Paronomasia** (<span lang="grc">παρονομασίᾱ</span>) is play upon words.

<span lang="grc">οὐ γὰρ τὸν τρόπον ἀλλὰ τὸν τόπον μετήλλαξεν</span> _for he changed not his disposition but his position_ <cite data-ref="Aeschin. 3.78">Aes. 3.78</cite>. Often in etymological word-play; as <span lang="grc">Πρόθοος θοός</span> B 758, <span lang="grc">Μέλητος . . . ἐμέλησεν</span> <cite data-ref="Plat. Apol. 26a">P. A. 26a</cite>, <span lang="grc">Παυσανίου παυσαμένου</span> <cite data-ref="Plat. Sym. 185c">P. S. 185c</cite>, <span lang="grc">εἰς . . . τόπον . . . ἀειδῆ, εἰς Αἵδου</span> _to an invisible place, to Hades_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Phaedo 80d">P. Ph. 80d</cite>. Cp. “Old Gaunt indeed, and gaunt in being old”: Shakespeare. Sometimes this figure deals with the same word taken in different senses (_homonyms_): <span lang="grc">ἅμα γὰρ ἡμεῖς τε τῆς ἀρχῆς ἀπεστερούμεθα καὶ τοῖς Ἕλλησιν ἀρχὴ τῶν κακῶν ἐγίγνετο</span> ‘_no sooner were we deprived of the first place than the first disaster came upon the Greeks_’ <cite data-ref="Isoc. 4.119">I. 4.119</cite>.

##### <a id="s3041"></a> §3041

**Periphrasis** (<span lang="grc">περίφρασις</span> _circumlocution_) is the use of more words than are necessary to express an idea.

<span lang="grc">θρέμματα Νείλου</span> _nurslings of the Nile = the Egyptians_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Laws 953e">P. L. 953e</cite>, <span lang="grc">οἰδίπου κάρᾱ</span> _Oedipus_ <cite data-ref="Soph. OT 40">S. O. T. 40</cite> (<span lang="grc">κάρᾱ</span> expresses reverence or affection). The substantive on which another substantive depends often stands for an adjective, as <span lang="grc">ἲ̄ς Τηλεμάχοιο</span> = _mighty Telemach_ (cp. [1014](12-peculiarities-in-the-use-of-gender.md#s1014)). For various other periphrases, see the Index.

##### <a id="s3042"></a> §3042

**Pleonasm** (<span lang="grc">πλεονασμός</span> _excess_), or redundancy, is the admission of a word or words which are not necessary to the complete logical expression of the thought. Such words, though logically superfluous, enrich the thought by adding greater definiteness and precision, picturesqueness, vigour and emphasis; and by expressing subtle shadings of feeling otherwise impossible. Cp. “All ye inhabitants of the world, and dwellers on the earth.”

**a.** Adverbs or adverbial expressions combined: of _time_, as <span lang="grc">πάλιν αὖ, αὖθις αὖ πάλιν, πάλιν μετὰ ταῦτα ὕστερον, ἔπειτα μετὰ ταῦτα, διὰ τέλους τὸν πάντα χρόνον</span>; of _manner_, as <span lang="grc">κατὰ ταὐτὰ ὡσαύτως, μάτην ἄλλως, εἰς δυνατὸν ὅτι μάλιστα</span>; of _infer-_ <!-- page 682 --> _ence_, as <span lang="grc">τοιγάρτοι διὰ ταῦτα, ἐκ τούτου . . . διὰ ταῦτα</span>; of _verification_, as <span lang="grc">ἀληθῶς τῷ ὄντι</span>; and various other expressions, as <span lang="grc">ἴσως τάχ᾽ ἄν, λόγῳ εἰπεῖν</span>.

**b.** Adverb and adjective combined (usually poetical): <span lang="grc">κεῖτο μέγας μεγαλωστί</span> _huge he lay with his huge length_ II 776.

**c.** Adjective and verb: <span lang="grc">ὡς δὲ μὴ μακροὺς τείνω λόγους</span> _but not to speak at length_ <cite data-ref="Eur. Hec. 1177">E. Hec. 1177</cite>.

**d.** Adjective and substantive in the dative: <span lang="grc">νῆσος μεγάθει μὲν οὐ μεγάλη</span> _an island not large in size_ <cite data-ref="Hdt. 5.31">Hdt. 5.31</cite>.

**e.** Verb with an abstract substantive in the dative or accusative ([1516](18-the-cases/06-dative.md#s1516), [1564](18-the-cases/07-accusative.md#s1564)): <span lang="grc">βασιλεὺς . . . φύσει πεφῡκέναι</span> _to be a true-born king_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 5.1.24">X. C. 5.1.24</cite>.

**f.** Compound verb or substantives with substantives: <span lang="grc">οἶκον καλῶς οἰκονομεῖν</span> _to build a house well_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Mem. 4.5.10">X. M. 4.5.10</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἡ τῶν νεογνῶν τέκνων παιδοτροφίᾱ</span> _the rearing of young children_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Ec. 7.21">X. O. 7.21</cite>. Here the force of the first member of the compound is quiescent.

**g.** Compound verb and adverb: <span lang="grc">προύγραψα πρῶτον</span> _I wrote first_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 1.23">T. 1.23</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἀπαγαγὼν δ᾽ ὑ̄μᾶς ἄπωθεν ἀπὸ τοῦ κλέμματος</span> _having diverted your attention away from the fraud_ <cite data-ref="Aeschin. 3.100">Aes. 3.100</cite>.

**h.** Verb and participle ([2147 b](22-the-participle.md#s2147)): <span lang="grc">τί δὴ λέγοντες διέβαλλον οἱ διαβάλλοντες</span>; _in what words then did my calumniators calumniate me?_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Apol. 19b">P. A. 19b</cite>.

**i.** Amplification by synonymous doublets (especially common in Demosthenes): <span lang="grc">ἀξιῶ καὶ δέομαι</span> _I beg and beseech_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 18.6">D. 18.6</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἐναργὲς καὶ σαφές</span> _visible and clear_ 14. 4.

**j.** Parallelism of positive and negative: <span lang="grc">ὡς ἔχω περὶ τούτων, λέξω πρὸς ὑ̄μᾶς καὶ οὐκ ἀποκρύψομαι</span> _I will tell you and I will not conceal my opinion on these matters_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 8.73">D. 8.73</cite>, <span lang="grc">οὐκ ἄκλητοι, παρακληθέντες δέ</span> _not unbidden but invited_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 6.87">T. 6.87</cite>.

**k.** A person and a characteristic or quality connected by <span lang="grc">καί</span> or <span lang="grc">τέ</span>; as <span lang="grc">καταδείσαντες τοῦτον καὶ τὸ τούτου θράσος</span> _fearing him and his audacity_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 21.20">D. 21.20</cite>.

**l.** A relative clause takes up a preceding expression: <span lang="grc">καὶ εὐχὴν δέ τινες αὐτοῦ ἐξέφερον ὡς εὔχοιτο κτλ</span>. _and some reported also a prayer he made_, etc. (lit. _how he prayed_) <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 1.9.11">X. A. 1.9.11</cite>.

**m.** ‘Polar’ expressions may be placed here. These are opposites placed in pairs so as to intensify such ideas as _all_, _no one, at all times, everywhere_, _everything possible_. Thus, <span lang="grc">καὶ ἐν θεοῖς καὶ ἐν ἀνθρώποις</span> _both among the gods and among men_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Gorg. 508a">P. G. 508a</cite>, <span lang="grc">οὐδὲν οὔτε μέγα οὔτε μῑκρόν</span> _nothing either great or small = absolutely nothing_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Apol. 19c">P. A. 19c</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἐν γῇ καὶ θαλάττῃ</span> _on land and sea_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 18.324">D. 18.324</cite>, <span lang="grc">οὔτε δοῦλος οὔτ᾽ ἐλεύθερος</span> _nor bond nor free_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 2.78">T. 2.78</cite>, <span lang="grc">ῥητὰ καὶ ἄρρητα</span> _fanda nefanda_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 18.122">D. 18.122</cite>. For other cases of pleonasm, see the Index.

##### <a id="s3043"></a> §3043

**Polysyndeton** (cp. _Asyndeton_) is the repetition of conjunctions in a series of coördinate words or phrases.

<span lang="grc">καὶ τοσούτων καὶ ἑτέρων κακῶν καὶ αἰσχρῶν καὶ πάλαι καὶ νεωστὶ καὶ μῑκρῶν καὶ μεγάλων αἰτίου γεγενημένου</span> _who has shown himself the guilty cause of so many other base and disgraceful acts, both long ago and lately, both small and great_ <cite data-ref="Lys. 12.78">L. 12.78</cite>. Cp. <cite data-ref="Dem. 4.36">D. 4.36</cite>.

##### <a id="s3044"></a> §3044

**Praegnans Constructio** is a form of brachylogy by which two expressions or clauses are condensed into one.

Here belong, apart from 1659 ff., such cases as <span lang="grc">εἰς τὸ βαλανεῖον βούλομαι</span> _I want_ <!-- page 683 --> to go _to the bath_ <cite data-ref="Aristoph. Frogs 1279">Ar. Ran. 1279</cite> (cp. “he will directly to the lords”: Milton, Samson Agon. [1250](17-pronouns.md#s1250)) and <span lang="grc">φανερὸς ἦν οἴκαδε παρασκευαζόμενος</span> _he was evidently preparing_ to go _home_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 7.7.57">X. A. 7.7.57</cite>. In <span lang="grc">παραγγέλλει ἐπὶ τὰ ὅπλα</span> _he ordered them to get under arms_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 1.5.13">X. A. 1.5.13</cite> the command was <span lang="grc">ἐπὶ τὰ ὅπλα</span> _to arms!_

##### <a id="s3045"></a> §3045

**Prolēpsis** (<span lang="grc">πρόληψις</span> _taking before_) in the case of objective predicate adjectives or nouns is the anticipation of the result of the action of a verb. Examples in 1579.

On the prolepsis of the subject of dependent clauses which is put into the main clause, see [2182](27-syntax-of-the-complex-sentence.md#s2182). So in “Consider the lilies of the field how they grow.” Prolepsis is also used to designate the anticipation of an opponent's arguments and objections. One variety is _prodiorthōsis_ or preparatory apology (<cite data-ref="Plat. Apol. 20e">P. A. 20e</cite>, <cite data-ref="Dem. 18.199">D. 18.199</cite>, [256](../part-2-inflection/04-substantives/05-third-declension.md#s256)).

##### <a id="s3046"></a> §3046

**Symploce** (<span lang="grc">συμπλοκή</span> _interweaving_) is the repetition, in one or more successive clauses, of the first and last words of the preceding clause.

<span lang="grc">ἐπὶ σαυτὸν καλεῖς, ἐπὶ τοὺς νόμους καλεῖς, ἐπὶ τὴν δημοκρατίᾱν καλεῖς</span> _it is against yourself that you are summoning him, it is against the laws that you are summoning him, it is against the democratic constitution that you are summoning him_ <cite data-ref="Aeschin. 3.202">Aes. 3.202</cite>.

##### <a id="s3047"></a> §3047

**Synecdoche** (<span lang="grc">συνεκδοχή</span> _understanding one thing with another_) is the use of the part for the whole, or the whole for the part. The name of an animal is often used for that which comes from, or is made from, the animal. Cp. “they sought his blood”; “Belinda smiled, and all the world was gay”: Pope.

<span lang="grc">δόρυ</span> _ship_ for _plank_, _beam_, <span lang="grc">ἀλώπηξ</span> _fox-skin_ for _fox_, <span lang="grc">χελώνη</span> _tortoise-shell_ for _tortoise_, <span lang="grc">πορφύρᾱ</span> _purple dye_ for _purple-fish_, <span lang="grc">ἐλέφᾱς</span> _ivory_ for _elephant_, <span lang="grc">μελίσσα</span> _honey_ for _bee_.

##### <a id="s3048"></a> §3048

**Zeugma** (<span lang="grc">ζεῦγμα</span> _junction_, _band_) is a form of brachylogy by which two connected substantives are used jointly with the same verb (or adjective) though this is strictly appropriate to only one of them. Such a verb expresses an idea that may be taken in a wider, as well as in a narrower, sense, and therefore suggests the verb suitable to the other substantive. Cp. “Nor Mars his sword, nor war's quick fire shall burn The living record of your memory.”

<span lang="grc">οὔτε φωνὴν οὔτε του μορφὴν βροτῶν ὄψει</span> _thou shalt know neither voice nor form of mortal man_ <cite data-ref="Aesch. PB 21">A. Pr. 21</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἀλλ᾽ ἢ πνοαῖσιν ἢ βαθυσκαφεῖ κόνει κρύψον νιν</span> _no_, _either_ give them _to the winds or in the deep-dug soil bury them_ <cite data-ref="Soph. El. 435">S. El. 435</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἔδουσί τε πἱ̄ονα μῆλα οἶνόν τ᾽ ἔξαιτον</span> _they eat fat sheep_ and drink _choice wine_ M 319.

**a.** Different from zeugma is _syllēpsis_ (<span lang="grc">σύλληψις</span> _taking together_), by which the same verb, though governing two different objects, is taken both in its literal and its metaphorical sense; but does not properly change its meaning. Thus, <span lang="grc">χρήματα τελοῦντες τούτοις . . . καὶ χάριτας</span> _paying money and rendering thanks to them_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Crito 48c">P. Cr. 48c</cite>.
