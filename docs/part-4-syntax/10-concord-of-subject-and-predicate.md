# CONCORD OF SUBJECT AND PREDICATE

##### <a id="s949"></a> §949

A finite verb agrees with its subject in number and person.

Thus, <span lang="grc">τοῦτο τὸ ψήφισμα ἐγένετο</span> _this bill was passed_ <cite data-ref="Lys. 13.56">L. 13.56</cite>, <span lang="grc">ὃ δέδοικ᾽ ἐγὼ μὴ πάθηθ᾽ ὑ̄μεῖς</span> _which I fear lest you may suffer_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 9.65">D. 9.65</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἢν δ᾽ ἀποψηφίσωνται οἱ ἄλλοι</span>, <!-- page 263 --> <span lang="grc">ἄπιμεν ἅπαντες τοὔμπαλιν</span> _but if the rest vote against_ (following), _we shall all return back again_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 1.4.15">X. A. 1.4.15</cite>, <span lang="grc">τὼ ξένω τώδε φίλω ἐστὸν ἐμώ</span> _these two strangers are friends of mine_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Gorg. 487a">P. G. 487a</cite>.

**a.** The verbal predicate, when a copulative verb ([917](02-syntax-of-the-simple-sentence.md#s917)), may be attracted to the number of a predicate noun, which often stands between subject and verb: <span lang="grc">τὸ χωρίον τοῦτο, ὅπερ πρότερον Ἐννέα ὁδοὶ ἐκαλοῦντο</span> _this place which was formerly called Nine Ways_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 4.102">T. 4.102</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἅπᾱν τὸ μέσον τῶν τειχῶν ἦσαν στάδιοι τρεῖς</span> _the entire space between the walls was three stades_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 1.4.4">X. A. 1.4.4</cite>. So with the participles of such copulative verbs: <span lang="grc">τὴν ἡδονὴν διώκετε ὡς ἀγαθὸν ὄν</span> (for <span lang="grc">οὖσαν</span>) _you chase after pleasure as if it were a good_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Prot. 354c">P. Pr. 354c</cite>.

## WITH ONE SUBJECT

### Subject in the Singular_, _Verb in the Plural

##### <a id="s950"></a> §950

With singular collective substantives ([996](11-peculiarities-in-the-use-of-number.md#s996)) denoting persons and with like words implying a plural, the verb may stand in the plural.

Thus, <span lang="grc">τὸ στρατόπεδον ἐν αἰτίᾳ ἔχοντες τὸν Ἆγιν ἀνεχώρουν</span> _the army returned holding Agis at fault_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 5.60">T. 5.60</cite>, <span lang="grc">τοιαῦτα ἀκούσᾱσα ἡ πόλις Ἀ̄γησίλᾱον εἵλοντο βασιλέᾱ</span> _the city, after hearing such arguments, chose Agesilaus king_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Hell. 3.3.4">X. H. 3.3.4</cite>. So with <span lang="grc">βουλή</span> _senate_, <span lang="grc">μέρος</span> _part_, <span lang="grc">πλῆθος</span> _multitude_, <span lang="grc">δῆμος</span> _people_, <span lang="grc">ὄχλος</span> _throng_.

##### <a id="s951"></a> §951

So with <span lang="grc">ἕκαστος· τῶν ἑαυτοῦ ἕκαστος καὶ παίδων καὶ χρημάτων ἄρχουσι</span> _every man is master of his own children and property_ X. R. <cite data-ref="Lys. 6.1">L. 6.1</cite>.

##### <a id="s952"></a> §952

If <span lang="grc">ἕκαστος, ἑκάτερος, ἄλλος</span> are added in apposition to a plural subject, the verb generally remains plural: <span lang="grc">ἐγώ τε καὶ σὺ μακρὸν λόγον ἑκάτερος ἀπετείναμεν</span> _both you and I have carried on a long controversy_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Prot. 361a">P. Pr. 361a</cite>. If the verb follows the apposition, it may be singular: <span lang="grc">οὗτοι μὲν ἄλλος ἄλλα λέγει</span> _these say_, _some one thing, some another_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 2.1.15">X. A. 2.1.15</cite>. Cp. [982](#s982).

##### <a id="s953"></a> §953

A subject in the singular, followed by a clause containing the preposition <span lang="grc">μετά</span> _with_, rarely takes a plural verb: <span lang="grc">Ἀλκιβιάδης μετὰ Μαντιθέου ἵππων εὐπορήσαντες ἀπέδρᾱσαν</span> _Alcibiades and Mantitheus escaped because they were well provided with horses_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Hell. 1.1.10">X. H. 1.1.10</cite>.

### Subject in the Dual, Verb in the Plural

##### <a id="s954"></a> §954

The first person dual agrees in form with the first person plural ([462](../part-2-inflection/09-verbs/08-endings-of-the-verb-personal-endings.md#s462)).

##### <a id="s955"></a> §955

A dual subject may take a plural verb: <span lang="grc">Ξενοφῶντι προσέτρεχον δύο νεᾱνίσκω</span> _two youths ran up to Xenophon_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 4.3.10">X. A. 4.3.10</cite>. In the orators the dual verb is almost always used.

##### <a id="s956"></a> §956

The dual and plural verb may alternate: <span lang="grc">αἵρεσιν εἱλέτην τε καὶ διεπρά̄ξαντο</span> _the_ two souls _have made their choice and put it into effect_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Phaedrus 256c">P. Phae. 256c</cite>.

##### <a id="s957"></a> §957

The neuter dual may be followed by the dual, the plural, or the singular verb (A 104, 200, M [466](../part-2-inflection/09-verbs/08-endings-of-the-verb-personal-endings.md#s466)).

### Subject in the Plural_, _Verb in the Singular

<!-- page 264 -->

##### <a id="s958"></a> §958

A neuter plural subject is regarded as a collective ([996](11-peculiarities-in-the-use-of-number.md#s996)), and has its verb in the singular: <span lang="grc">καλὰ ἦν τὰ σφάγια</span> _the sacrifices were propitious_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 4.3.19">X. A. 4.3.19</cite>.

N.—The neuter plural seems to have been originally in part identical in form with the feminine singular in <span lang="grc">ᾱ</span>, and to have had a collective meaning.

##### <a id="s959"></a> §959

A plural verb may be used when stress is laid on the fact that the neuter plural subject is composed of persons or of several parts: <span lang="grc">τὰ τέλη τῶν Λακεδαιμονίων αὐτὸν ἐξέπεμψαν</span> _the Lacedaemonian magistrates despatched him_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 4.88">T. 4.88</cite>, <span lang="grc">φανερὰ ἦσαν καὶ ἵππων καὶ ἀνθρώπων ἴχνη πολλά</span> _many traces both of horses and of men were plain_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 1.7.17">X. A. 1.7.17</cite>.

**a.** With the above exception Attic regularly uses the singular verb. Homer uses the singular three times as often as the plural, and the plural less frequently with neuter adjectives and pronouns than with substantives. In some cases (B [135](../part-1-letters-sounds-syllables-accent/05-movable-consonants.md#s135)) the metre decides the choice.

##### <a id="s960"></a> §960

Following the construction of <span lang="grc">δοκεῖ ταῦτα</span>, we find <span lang="grc">δόξαν ταῦτα</span> _when it had been thus decided_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 4.1.13">X. A. 4.1.13</cite>, and also <span lang="grc">δόξαντα ταῦτα</span> <cite data-ref="Xen. Hell. 3.2.19">X. H. 3.2.19</cite>. See [2078 a](22-the-participle.md#s2078).

##### <a id="s961"></a> §961

_Pindaric Construction_. A masculine or feminine plural subject occasionally is used with <span lang="grc">ἔστι, ἦν, γίγνεται</span>, as: <span lang="grc">ἔστι καὶ ἐν ταῖς ἄλλαις πόλεσιν ἄρχοντές τε καὶ δῆμος</span> _there are in the other cities too rulers and populace_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Rep. 462e">P. R. 462e</cite>. The verb usually precedes, and the subject is still undetermined; hence the plural is added as an afterthought. (Cp. Shakesp. “far behind his worth | Comes all the praises.”) In Greek poetry this construction is rarely used with other verbs. On <span lang="grc">ἔστιν οἵ</span>, see [2513](30-adjective-clauses-relative-clauses-2488-2573.md#s2513).

**a.** <span lang="grc">ἦν</span> was originally plural ([464 e](../part-2-inflection/09-verbs/08-endings-of-the-verb-personal-endings.md#s464). D), and seems to survive in that use.

### Subject in the Plural_, _Verb in the Dual

##### <a id="s962"></a> §962

A plural subject may take a dual verb when the subject is a pair or two pairs: <span lang="grc">αἱ ἵπποι δραμέτην</span> _the span of mares ran_ <span lang="grc"> **Ψ** </span> 392.

**a.** This is common when <span lang="grc">δύο, ἄμφω, ἀμφότεροι</span> are used with a plural subject: <span lang="grc">δύο ἄνδρες προσελθόντε Ἄ̄γιδι διελεγέσθην μὴ ποιεῖν μάχην</span> _two men coming to Agis urged him not to fight_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 5.59">T. 5.59</cite>. But even with these words the plural is preferred. The neuter plural with <span lang="grc">δύο</span> rarely takes the dual verb (<cite data-ref="Plat. Tim. 56e">P. Tim. 56e</cite>).

## WITH TWO OR MORE SUBJECTS

##### <a id="s963"></a> §963

(I) When the subjects are different individuals or things and stand in the _third_ person

##### <a id="s964"></a> §964

With two subjects in the singular, the verb may be dual or plural: <span lang="grc">Κριτίᾱς καὶ Ἀλκιβιάδης ἐδυνάσθην ἐκείνῳ χρωμένω συμμάχῳ τῶν ἐπιθῡμιῶν κρατεῖν</span> _Critias and Alcibiades were able to keep control of their appetites by the help_ <!-- page 265 --> _of his example_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Mem. 1.2.24">X. M. 1.2.24</cite>, <span lang="grc">Εὐρυμέδων καὶ Σοφοκλῆς ἀφικόμενοι ἐς Κέρκῡραν ἐστράτευσαν</span> _on their arrival in Corcyra Eurymedon and Sophocles proceeded to make an attack_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 4.46">T. 4.46</cite>.

##### <a id="s965"></a> §965

In Homer the verb may intervene between the subjects (_Alcmanic Construction_): <span lang="grc">εἰς Ἀχέροντα Πυριφλεγέθων τε ῥέουσιν Κώκῡτός τε</span> _Pyriphlegethon and Cocytus flow into Acheron_ <cite data-ref="Hom. Od. 10.513"><span lang="grc">κ</span> 513</cite>.

##### <a id="s966"></a> §966

The verb may agree with the nearest or most important of two or more subjects. The verb may be placed

**a.** Before both subjects: <span lang="grc">ἧκε μὲν ὁ Θερσαγόρᾱς καὶ ὁ Ἐξήκεστος εἰς Λέσβον καὶ ᾤκουν ἐκεῖ</span> _Thersagoras and Execestus came to Lesbos and settled there_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 23.143">D. 23.143</cite>.

**b.** After the first subject: <span lang="grc">ὅ τε Πολέμαρχος ἧκε καὶ Ἀδείμαντος καὶ Νικήρατος καὶ ἄλλοι τινές</span> _Polemarchus came and Adimantus and Niceratus and certain others_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Rep. 327b">P. R. 327b</cite>, <span lang="grc">Φαλῖνος ᾤχετο καὶ οἱ σὺν αὐτῷ</span> _Phalinus and his companions departed_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 2.2.1">X. A. 2.2.1</cite>.

**c.** After both subjects: <span lang="grc">τὸ βουλευτήριον καὶ ὁ δῆμος παρορᾶται</span> _the senate and the people are disregarded_ <cite data-ref="Aeschin. 3.250">Aes. 3.250</cite>. (Cp. Shakesp. “my mistress and her sister stays.”)

##### <a id="s967"></a> §967

(II) With several subjects referring to different persons the verb is in the plural; in the _first_ person, if one of the subjects is first person; in the _second_ person, if the subjects are second and third person: <span lang="grc">ὑ̄μεῖς δὲ καὶ ἐγὼ τάδε λέγομεν</span> _but you and I say this_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Laws 661b">P. L. 661b</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἡμεῖς καὶ οἵδε οὐκ ἄλλην ἄν τινα δυναίμεθα ᾠδὴν ᾁδειν</span> _we and these men could not sing any other song_ 666 d, <span lang="grc">οὐ σὺ μόνος οὐδὲ οἱ σοὶ φίλοι πρῶτοι ταύτην δόξαν ἔσχετε</span> _not you alone nor your friends are the first who have held this opinion_ 888 b.

##### <a id="s968"></a> §968

But the verb may be singular if it refers to the nearer or more important or more emphatic subject: <span lang="grc">πάρειμι καὶ ἐγὼ καὶ οὗτος Φρῡνίσκος καὶ Πολυκράτης</span>. _I am present and so are Phryniscus here and Polycrates_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 7.2.29">X. A. 7.2.29</cite>.

##### <a id="s969"></a> §969

The verb may agree in person with the nearer or more important subject: <span lang="grc">σύ τε γὰρ Ἕλλην εἶ καὶ ἡμεῖς</span> _for you are a Greek and so are we_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 2.1.16">X. A. 2.1.16</cite>.

##### <a id="s970"></a> §970

With subjects connected by the disjunctives <span lang="grc">ἤ</span> _or_, <span lang="grc">ἤ—ἤ</span> _either—or_, <span lang="grc">οὔτε—οὔτε</span> _neither—nor_, the verb agrees in number with the nearer subject when each subject is taken by itself: <span lang="grc">οἴτε σὺ οὔτ᾽ ἂν ἄλλος οὐδεὶς δύναιτ᾽ ἀντειπεῖν</span> _neither you nor anybody else could reply_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Mem. 4.4.7">X. M. 4.4.7</cite>.

##### <a id="s971"></a> §971

When the subjects are taken together, the plural occurs: <span lang="grc">ἃ Δημοφῶν ἢ Θηριππίδης ἔχουσι τῶν ἐμῶν</span> _what Demophon or Therippides have of my property_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 27.12">D. 27.12</cite>. This is unusual.

##### <a id="s972"></a> §972

When <span lang="grc">ἤ</span> _than_ unites two subjects, if the verb follows <span lang="grc">ἤ</span>, it agrees with the second subject: <span lang="grc">τύχη ἀεὶ βέλτῑον ἣ ἡμεῖς ἡμῶν αὐτῶν ἐπιμελούμεθα</span> _fortune always takes better care of us than we do of ourselves_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 4.12">D. 4.12</cite>.

## CONCORD OF PREDICATE SUBSTANTIVES

##### <a id="s973"></a> §973

A predicate substantive agrees with its subject in case: <span lang="grc">Μιλτιάδης ἦν στρατηγός</span> _Miltiades was a general_. <!-- page 266 -->

##### <a id="s974"></a> §974

A predicate substantive may agree in gender and number with its subject; but this is often impossible: <span lang="grc">τύχη τὰ θνητῶν πρά̄γματα</span> _the affairs of mortals are chance_ Trag. frag. p. 782, <span lang="grc">πάντ᾽ ἦν Ἀλέξανδρος</span> _Alexander was everything_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 23.120">D. 23.120</cite>.

##### <a id="s975"></a> §975

A predicate substantive or adjective agrees with the subject of the governing verb when the subject of the infinitive is omitted because it is the same as that of the governing verb ([937](07-omission-of-the-subject.md#s937)): <span lang="grc">οὐχ ὁμολογήσω ἄκλητος ἥκειν</span> _I shall not admit that I have come uninvited_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Sym. 174d">P. S. 174d</cite>, <span lang="grc">εἴπερ ἀξιοῦμεν ἐλεύθεροι εἶναι</span> _if indeed we claim to be free_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 8.1.4">X. C. 8.1.4</cite>.

On the agreement of demonstrative and relative pronouns with a predicate substantive, see [1239](17-pronouns.md#s1239), [2502](30-adjective-clauses-relative-clauses-2488-2573.md#s2502) e.

## APPOSITION

##### <a id="s976"></a> §976

**Concord**.—An appositive ([916](02-syntax-of-the-simple-sentence.md#s916)) agrees in case with the word it describes: <span lang="grc">κόλακι, δεινῷ θηρίῳ καὶ μεγίστῃ βλάβῃ</span> _to a flatterer, a terrible beast and a very great source of injury_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Phaedrus 240b">P. Phae. 240b</cite>. An appositive also agrees in case with the pronoun contained in a verb: <span lang="grc">Ταλθύβιος, ἥκω, Δανα̈́δων ὑπηρέτης</span> _I_, _Talthybius_, _have come, the servant of the Danaids_ <cite data-ref="Eur. Hec. 503">E. Hec. 503</cite>. Cp. [942](08-case-of-the-subject-the-nominative.md#s942).

##### <a id="s977"></a> §977

An appositive to a possessive pronoun stands in the genitive, in agreement with the personal pronoun implied in the possessive: <span lang="grc">τὸν ἐμὸν</span> ( = <span lang="grc">ἐμοῦ</span>) <span lang="grc">τοῦ ταλαιπώρου βίον</span> _the life of me, wretched one_ <cite data-ref="Aristoph. Pl. 33">Ar. Plut. 33</cite>, <span lang="grc">τὰ ὑ̄μέτερ᾽</span> ( = <span lang="grc">ὑ̄μῶν</span>) <span lang="grc">αὐτῶν κομιεῖσθε</span> _you will regain your own_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 4.7">D. 4.7</cite>. Cp. [1200](17-pronouns.md#s1200). 2. b, 1202. 2. b.

##### <a id="s978"></a> §978

An appositive in the genitive may follow an adjective equivalent to a genitive: <span lang="grc">Ἀθηναῖος</span> ( = <span lang="grc">Ἀθηνῶν</span>) <span lang="grc">ὤν, πόλεως τῆς μεγίστης</span> _being an Athenian_, _a citizen of the greatest city_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Apol. 29d">P. A. 29d</cite>.

##### <a id="s979"></a> §979

Agreement in _number_ between the appositive and its noun is unnecessary and often impossible: <span lang="grc">Θῆβαι, πόλις ἀστυγείτων</span> _Thebes_, _a neighbouring city_ <cite data-ref="Aeschin. 3.133">Aes. 3.133</cite>. So with <span lang="grc">δῶρα</span> in poetry: <span lang="grc">γάμος, χρῡσῆς Ἀφροδί̄της δῶρα</span>, _marriage_, _gift of golden Aphrodite_ Theognis 1293.

##### <a id="s980"></a> §980

An appositive to two substantives is dual or plural: <span lang="grc">θάρρος καὶ φόβος, ἄφρονε ξυμβούλω</span> _daring and fear, two unintelligent counsellors_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Tim. 69d">P. Tim. 69d</cite>, <span lang="grc">ὕπνος πόνος τε, κύ̄ριοι συνωμόται</span> _sleep and toil, supreme conspirators_ <cite data-ref="Aesch. Eum. 127">A. Eum. 127</cite>.

##### <a id="s981"></a> §981

**Partitive Apposition** (<span lang="grc">σχῆμα καθ᾽ ὅλον καὶ μέρος</span>, _construction of the whole and part_). The parts are represented by the appositives, which stand in the same case as the whole, which is placed first to show the subject or object of the sentence: <span lang="grc">τὼ ὁδώ, ἡ μὲν εἰς μακάρων νήσους, ἡ δ᾽ εἰς τάρταρον</span> _two roads, the one to the Islands of the Blest_, _the other to Tartarus_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Gorg. 524a">P. G. 524a</cite> (_distributive_ apposition). The appositives are generally in the nominative (<span lang="grc">ὁ μέν, ἡ δέ; οἱ μέν, οἱ δέ</span>), rarely in the accusative. <!-- page 267 -->

**a.** The whole may stand in the singular: <span lang="grc">λέγεται ψῡχὴ ἡ μὲν νοῦν ἔχειν, ἡ δὲ ἄνοιαν</span>; _with regard to the soul, is one said to have intelligence, the other folly?_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Phaedo 93b">P. Ph. 93b</cite>.

##### <a id="s982"></a> §982

To the word denoting the whole the appositive may be a collective singular (_adjunctive_ apposition): <span lang="grc">οὗτοι μὲν ἄλλος ἄλλα λέγει</span> _these say, some one thing, some another_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 2.1.15">X. A. 2.1.15</cite> (cp. <span lang="grc">ἠρώτων δὲ ἄλλος ἄλλο</span> <cite data-ref="Plat. Charm. 153c">P. Charm. 153c</cite>), <span lang="grc">οἱ στρατηγοὶ βραχέως ἕκαστος ἀπελογήσατο</span> _each of the generals defended himself briefly_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Hell. 1.7.5">X. H. 1.7.5</cite>. Cp. [952](#s952).

##### <a id="s983"></a> §983

The apposition may be limited to one or more parts: <span lang="grc">Πελοποννήσιοι καὶ οἱ ξύμμαχοι τὰ δύο μέρη</span> _two-thirds of the Peloponnesians and the allies_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 2.47">T. 2.47</cite>. Often with participles: (<span lang="grc">οἱ Ἀθηναῖοι</span>) <span lang="grc">ἀνεμνήσθησαν καὶ τοῦδε τοῦ ἔπους, φάσκοντες οἱ πρεσβύτεροι πάλαι ᾁδεσθαι</span> _the Athenians bethought themselves of this verse too_, _the old men saying that it had been uttered long before_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 2.54">T. 2.54</cite>.

##### <a id="s984"></a> §984

In partitive apposition emphasis is laid on the _whole_, which is stated at once as the subject or object of the sentence. In the genitive of the divided whole ([1306](18-the-cases/03-genitive.md#s1306)) emphasis is laid on the _parts;_ thus, <span lang="grc">τῶν πόλεων αἱ μὲν τυραννοῦνται, αἱ δὲ δημοκρατοῦνται, αἱ δὲ ἀριστοκρατοῦνται</span> _of states some are despotic, others democratic_, _others aristocratic_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Rep. 338d">P. R. 338d</cite>.

##### <a id="s985"></a> §985

**Construction of the Whole and Part in Poetry**.—In Homer and later poets a verb may take two objects, one denoting the person, the other the part especially affected by the action: <span lang="grc">τὸν δ᾽ ἄορι πλῆξ᾽ αὐχένα</span> _him he smote in the neck with his sword_ <cite data-ref="Hom. Il. 11.240"><span lang="grc">Λ</span> 240</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἥ σε πόδας νίψει</span> _she will wash thy feet_ <cite data-ref="Hom. Od. 19.356"><span lang="grc">τ</span> 356</cite>. But the accusative of the part, often explained as an appositive, was an external object ([1554 b](18-the-cases/07-accusative.md#s1554)) that became an accusative of respect ([1601 a](18-the-cases/07-accusative.md#s1601)). In <span lang="grc">Ἀχαιοῖσιν δὲ μέγα σθένος ἔμβαλ᾽ ἑκάστῳ καρδίῃ</span> _and she set mighty strength in the heart of each of the Achaeans_ <cite data-ref="Hom. Il. 11.11"><span lang="grc">Λ</span> 11</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἑκάστῳ</span> is a partitive appositive, <span lang="grc">καρδίῃ</span> is local dative and grammatically independent of <span lang="grc">Ἀχαιοῖσιν</span>. The construction is very rare in prose: <span lang="grc">τοῖς ϝἱέσιν αὐτῶν ἀρετὴ παραγενομένη ταῖς ψῡχαῖς</span> _if virtue is imparted in the souls of their sons_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Lach. 190b">P. Lach. 190b</cite>.

##### <a id="s986"></a> §986

**Attributive Apposition**.—A substantive may be used as an attributive to another substantive. This is common with substantives denoting _occupation_, _condition_ , or _age_ (usually with <span lang="grc">ἀνήρ, ἄνθρωπος, γυνή</span>): <span lang="grc">ἀνὴρ ῥήτωρ</span> _a public speaker_, <span lang="grc">ἀνὴρ τύραννος</span> _a despot_, <span lang="grc">πρεσβῦται ἄνθρωποι</span> _old men_, <span lang="grc">γραῦς γυνή</span> _an old woman_. So also <span lang="grc">πελτασταὶ Θρᾷκες</span> _Thracian targeteers_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 1.2.9">X. A. 1.2.9</cite>, <span lang="grc">ὄλεθρος Μακεδών</span> _a scoundrel of a Macedonian_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 9.31">D. 9.31</cite>, <span lang="grc">Ἕλλην</span> (for <span lang="grc">Ἑλληνικός</span>), as <span lang="grc">οἱ Ἕλληνες πελτασταί</span> _the Greek targeteers_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 6.5.26">X. A. 6.5.26</cite>.

**a.** In standard prose <span lang="grc">Ἕλλην</span> is used as an adjective only of persons (in poetry also of things).

**b.** The addition of <span lang="grc">ἀνήρ</span> often implies respect: <span lang="grc">ἄνδρες στρατιῶται</span> _fellow soldiers_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 1.3.3">X. A. 1.3.3</cite>, <span lang="grc">ὦ ἄνδρες δικασταί</span> _jurymen_, _gentlemen of the jury_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 27.1">D. 27.1</cite>. (Cp. _foemen_.) The addition of <span lang="grc">ἄνθρωπος</span> often implies contempt: <span lang="grc">ἄνθρωπος γόης</span> _a juggling fellow_ <cite data-ref="Aeschin. 2.153">Aes. 2.153</cite>.

**c.** Many of the substantives thus qualified by an attributive substantive were originally participles, as <span lang="grc">γέρων ἀνήρ</span> _an old man_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Lysis 223b">P. Lys. 223b</cite>. <!-- page 268 -->

##### <a id="s987"></a> §987

**Descriptive Apposition**.—Here the appositive describes something definite that has just been mentioned: <span lang="grc">ἡ ἡμετέρᾱ πόλις, ἡ κοινὴ καταφυγὴ τῶν Ἑλλήνων</span> _our city, the common refuge of the Greeks_ <cite data-ref="Aeschin. 3.134">Aes. 3.134</cite>.

##### <a id="s988"></a> §988

**Explanatory Apposition**.—Here the appositive explains a general or vague statement: <span lang="grc">τούτου τῑμῶμαι, ἐν πρυτανείῳ σῑτήσεως</span> _I propose this as the penalty, maintenance in the Prytaneum_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Apol. 37a">P. A. 37a</cite>, <span lang="grc">μεγίστου κακοῦ ἀπαλλαγή, πονηρίᾱς</span> _deliverance from the greatest of evils_, _vice_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Gorg. 478d">P. G. 478d</cite>. So in geographical statements: <span lang="grc">Κύπρον ἵκᾱνε . . . ἐς Πάφον</span> _she came to Cyprus_, _to Paphos_ <cite data-ref="Hom. Il. 8.362"><span lang="grc">Θ</span> 362</cite>; cp. <span lang="grc">ἐς Δωριᾶς, Βοιόν</span> _to the territory of the Dorians in which Boeum lies_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 1.107">T. 1.107</cite>.

##### <a id="s989"></a> §989

In Homer the substantival article at the beginning of a sentence may be followed by an appositive noun at or near the end: <span lang="grc">ἡ δ᾽ ἀέκουσ᾽ ἅμα τοῖσι γυνὴ κίεν</span> _but she, the woman, went unwillingly with them_ A 348.

##### <a id="s990"></a> §990

<span lang="grc">τοῦτο, αὐτὸ τοῦτο, αὐτό, ἐκεῖνο</span> often introduce emphatically a following substantive (or an equivalent, [908](02-syntax-of-the-simple-sentence.md#s908)): <span lang="grc">ἐκεῖνο κερδαίνειν ἡγεῖται, τὴν ἡδονήν</span> _this_ (namely) _pleasure it regards as gain_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Rep. 606b">P. R. 606b</cite>. Cp. [1248](17-pronouns.md#s1248).

##### <a id="s991"></a> §991

**Apposition to a Sentence**.—A noun in the nominative or accusative may stand in apposition to the action expressed by a whole sentence or by some part of it.

**a.** The appositive is nominative when a nominative precedes: <span lang="grc">ἐμέθυον· ἱκανὴ πρόφασις</span> _I was tipsy, a sufficient excuse_ Philemon (Com. frag. 2. [531](../part-2-inflection/10-verb-stem-and-present-stem/02-1-present-system.md#s531)).

**b.** The appositive is accusative, and states a reason, result, intention, effect, or the like: <span lang="grc">ῥί̄ψει ἀπὸ πύργου, λυγρὸν ὄλεθρον</span> _will hurl thee from the battlement_, _a grievous death_ <cite data-ref="Hom. Il. 24.735"><span lang="grc">Ω</span> 735</cite>, <span lang="grc">Ἑλένην κτάνωμεν, Μενέλεῳ λύ̄πην πικρά̄ν</span> _let us slay Helen_ and thus cause _a sore grief to Menelaus_ <cite data-ref="Eur. Orest. 1105">E. Or. 1105</cite>, <span lang="grc">εὐδαιμονοίης, μισθὸν ἡδίστων λόγων</span> _blest be thou—a return for thy most welcome tidings_ <cite data-ref="Eur. El. 231">E. El. 231</cite>.

N.—The appositive accusative is often cognate ([1563 f](18-the-cases/07-accusative.md#s1563).): <span lang="grc">ὁρᾷς Εὐρυσθέᾱ, ἄελπτον ὄψιν</span> _thou beholdest Eurystheus_, _an unexpected sight_ E. Heracl. 930.

##### <a id="s992"></a> §992

An effect or result may be denoted by an appositive in other cases: <span lang="grc">ἐπῳδῶν προσδεῖσθαί μοι δοκεῖ μύ̄θων ἔτι τινῶν</span> _we need, it seems, some further words to act as a spell_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Laws 903b">P. L. 903b</cite>.

##### <a id="s993"></a> §993

From the construction in 991 b arose many adverbial accusatives ([1606 f](18-the-cases/07-accusative.md#s1606)f.) such as <span lang="grc">χάριν</span> _on account of_, <span lang="grc">πρόφασιν</span> _in pretence_, <span lang="grc">δωρεά̄ν</span> _gratis;_ as <span lang="grc">ὅς τις δὲ Τρώων ἐπὶ νηυσὶ φέροιτο . . . χάριν Ἕκτορος</span> _whoever of the Trojans rushed at the ships as a favour to Hector_ (_for Hector's sake_) O 744.

##### <a id="s994"></a> §994

Many neuter words are used in apposition to a sentence or clause, which they usually precede. Such are <span lang="grc">ἀμφότερον, ἀμφότερα</span> _both_, <span lang="grc">τὸ δεινότατον</span> _the most dreadful thing_, <span lang="grc">δυοῖν θά̄τερον</span> or <span lang="grc">θά̄τερα</span> _one or the other_, <span lang="grc">τὸ ἐναντίον</span> _the contrary_, <span lang="grc">τὸ κεφάλαιον</span> _the chief point_, <span lang="grc">τὸ λεγόμενον</span> _as the saying is_, <span lang="grc">οὐδέτερον</span> _neither thing_, <span lang="grc">σημεῖον δέ</span> _sign_, <span lang="grc">τεκμήριον δέ</span> _evidence_, <span lang="grc">τὸ τελευταῖον</span> _the last thing_, <span lang="grc">τὸ τῆς παροιμίᾱς</span> _as the proverb_ <!-- page 269 --> _runs_, <span lang="grc">αὐτὸ τοῦτο</span> _this very thing_, <span lang="grc">ταὐτὸ τοῦτο</span> _this same thing_. Thus, <span lang="grc">τους ἀμφότερα ταῦτα, καὶ εὔνους τῇ πόλει καὶ πλουσίους</span> _those who are both loyal to the State and rich_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 18.171">D. 18.171</cite>, <span lang="grc">εἶπεν ὅτι δεῖ δυοῖν θά̄τερον, ἢ κείνους ἐν Ολύνθῳ μὴ οἰκεῖν ἢ αὑτὸν ἐν Μακεδονίᾳ</span> _he said that one of two things was necessary—either that they should not live at Olynthus or he himself in Macedon_ 9. 11, <span lang="grc">τὸ δὲ μέγιστον, πόλεμον ἀντ᾽ εἰρήνης ἔχοντες</span> _and what is worst of all, having war instead of peace_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 2.65">T. 2.65</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἀλλ᾽ ἦ, τὸ λεγόμενον, κατόπιν ἑορτῆς ἥκομεν</span>; _but have we come ‘after a feast’ as the maying is?_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Gorg. 447a">P. G. 447a</cite>, <span lang="grc">τοῦτο αὐτὸ τὸ τοῦ Ὁμήρον</span> _in these very words of Homer_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Apol. 34d">P. A. 34d</cite>.

##### <a id="s995"></a> §995

Very common are introductory relative clauses forming a nominative predicate of the sentence that follows: <span lang="grc">ὃ δὲ πάντων δεινότατον</span> _but what is most terrible of all_ <cite data-ref="Lys. 30.29">L. 30.29</cite>. <span lang="grc">ἐστί</span> is regularly omitted ([944](09-the-predicate.md#s944)). Such relative clauses are followed by an independent sentence, a clause with <span lang="grc">ὅτι</span>, by <span lang="grc">ὅτε γάρ, ὅταν, ὅταν γάρ, &lt;\*&gt;</span>. Similarly <span lang="grc">τὸ δ᾽ ἔσχατον πάντων, ὅτι</span> _but what is worst of all_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Phaedo 66d">P. Ph. 66d</cite>, etc.
