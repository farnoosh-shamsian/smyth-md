# THE VERB: VOICES: IMPERATIVE

##### <a id="s1835"></a> §1835

The imperative is used in commands and prohibitions (negative <span lang="grc">μή</span>). All its tenses refer to the future.

**a.** Under commands are included requests, entreaties, summons, prescriptions, exhortations, etc.

**b.** For the tenses of the imperative, see [1840](#s1840); for the infinitive used as an imperative, see [2013](../21-verbal-nouns.md#s2013).

## **POSITIVE** (**COMMANDS**)

##### <a id="s1836"></a> §1836

In exhortations <span lang="grc">ἄγε, φέρε, ἴθι</span> (usually with <span lang="grc">δή</span>, sometimes with <span lang="grc">νύν</span>), often precede the imperative: <span lang="grc">ἄγε δὴ ἀκούσατε</span> _come listen_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Apol. 14">X. Ap. 14</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἄγετε δειπνήσατε</span> _go now, take your supper_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Hell. 5.1.18">X. H. 5.1.18</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἀλλ᾽ ἴθι εἰπέ</span> _but come, say_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Gorg. 489e">P. G. 489e</cite>.

##### <a id="s1837"></a> §1837

<span lang="grc">πᾶς</span> is sometimes used with the second person in poetry: <span lang="grc">ἄκουε πᾶς</span> _hear_, _every one_ <cite data-ref="Aristoph. Thes. 372">Ar. Thesm. 372</cite>.

##### <a id="s1838"></a> §1838

The third person may be used in questions: <span lang="grc">οὐκοῦν κείσθω ταῦτα</span>; _shall these points be established?_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Laws 820e">P. L. 820e</cite>. Cp. [1842 a](#s1842).

##### <a id="s1839"></a> §1839

The imperative may be used in assumptions (_hypothetical imperative_), to make a concession, or to grant permission: <span lang="grc">ἐμοῦ γ᾽ ἕνεκ᾽ ἔστω</span> _let it be assumed as far as I am concerned_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 20.14">D. 20.14</cite>, <span lang="grc">οὕτως ἐχέτω ὡς σὺ λέγεις</span> _assume it to be as you say_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Sym. 201c">P. S. 201c</cite>. So even as a protasis: <span lang="grc">δειξάτω, κἀ̄γὼ στέρξω</span> _let him set it forth and I will be content_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 18.112">D. 18.112</cite>.

## **NEGATIVE** (**PROHIBITIONS**)

##### <a id="s1840"></a> §1840

Prohibitions are expressed by <span lang="grc">μή</span> with the present or aorist subjunctive in the first person plural; by <span lang="grc">μή</span> with the present imperative or the aorist subjunctive in the second and third person singular or plural (cp. [1800](07-the-moods-in-simple-sentences.md#s1800)). The aorist imperative is rare in prohibitions.

A. **I Person**.—<span lang="grc"> **μὴ γράφωμεν** </span> (<span lang="grc"> **μὴ γράψωμεν** </span>): <span lang="grc">μὴ μαινώμεθα μηδ᾽ αἰσχρῶς ἀπολώμεθα</span> _let us not act like madmen nor perish disgracefully_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 7.1.29">X. A. 7.1.29</cite>.

B. **2 Person**.—<span lang="grc"> **μὴ γράφε** </span> (<span lang="grc"> **μὴ γράφετε** </span>): <span lang="grc">μὴ θαύμαζε</span> _don't be astonished_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Gorg. 482a">P. G. 482a</cite>, <span lang="grc">μὴ θορυβεῖτε</span> _don't raise a disturbance_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Apol. 21a">P. A. 21a</cite>, <span lang="grc">τὰ μὲν ποίει, τὰ δὲ μὴ ποίει</span> _do this and refrain from doing that_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Prot. 325d">P. Pr. 325d</cite>, <span lang="grc">μὴ μέγα λέγε</span> _don't boast so_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Phaedo 95b">P. Ph. 95b</cite>.—<span lang="grc"> **μὴ γράψῃς** </span> (<span lang="grc"> **μὴ γράψητε** </span>): <span lang="grc">μηδὲ θαυμάσῃς τόδε</span> _and do not wonder at this_ <cite data-ref="Aesch. Ag. 879">A. Ag. 879</cite>, <span lang="grc">μὴ θορυβήσητε</span> _don't raise a disturbance_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Apol. 20e">P. A. 20e</cite>, <span lang="grc">μὴ ἄλλως ποιήσῃς</span> _don't do otherwise_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Lach. 201b">P. Lach. 201b</cite>, <span lang="grc">μηδαμῶς ἄλλως ποιήσῃς</span> <cite data-ref="Aristoph. Birds 133">Ar. Av. 133</cite>.

N.—The type <span lang="grc"> **μὴ γράφῃς** </span> is never used. <span lang="grc"> **μὴ γράψον** </span> occurs rarely in poetry (<cite data-ref="Hom. Il. 4.410"><span lang="grc">Δ</span> 410</cite>, <cite data-ref="Hom. Il. 18.134"><span lang="grc">Σ</span> 134</cite>.—<cite data-ref="Hom. Od. 24.248"><span lang="grc">ω</span> 248</cite>, S. fr. 453 parodied in <cite data-ref="Aristoph. Thes. 870">Ar. Thesm. 870</cite>). <!-- page 410 -->

C. **3 Person**.—<span lang="grc"> **μὴ γραφέτω** </span> (<span lang="grc"> **μὴ γραφόντων** </span>): <span lang="grc">μηδεὶς διδασκέτω</span> _let no one tell me_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 1.86">T. 1.86</cite>, <span lang="grc">μηδεὶς τοῦτ᾽ ἀγνοείτω</span> _let no one be ignorant of this fact_ <cite data-ref="Aeschin. 3.6">Aes. 3.6</cite>. <span lang="grc"> **μὴ γραψάτω** </span> (<span lang="grc"> **μὴ γραψάντων** </span>): <span lang="grc">μηδεὶς νομισάτω</span> _let no one think_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 7.5.73">X. C. 7.5.73</cite>, <span lang="grc">μήτ᾽ ἀπογνώτω μηδὲν μήτε καταγνώτω</span> _let him neither acquit nor condemn in any way_ <cite data-ref="Aeschin. 3.60">Aes. 3.60</cite>; and in five other passages giving the actual usage of the orators. In the third person the aorist imperative is much less common than the present imperative.

N.—The type <span lang="grc"> **μὴ γράφῃ** </span> is used only when the third person represents the first person ([1800 c](07-the-moods-in-simple-sentences.md#s1800)). <span lang="grc"> **μὴ γράψῃ** </span> is much more common than <span lang="grc">μὴ γραψάτω</span> in the orators, e.g. <span lang="grc">μηδεὶς θαυμάσῃ</span> _let no one be astonished_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 18.199">D. 18.199</cite>, <span lang="grc">μηδεὶς νομίσῃ</span> _let no one think_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 3.13">T. 3.13</cite>, <cite data-ref="Dem. 23.1">D. 23.1</cite>.

D. The perfect imperative is rare in prohibitions (<span lang="grc">μὴ πεφόβησθε</span> T 6. [17](../../part-1-letters-sounds-syllables-accent/01-the-letters.md#s17)) and is usually poetical. Cp. [698](../../part-2-inflection/10-verb-stem-and-present-stem/13-inflection-of-omega-verbs.md#s698), [712](../../part-2-inflection/10-verb-stem-and-present-stem/13-inflection-of-omega-verbs.md#s712).

##### <a id="s1841"></a> §1841

**a.** <span lang="grc">μὴ γράφε</span>, like _don't write_, is ambiguous and may mean, according to the situation, either _cease writing_ or _abstain from writing_. Commonly <span lang="grc">μὴ γράφε</span> means _do not go on writing, write no more_, and is an order to stop an action already begun. In many cases, however, <span lang="grc">μή</span> with the present imperative does not refer to the interruption of an action already begun, but to an action still in the more or less distant future against which the speaker urges resistance. Sometimes the reference to the future is directly or indirectly indicated by the context.

**b.** <span lang="grc">μὴ γράψῃς</span> usually has the force of (I beg that) _you will not write_, (take care that you) _don't write_, and is commonly a complete prohibition against doing something not already begun. Sometimes, and especially in expressions of a colloquial character, <span lang="grc">μή</span> with the aorist subjunctive marks the speaker's interruption, by anticipation, of a mental (less often of a physical) action that is being done by the person he addresses; as <span lang="grc">μὴ θαυμάσῃς</span> (<cite data-ref="Plat. Laws 804b">P. L. 804b</cite>) in reply to an exclamation of surprise. Here the type <span lang="grc">μὴ γράψῃς</span> often expresses impatience.

**c.** If <span lang="grc">μὴ γράφε</span> elicits a reply, it is (<span lang="grc">ἀλλ᾽</span>) <span lang="grc">οὐ γράφω</span>, while <span lang="grc">μὴ γράψῃς</span> is answered by (<span lang="grc">ἀλλ᾽</span>) <span lang="grc">οὐ γράψω</span>. Thus, <span lang="grc">μή μ᾽ ἐκδίδασκε τοῖς φίλοις εἶναι κακήν. ἀλλ᾽ ου᾽ διδάσκω</span> _do not teach me to be base to my friends. But I do not_ <cite data-ref="Soph. El. 395">S. El. 395</cite>, <span lang="grc">εἰ οὖν ἔχεις ἐναργέστερον ἡμῖν ἐπιδεῖξαι ὡς διδακτόν ἐστιν ἡ ἀρετή, μὴ φθονήσῃς ἀλλ᾽ ἐπίδειξον. ἀλλ᾽ . . . οὐ φθονήσω</span> _now if you can show us more clearly that virtue is capable of being taught, don't refuse, but show us. Well_, _I will not refuse_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Prot. 320c">P. Pr. 320c</cite>. So <span lang="grc">μὴ γράφε</span> commonly answers <span lang="grc">γράφω</span>, as <span lang="grc">θαυμάζω, ἦν δ᾽ ἐγώ, καὶ αὐτός. ἀλλὰ μὴ θαύμαζ᾽, ἔφη</span> _I myself am astonished, said I. Cease your astonishment_, _said she_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Sym. 205b">P. S. 205b</cite>, cp. <cite data-ref="Soph. El. 395">S. El. 395</cite>. So <span lang="grc">μὴ γράψῃς</span> answers <span lang="grc">γράψω</span>, as in <cite data-ref="Hdt. 3.140">Hdt. 3.140</cite>, <cite data-ref="Aristoph. Lys. 1036">Ar. Lys. 1036</cite>.

**d.** <span lang="grc">μὴ γράφε</span> and <span lang="grc">μὴ γράψῃς</span> are often found in closely connected clauses, as <span lang="grc">μηδαμῶς θύ̄μαινέ μοι, μηδέ μ᾽ ἐπιτρί̄ψῃς</span> _don't be angry with me at all, nor ruin me_ <cite data-ref="Aristoph. Cl. 1478">Ar. Nub. 1478</cite>, <span lang="grc">μήτ᾽ ὀκνεῖτε μήτ᾽ ἀφῆτ᾽ ἔπος κακόν</span> _do not shrink from me nor utter any harsh words_ <cite data-ref="Soph. OC 731">S. O. C. 731</cite>. The second prohibition may be more specific than the first, as <span lang="grc">σιώπᾱ· μηδὲν εἴπῃς νήπιον</span> _be silent, don't say anything childish_ <cite data-ref="Aristoph. Cl. 105">Ar. Nub. 105</cite>. Less often <span lang="grc">μὴ γράψῃς</span> is followed by <span lang="grc">μὴ γράφε</span>, as <span lang="grc">μὴ βοηθήσατε τῷ πεπονθότι δεινά· μὴ εὐορκεῖτε</span> (they will say) ‘_do not come to the aid of one who has suffered grievously; have no regard for your oath_’ <cite data-ref="Dem. 21.211">D. 21.211</cite>.

**e.** The difference between <span lang="grc">μὴ γράφε</span> and <span lang="grc">μη` γράψῃς</span> is virtually a difference <!-- page 411 --> of tenses, the present denoting an action continuing, in process; the aorist, an action concluded, summarized. So <span lang="grc">μὴ φοβοῦ</span> _don't be fearful_, <span lang="grc">μὴ φοβηθῇς</span> _don't be frightened_. In maxims <span lang="grc">μή</span> with the present imperative is preferred: <span lang="grc">μὴ κλέπτε</span> _don't be a thief_, <span lang="grc">μὴ κλέψῃς</span> _don't steal_ this or that. <span lang="grc">μηκέτι</span> may be used in either construction. The distinction is often immaterial, often a difference of tone rather than of meaning; sometimes too subtle for dogmatic statement.

##### <a id="s1842"></a> §1842

The imperative may be used in subordinate clauses: <span lang="grc">κρᾱτῆρές εἰσιν . . . ὧν κρᾶτ᾽ ἔρεψον</span> _there are mixing-bowls_, _the brims of which thou must crown_ <cite data-ref="Soph. OC 473">S. O. C. 473</cite>.

**a.** Especially after <span lang="grc">οἶσθα</span> interrogative in dramatic poetry: <span lang="grc">οἶσθ᾽ ὃ δρᾶσον</span>; _do you know what you are to do?_ <cite data-ref="Eur. Hec. 225">E. Hec. 225</cite>, <span lang="grc">οἶσθ᾽ ὡς ποίησον</span>; _do you know how I bid you act?_ <cite data-ref="Soph. OT 543">S. O. T. 543</cite>. <span lang="grc">οἶσθ᾽ ὅ</span> has become a partially fossilized expression, and can be used as subject or be governed by a verb: <span lang="grc">οἶσθά νυν ἅ μοι γενέσθω</span>; _do you know what I must have done for me?_ <cite data-ref="Eur. IT 1203">E. I. T. 1203</cite>.

##### <a id="s1843"></a> §1843

The use of the imperative is to be explained as equivalent to <span lang="grc">δεῖ</span> or <span lang="grc">χρή</span> with the infinitive.

##### <a id="s1844"></a> §1844

<span lang="grc">ἄν</span> is not used with the imperative.
