# THE VERB: VOICES: THE MOODS IN SIMPLE SENTENCES

##### <a id="s1769"></a> §1769

§§ 1770-1849 treat of the use of the moods in independent sentences and principal clauses. The dependent construction of the moods was developed from their independent use. The use of the moods in subordinate clauses was not originally different from that in independent sentences and in the principal clauses of complex sentences. For the uses of the indicative, see also [1875](10-the-tenses.md#s1875)-1958.

## INDICATIVE WITHOUT <span lang="grc">ἄν</span>

##### <a id="s1770"></a> §1770

The indicative mood makes a simple, direct assertion of fact; or asks a question anticipating such an assertion: <span lang="grc">ἦλθε</span> _he came_, <span lang="grc">οὐκ ἦλθε</span> _he did not come_, <span lang="grc">ἐλεύσεται</span> _he will come_, <span lang="grc">πότε ταῦτα ποιήσει</span>; _when will he do this?_

##### <a id="s1771"></a> §1771

The indicative states particular or general suppositions, makes affirmative or negative assertions, which may or may not be absolutely true. Thus, in assumptions, <span lang="grc">ἐξήμαρτέ τις ἄ̄κων· συγγνώμη ἀντὶ τῑμωρίᾱς τούτῳ</span> _suppose some one involuntarily committed an offence; for him there is pardon rather than punish-_ <!-- page 401 --> _ment_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 18.274">D. 18.274</cite>, and often after <span lang="grc">καὶ δή</span>, as <span lang="grc">καὶ δὴ τεθνᾶσι</span> _and suppose they are dead_ <cite data-ref="Eur. Med. 386">E. Med. 386</cite>.

##### <a id="s1772"></a> §1772

The indicative may be used to express a doubtful assertion about a present or past action (negative <span lang="grc">μή</span> or <span lang="grc">μὴ οὐ</span>): <span lang="grc">ἀλλ᾽ ἄρα . . . μὴ ὁ Κτήσιππος ἦν ὁ ταῦτ᾽ εἰπών</span> _but_ I suspect (i.e. perhaps) _after all it was Ctesippus who said this_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Euthyd. 290e">P. Eu. 290e</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἀλλὰ μὴ τοῦτο οὐ καλῶς ὡμολογήσαμεν</span> _but perhaps we did not do well in agreeing to this_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Meno 89c">P. Men. 89c</cite>. Such sentences are often regarded as questions with the effect of doubtful affirmation.

##### <a id="s1773"></a> §1773

The indicative may be used alone where in English we employ an auxiliary verb: <span lang="grc">πιστεύων δὲ θεοῖς πῶς οὐκ εἶναι θεοὺς ἐνόμιζεν</span>; _since he trusted in the gods how could_ (or _should_) _he believe there were no gods?_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Mem. 1.1.5">X. M. 1.1.5</cite>, <span lang="grc">ὀλίγου εἷλον τὴν πόλιν</span> _a little more and they would have taken the city_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 8.35">T. 8.35</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἀπωλλύμεθα</span> _we might have perished_ (_we were in danger of perishing_) <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 5.8.2">X. A. 5.8.2</cite>. Cp. [2319](../29-adverbial-complex-sentences-2193-2487/10-classification-of-conditional-sentences.md#s2319).

##### <a id="s1774"></a> §1774

**Unfulfilled Obligation** (**Propriety, Possibility**).—With the imperfect indicative of impersonal expressions denoting obligation, propriety, necessity, or possibility, the action of a dependent infinitive is usually not realized. (Examples 1775-1776.)

Such expressions are <span lang="grc">ἔδει, χρῆν</span> (or <span lang="grc">ἐχρῆν</span>), <span lang="grc">προσῆκε, καιρὸς ἦν, ἄξιον ἦν, εἰκὸς ἦν, δίκαιον ἦν, αἰσχρὸν ἦν, ἐξῆν, καλῶς εἶχεν</span>, verbals in <span lang="grc">-τόν</span> or <span lang="grc">-τέον</span> with <span lang="grc">ἦν</span>, etc.

**a.** For the use of these expressions (also with <span lang="grc">ἄν</span>) in the apodosis of unreal conditions, see [2313](../29-adverbial-complex-sentences-2193-2487/10-classification-of-conditional-sentences.md#s2313), [2315](../29-adverbial-complex-sentences-2193-2487/10-classification-of-conditional-sentences.md#s2315).

##### <a id="s1775"></a> §1775

**Present**.—Thus, <span lang="grc">ἔδει σε ταῦτα ποιεῖν</span> _you ought to be doing this_ (but are _not_ doing it), <span lang="grc">τούσδε μὴ ζῆν ἔδει</span> _these men ought not to be alive_ <cite data-ref="Soph. Phil. 418">S. Ph. 418</cite>, <span lang="grc">τί σῑγᾷς; οὐκ ἐχρῆν σῑγᾶν</span> _why art thou silent? Thou shouldst not be silent_ <cite data-ref="Eur. Hipp. 297">E. Hipp. 297</cite>, <span lang="grc">εἰκὸς ἦν ὑ̄μᾶς . . . μὴ μαλακῶς, ὥσπερ νῦν, ξυμμαχεῖν</span> _you should not be slack in your alliance, as you are at present_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 6.78">T. 6.78</cite>.

##### <a id="s1776"></a> §1776

**Past**.—<span lang="grc">ἔδει σε ταῦτα ποιῆσαι</span> (or <span lang="grc">ποιεῖν</span>) _you ought to have done this_ (but did _not_ do it), <span lang="grc">ἐξῆν σοι ἐλθεῖν</span> _you might have gone_ (but did _not_ go), <span lang="grc">ἐνῆν αὐτῷ ταῦτα ποιῆσαι</span> _he could have done this_ (almost equivalent to the potential indicative <span lang="grc">ταῦτα ἐποίησεν ἄν</span>, [1784](#s1784)), <span lang="grc">ἔδει τὰ ἐνέχυρα τότε λαβεῖν</span> _I ought to have taken the pledges then_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 7.6.23">X. A. 7.6.23</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἄξιον ἦν ἀκοῦσαι</span> _it would have been worth hearing_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Euthyd. 304d">P. Eu. 304d</cite>, <span lang="grc">μένειν ἐξῆν</span> _he might have remained_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 3.17">D. 3.17</cite>.

##### <a id="s1777"></a> §1777

The Greek usage simply states the obligation (propriety, possibility) as a fact which existed in the past (and may continue to exist in the present). In English we usually express the non-fulfilment of the _action_.

##### <a id="s1778"></a> §1778

Present or past time is denoted when the present infinitive is used. When the reference is to present time, the action of the present infinitive is always denied. Past time is denoted when the aorist infinitive is used.

##### <a id="s1779"></a> §1779

The expressions in 1774 may also refer to simple past obligation (propriety, possibility) and have the ordinary force of past indicatives: <span lang="grc">ἔδει μένειν</span> _he had to remain_ (and did remain) <cite data-ref="Dem. 19.124">D. 19.124</cite>. The context determines the meaning; thus <span lang="grc">τί τὸν σύμβουλον ἐχρῆν ποιεῖν</span>; (<cite data-ref="Dem. 18.190">D. 18.190</cite>) by itself might mean either _what was it the duty of the statesman to do_ or _what was it the duty of the statesman to have done?_ <!-- page 402 -->

##### <a id="s1780"></a> §1780

**Unattainable Wish**.—A wish, referring to the present or past, which cannot be realized, is expressed by a past tense of the indicative with <span lang="grc">εἴθε</span> or with <span lang="grc">εἰ γάρ</span> (negative <span lang="grc">μή</span>). The imperfect refers to present time, the aorist to past time (cp. [2304](../29-adverbial-complex-sentences-2193-2487/10-classification-of-conditional-sentences.md#s2304), [2305](../29-adverbial-complex-sentences-2193-2487/10-classification-of-conditional-sentences.md#s2305)).

<span lang="grc">εἴθ᾽ εἶχες βελτἱ̄ους φρένας</span> _would that thou hadst_ (now) _a better heart_ <cite data-ref="Eur. El. 1061">E. El. 1061</cite>, <span lang="grc">εἴθε σοι τότε συνεγενόμην</span> _would that I had then been with thee_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Mem. 1.2.46">X. M. 1.2.46</cite>.

##### <a id="s1781"></a> §1781

An unattainable wish may also be expressed by <span lang="grc">ὤφελον</span> (_ought_) with the present or aorist infinitive: <span lang="grc">ὤφελε Κῦρος ζῆν</span> _would that Cyrus were_ (now) _alive_ (Cyrus ought to be alive) <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 2.1.4">X. A. 2.1.4</cite> ([1775](#s1775)). The negative is <span lang="grc">μή</span>: <span lang="grc">μήποτ᾽ ὤφελον λιπεῖν τὴν Σκῦρον</span> _would that I had never left Scyros_ <cite data-ref="Soph. Phil. 969">S. Ph. 969</cite>. <span lang="grc">εἴθε</span> or <span lang="grc">εἰ γάρ</span> (poet. <span lang="grc">αἴθε, ὡς</span>) may be used before <span lang="grc">ὤφελον</span>: <span lang="grc">εἰ γὰρ ὤφελον οἷοί τε εἶναι οἱ πολλοὶ κακὰ ἐργάζεσθαι</span> _would that the multitude were able to do evil_ Pl. Cr. 44 d.

##### <a id="s1782"></a> §1782

<span lang="grc">ἐβουλόμην</span> followed by an infinitive may express an unattainable wish: <span lang="grc">ἐβουλόμην μὲν οὐκ ἐρίζειν ἐνθάδε</span> _I would that I were not contending here_ (as I am) <cite data-ref="Aristoph. Frogs 866">Ar. Ran. 866</cite>. (<span lang="grc">ἐβουλόμην ἄν</span> _vellem_, 1789.)

##### <a id="s1783"></a> §1783

The indicative is also used in other than simple sentences: in final sentences ([2203](../29-adverbial-complex-sentences-2193-2487/02-purpose-clauses-final-clauses.md#s2203)); in object sentences after _verbs of effort_ ([2211](../29-adverbial-complex-sentences-2193-2487/03-object-clauses.md#s2211)), of _caution_ ([2220 a](../29-adverbial-complex-sentences-2193-2487/03-object-clauses.md#s2220)), of _fearing_ ([2231](../29-adverbial-complex-sentences-2193-2487/03-object-clauses.md#s2231), [2233](../29-adverbial-complex-sentences-2193-2487/03-object-clauses.md#s2233)); in consecutive sentences with <span lang="grc">ὥστε</span> _so that_ ([2274](../29-adverbial-complex-sentences-2193-2487/07-omegasigmatauepsilon-omegasigma-with-a-finite-verb.md#s2274)), in conditional sentences ([2300](../29-adverbial-complex-sentences-2193-2487/10-classification-of-conditional-sentences.md#s2300), [2303](../29-adverbial-complex-sentences-2193-2487/10-classification-of-conditional-sentences.md#s2303), 2323, [2326](../29-adverbial-complex-sentences-2193-2487/10-classification-of-conditional-sentences.md#s2326)); in temporal sentences ([2395](../29-adverbial-complex-sentences-2193-2487/12-temporal-clauses.md#s2395)); in object sentences after <span lang="grc">ὅτι</span> and <span lang="grc">ὡς</span> with a _verb of saying_, etc. ([2577 f](../31-dependent-substantive-clauses-2574-2635.md#s2577)f.).

## INDICATIVE WITH <span lang="grc">ἄν</span>

##### <a id="s1784"></a> §1784

**Past Potential**.—The past tenses (usually the aorist, less commonly the imperfect) of the indicative with <span lang="grc">ἄν</span> (<span lang="grc">κέν</span>) denote past potentiality, probability (cautious statement), or necessity: <span lang="grc">ὃ οὐκ ἂν ᾤοντο</span> _which they could not have expected_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 7.55">T. 7.55</cite>, <span lang="grc">τίς γὰρ ἂν ᾠήθη ταῦτα γενέσθαι</span>; _for who would have expected these things to happen?_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 9.68">D. 9.68</cite> (note that <span lang="grc">ἄν</span> does not go with <span lang="grc">γενέσθαι</span> by [1764](06-the-particle-alphanu.md#s1764)), <span lang="grc">ἔγνω ἄν τις</span> _one might_ (_could_, _would_) _have known_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 7.1.38">X. C. 7.1.38</cite>, <span lang="grc">ὑπό κεν ταλασίφρονά περ δέος εἷλεν</span> _fear might have seized even a man of stout heart_ <cite data-ref="Hom. Il. 4.421"><span lang="grc">Δ</span> 421</cite>.

**a.** This is especially frequent with <span lang="grc">τὶς</span> and with the ideal second person (cp. _putares_, _crederes_): <span lang="grc">ἐπέγνως ἄν</span> _you would_ (_could_, _might_) _have observed_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 8.1.33">X. C. 8.1.33</cite>.

**b.** The potential optative ([1829](#s1829)) in Homer refers also to the past.

##### <a id="s1785"></a> §1785

A protasis may often be extracted from a participle, or is intimated in some other word; but there is no reference to any definite condition, hence a definite ellipsis is not to be supplied.

##### <a id="s1786"></a> §1786

**Unreal Indicative**.—The indicative of the historical tenses with <span lang="grc">ἄν</span> (<span lang="grc">κέν</span>) may denote unreality: <span lang="grc">τότε δ᾽ αὐτὸ τὸ πρᾶγμ᾽ ἂν ἐκρί̄νετο ἐφ᾽ αὑτοῦ</span> _but the case would then have been decided on its own merits_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 18.224">D. 18.224</cite>, <span lang="grc">καί κεν πολὺ κέρδιον ἦεν</span> _and in that case it were far better_ <cite data-ref="Hom. Il. 3.41"><span lang="grc">Γ</span> 41</cite>.

##### <a id="s1787"></a> §1787

This use of the indicative with <span lang="grc">ἄν</span> to denote unreality is not inherent in the meaning of the past tenses of that mood, but has been developed from the <!-- page 403 --> past potential with which the unreal indicative is closely connected. On the common use of this construction in the apodosis of unreal conditions see [2303](../29-adverbial-complex-sentences-2193-2487/10-classification-of-conditional-sentences.md#s2303). On <span lang="grc">ἔδει ἄν</span>, etc., see [2315](../29-adverbial-complex-sentences-2193-2487/10-classification-of-conditional-sentences.md#s2315).

##### <a id="s1788"></a> §1788

The imperfect refers to the present or the past, the aorist to the past (rarely to the present), the pluperfect to the present (less commonly to the past).

##### <a id="s1789"></a> §1789

<span lang="grc">ἐβουλόμην ἄν</span> (_vellem_) _I should like_ or _should have liked_ may express an unattainable wish: <span lang="grc">ἐβουλόμην ἂν Σίμωνα τὴν αὐτὴν γνώμην ἐμοὶ ἔχειν</span> _I should have liked Simon to be_ (or _I wish Simon were_) _of the same mind as myself_ <cite data-ref="Lys. 3.21">L. 3.21</cite>. On <span lang="grc">ἐβουλόμην</span> without <span lang="grc">ἄν</span>, see [1782](#s1782).

##### <a id="s1790"></a> §1790

**Iterative Indicative** (**repeated action**).—The imperfect and aorist with <span lang="grc">ἄν</span> are used to express repeated or customary past action (post-Homeric): <span lang="grc">διηρώτων ἄν</span> _I used to ask_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Apol. 22b">P. A. 22b</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἂν ἔλεξεν</span> _he was wont to say_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 7.1.10">X. C. 7.1.10</cite>.

##### <a id="s1791"></a> §1791

This construction is connected with the past potential and denoted originally what _could_ or _would_ take place under certain past circumstances. Thus, <span lang="grc">ἀναλαμβάνων οὖν αὐτῶν τὰ ποιήματα . . . διηρώτων ἂν αὐτοὺς τί λέγοιεν</span> _accordingly_, _taking up their poems_, _I used to_ (_would_) _ask them_ (as an opportunity presented itself) _what they meant_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Apol. 22b">P. A. 22b</cite>. In actual use, since the action of the verb _did_ take place, this construction has become a statement of _fact_.

##### <a id="s1792"></a> §1792

In Herodotus this construction is used with the iterative forms: <span lang="grc">κλαίεσκε ἄν</span> _she kept weeping_ 3. 119, <span lang="grc">οἱ δὲ ἂν Πέρσα&lt;\*&gt; λάβεσκον τὰ πρόβατα</span> _the Persians were wont to seize the cattle_ 4. 130.

##### <a id="s1793"></a> §1793

Homer and the early poets use <span lang="grc">ἄν</span> (<span lang="grc">κέν</span>) with the future indicative with a conditional or limiting force: <span lang="grc">καί κέ τις ὧδ᾽ ἐρέει</span> _and in such a case some one will_ (_may_) _say thus_ <cite data-ref="Hom. Il. 4.176"><span lang="grc">Δ</span> 176</cite>. This use is found also in conditional relative sentences ([2565 b](../30-adjective-clauses-relative-clauses-2488-2573.md#s2565)). In Attic <span lang="grc">ἄν</span> is found with the future in a few passages which are now generally emended. In <cite data-ref="Plat. Apol. 29c">P. A. 29c</cite> there is an anacoluthon.

##### <a id="s1794"></a> §1794

<span lang="grc">ἄν</span> is not used with the present and perfect indicative.

## SUBJUNCTIVE WITHOUT <span lang="grc">ἄν</span>

##### <a id="s1795"></a> §1795

The chief uses of the independent subjunctive are the hortatory ([1797](#s1797)), the prohibitive ([1800](#s1800)), and the deliberative ([1805](#s1805)).

**a.** The name _subjunctive_ is due to the belief of the ancient grammarians that the mood was always subordinate. Thus, <span lang="grc">εἴπω</span> _shall I speak?_ ([1805](#s1805)) was explained as due to the omission of a preceding <span lang="grc">βούλει</span>, i.e. _do you wish that I speak?_

##### <a id="s1796"></a> §1796

The independent subjunctive refers to future time. It has three main uses: (1) the voluntative, expressing the _will_ of the speaker. This is akin to the imperative. (2) The deliberative. This is possibly a form of the voluntative. (3) The anticipatory (or futural). This anticipates an action as an immediate future possibility. Whether the anticipatory is a form of the voluntative is uncertain (cp. _ich will sehen, je veux voir_, dialectal _il veut pleuvoir_).

##### <a id="s1797"></a> §1797

**Hortatory Subjunctive**.—The hortatory subjunctive (present or aorist) is used to express a request or a proposal (negative <span lang="grc">μή</span>). <!-- page 404 -->

**a.** Usually in the first person plural: <span lang="grc">νῦν ἴωμεν καὶ ἀκούσωμεν τοῦ ἀνδρός</span> _let us go now and hear the man_ P. Prot. 314 b, <span lang="grc">μήπω ἐκεῖσε ἴωμεν</span> _let's not go there yet_ 311 a. <span lang="grc">ἄγε, φέρε</span> (<span lang="grc">δή</span>), in Hom. <span lang="grc">ἄγε</span> (<span lang="grc">δή</span>), sometimes precedes, as <span lang="grc">ἄγε σκοπῶμεν</span> _come_, _let us consider_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 5.5.15">X. C. 5.5.15</cite>. <span lang="grc">ἴθι</span> (<span lang="grc">δή</span>) rarely precedes.

**b.** Less frequently in the first person singular, which is usually preceded (in affirmative sentences) by <span lang="grc">φέρε</span> (<span lang="grc">δή</span>), in Hom. by <span lang="grc">ἄγε</span> (<span lang="grc">δή</span>): <span lang="grc">φέρε δὴ περὶ τοῦ ψηφίσματος εἴπω</span> _let me now speak about the bill_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 19.234">D. 19.234</cite>.

##### <a id="s1798"></a> §1798

The first person singular in negative exhortations (rare and poetic) may convey a warning or a threat: <span lang="grc">μή σε, γέρον, κοίλῃσιν παρὰ νηυσὶ κιχείω</span> _old man_, _let me not find thee by the hollow ships_ A 26. This use is often regarded as prohibitive ([1800](#s1800)).

##### <a id="s1799"></a> §1799

The hortatory use of the subjunctive compensates for the absence of an imperative of the first person.

##### <a id="s1800"></a> §1800

**Prohibitive Subjunctive**.—The subjunctive (in the second and third persons of the aorist) is often used to express prohibitions (negative <span lang="grc">μή</span>).

**a.** Usually in the second person: <span lang="grc">μηδὲν ἀθῡμήσητε</span> _do not lose heart_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 5.4.19">X. A. 5.4.19</cite>. For the aorist subjunctive the present imperative may be employed ([1840](08-imperative.md#s1840)): <span lang="grc">μὴ ποιήσῃς</span> (or <span lang="grc">μὴ ποίει</span>) <span lang="grc">ταῦτα</span> _do not do this_ (_not_ <span lang="grc">μὴ ποιῇς</span>).

**b.** Less commonly in the third person, which usually represents the second: <span lang="grc">ὑπολάβῃ δὲ μηδείς</span> _and let no one suppose_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 6.84">T. 6.84</cite> ( = <span lang="grc">μὴ ὑπολάβητε</span> _do not suppose_).

**c.** The third person of the present subjunctive is rare: <span lang="grc">μὴ τοίνυν τις οἴηται</span> ( = <span lang="grc">μὴ οἰώμεθα</span>) _let not any one think_ P. L. 861 E.

N.—<span lang="grc">οὐ μή</span> with the subjunctive of the second person in the dramatic poets occasionally expresses a strong prohibition: <span lang="grc">οὐ μὴ ληρήσῃς</span> _don't talk nonsense_ <cite data-ref="Aristoph. Cl. 367">Ar. Nub. 367</cite>.

##### <a id="s1801"></a> §1801

**Doubtful Assertion**.—The present subjunctive with <span lang="grc">μή</span> may express a doubtful assertion, with <span lang="grc">μὴ οὐ</span> a doubtful negation. The idea of apprehension or anxiety (real or assumed) is due to the situation. A touch of irony often marks this use, which is chiefly Platonic. With <span lang="grc">μή</span> (of what may be true): <span lang="grc">μὴ ἀγροικότερον ᾖ τὸ ἀληθὲς εἰπεῖν</span> _I suspect it's rather bad form_ (lit. _too rude_) _to tell the truth_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Gorg. 462e">P. G. 462e</cite>. With <span lang="grc">μὴ οὐ</span> (of what may not be true): <span lang="grc">ἀλλὰ μὴ οὐχ οὕτως ἔχῃ</span> _but I rather think this may not be so_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Crat. 436b">P. Crat. 436b</cite>, <span lang="grc">μὴ οὐκ ᾖ διδακτὸν ἀρετή</span> _virtue is perhaps not a thing to be taught_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Meno 94e">P. Men. 94e</cite>.

##### <a id="s1802"></a> §1802

In Hom. <span lang="grc">μή</span> with the independent subjunctive is used to indicate fear and warning, or to suggest danger: <span lang="grc">μή τι χολωσάμενος ῥέξῃ κακὸν υἷας Ἀχαιῶν</span> _may he not_ (as I fear he may) _in his anger do aught to injure the sons of the Achaeans_ B 195. Usually with the aorist, rarely with the present subjunctive (<cite data-ref="Hom. Od. 15.19"><span lang="grc">ο</span> 19</cite>). The constructions of 1801, 1802 are used as object clauses after _verbs of fearing_ ([2221](../29-adverbial-complex-sentences-2193-2487/03-object-clauses.md#s2221)).

##### <a id="s1803"></a> §1803

<span lang="grc">ὅπως μή</span> is occasionally so used with the aorist subjunctive, and with an idea of command: <span lang="grc">ὅπως μὴ φήσῃ τις</span> _may no one say_ (as I fear he may) <cite data-ref="Xen. Sym. 4">X. S. 4</cite>. 8. See [1921](10-the-tenses.md#s1921).

##### <a id="s1804"></a> §1804

From the use in 1801 is probably developed the construction of <span lang="grc">οὐ μή</span> <!-- page 405 --> with the aorist (less often the present) subjunctive to denote an emphatic denial; as <span lang="grc">οὐ μὴ παύσωμαι φιλοσοφῶν</span> _I will not cease from searching for wisdom_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Apol. 29d">P. A. 29d</cite>, <span lang="grc">οὐκέτι μὴ δύνηται βασιλεὺς ἡμᾶς καταλαβεῖν</span> _the king will no longer be able to overtake us_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 2.2.12">X. A. 2.2.12</cite>.

##### <a id="s1805"></a> §1805

**Deliberative Subjunctive**.—The deliberative subjunctive (present or aorist) is used in questions when the speaker asks _what he is to do_ or _say_ (negative <span lang="grc">μή</span>).

**a.** Usually in the first person: <span lang="grc">εἴπωμεν ἢ σῑγῶμεν</span>; _shall we speak or keep silence?_ <cite data-ref="Eur. Ion 758">E. Ion 758</cite>, <span lang="grc">τί δρά̄σω; ποῖ φύγω</span>; _what am I to do? whither shall I fly?_ <cite data-ref="Eur. Med. 1271">E. Med. 1271</cite>, <span lang="grc">μὴ φῶμεν</span>; _shall we not say?_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Rep. 554b">P. R. 554b</cite>.

**b.** The (rare) second person is used in repeating a question: A. <span lang="grc">τί σοι πιθώμεθα</span>; B. <span lang="grc">ὅ τι πίθησθε</span>; A. _In what shall we take your advice?_ B. _In what shall you take my advice?_ <cite data-ref="Aristoph. Birds 164">Ar. Av. 164</cite>.

**c.** The third person is generally used to represent the first person; commonly with <span lang="grc">τὶς</span>, as <span lang="grc">τί τις εἶναι τοῦτο φῇ</span>; _how shall anyone say this is so?_ ( = <span lang="grc">τί φῶμεν</span>;) <cite data-ref="Dem. 19.88">D. 19.88</cite>.

N.—The subjunctive question does not refer to a future fact, but to what is, under the present circumstances, advantageous or proper to do or say.

##### <a id="s1806"></a> §1806

<span lang="grc">βούλει, βούλεσθε</span> (poet. <span lang="grc">θέλεις, θέλετε</span>) _do you wish_ often precede the subjunctive: <span lang="grc">βούλει σοι εἴπω</span>; _do you wish me to say to you?_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Gorg. 521d">P. G. 521d</cite>. This is a fusion of two distinct questions: <span lang="grc">βούλει</span> _do you wish?_ and <span lang="grc">εἴπω</span> _shall I say?_

##### <a id="s1807"></a> §1807

The deliberative subjunctive may be replaced by a periphrasis with <span lang="grc">δεῖ</span> or <span lang="grc">χρή</span> and the infinitive, or by the verbal adjective in <span lang="grc">-τέον ἐστί</span>. Thus, <span lang="grc">ἡμεῖς δὲ προσμένωμεν; ἢ τί χρὴ ποιεῖν</span>; _and shall we wait? or what must we do?_ <cite data-ref="Soph. Trach. 390">S. Tr. 390</cite>, <span lang="grc">τί ποιητέον</span>; ( = <span lang="grc">τί ποιῶμεν</span>;) _what are we to do?_ <cite data-ref="Aristoph. Peace 922">Ar. P. 922</cite>.

**a.** For the deliberative future see [1916](10-the-tenses.md#s1916).

##### <a id="s1808"></a> §1808

Deliberation in the past may be expressed by <span lang="grc">ἔδει, χρῆν</span> (<span lang="grc">ἐχρῆν</span>), <span lang="grc">ἔμελλον</span> with the infinitive, and by <span lang="grc">-τέον</span> (verbal adj.) <span lang="grc">ἦν</span>.

##### <a id="s1809"></a> §1809

**The Negative in Questions**.—The use of <span lang="grc">μή</span> (not <span lang="grc">οὐ</span>) in questions is due to the fact that the construction of 1805 is simply the interrogative form of the hortatory subjunctive: <span lang="grc">φῶμεν</span> _let us say_, <span lang="grc">μὴ φῶμεν</span>; _are we not to say?_ Distinguish <span lang="grc">πότερον βίᾱν φῶμεν ἢ μὴ φῶμεν εἶναι</span>; _shall we say that it is force or that it is not?_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Mem. 1.2.45">X. M. 1.2.45</cite>, from <span lang="grc">φῶμεν ταῦτ᾽ ὀρθῶς λέγεσθαι ἢ οὔ</span> _shall we say that this is well said or not?_ (<span lang="grc">οὔ ῀ οὐκ ὀρθῶς λέγεσθαι</span>) <cite data-ref="Plat. Gorg. 514c">P. G. 514c</cite>.

##### <a id="s1810"></a> §1810

**Anticipatory Subjunctive** (**Homeric Subjunctive**).—In Homer the subjunctive is often closely akin to the future indicative, and refers by anticipation to a future event (negative <span lang="grc">οὐ</span>): <span lang="grc">οὐ γάρ πω τοίους ἴδον ἀ̄νέρας, οὐδὲ ἴδωμαι</span> _for never yet saw I such men, nor shall I see them_ A 262, <span lang="grc">καί νύ τις ὧδ᾽ εἴπῃσι</span> _and one will say_ <cite data-ref="Hom. Od. 14.275"><span lang="grc">ξ</span> 275</cite>. <span lang="grc">ἄν</span> (<span lang="grc">κέν</span>) usually limits this subjunctive in Hom. ([1813](#s1813)).

**a.** This futural subjunctive is retained in Attic only in subordinate clauses ([2327](../29-adverbial-complex-sentences-2193-2487/10-classification-of-conditional-sentences.md#s2327)), and in <span lang="grc">τί πάθω</span> ([1811](#s1811)).

##### <a id="s1811"></a> §1811

The subjunctive is used in <span lang="grc">τί πάθω</span>; _what will become of me; what am I to do?_ (lit. _what shall I undergo?_) as <cite data-ref="Plat. Euthyd. 302d">P. Eu. 302d</cite>. So <span lang="grc">τί γένωμαι</span>; _quid me fiet?_ Thus, <span lang="grc">ὤ μοι ἐγώ, τί πάθω; τί νύ μοι μήκιστα γένηται</span>; _ah_, _woe's me!_ <!-- page 406 --> _what is to become of me? what will happen unto me at the last?_ <cite data-ref="Hom. Od. 5.465"><span lang="grc">ε</span> 465</cite>. The subjunctive here is not deliberative, but refers to a future event.

##### <a id="s1812"></a> §1812

The subjunctive without <span lang="grc">ἄν</span> is also used in dependent clauses of purpose ([2196](../29-adverbial-complex-sentences-2193-2487/02-purpose-clauses-final-clauses.md#s2196)), after _verbs of fearing_ ([2225](../29-adverbial-complex-sentences-2193-2487/03-object-clauses.md#s2225)), in the protasis of conditional ([2327](../29-adverbial-complex-sentences-2193-2487/10-classification-of-conditional-sentences.md#s2327), [2339](../29-adverbial-complex-sentences-2193-2487/10-classification-of-conditional-sentences.md#s2339)) and conditional relative sentences ([2567 b](../30-adjective-clauses-relative-clauses-2488-2573.md#s2567)).

## SUBJUNCTIVE WITH <span lang="grc">ἄν</span>

##### <a id="s1813"></a> §1813

The subjunctive with <span lang="grc">ἄν</span> (more commonly <span lang="grc">κέν</span>) is used in Homer in independent sentences and clauses (negative <span lang="grc">οὐ</span>). Cp. [1810](#s1810). Thus, <span lang="grc">ἐγὼ δέ κ᾽ ἄγω Βρῑσηίδα</span> _but in that case I will take Briseis_ A 184, <span lang="grc">οὐκ ἄν τοι χραίσμῃ βιός</span> _of no avail to thee shall be thy bow_ <cite data-ref="Hom. Il. 11.387"><span lang="grc">Λ</span> 387</cite>.

## OPTATIVE WITHOUT <span lang="grc">ἄν</span>

##### <a id="s1814"></a> §1814

**Optative of Wish**.—In independent sentences the optative without <span lang="grc">ἄν</span> is used to express a wish referring to the future (negative <span lang="grc">μή</span>): <span lang="grc">ὦ παῖ, γένοιο πατρὸς εὐτυχέστερος</span> _ah_, _boy_, _mayest thou prove more fortunate than thy sire_ <cite data-ref="Soph. Aj. 550">S. Aj. 550</cite>. From this use is derived the name of the mood (Lat. opto _wish_).

**a.** So even in relative sentences: <span lang="grc">ἐά̄ν ποτε, δ̀ μὴ γένοιτο, λάβωσι τὴν πόλιν</span> _if ever they capture the city, which Heaven forbid_ <cite data-ref="Lys. 31.14">L. 31.14</cite>.

**b.** Under wishes are included execrations and protestations: <span lang="grc">ἐξολοίμην</span> _may I perish_ <cite data-ref="Aristoph. Ach. 324">Ar. Ach. 324</cite>, <span lang="grc">καί σ᾽ ἐπιδείξω, ἢ μὴ ζῴην, δωροδοκήσαντα</span> _and I will prove that you took bribes, or may I not live_ <cite data-ref="Aristoph. Kn. 833">Ar. Eq. 833</cite>.

##### <a id="s1815"></a> §1815

The optative of wish is often introduced by <span lang="grc">εἰ γάρ, εἴθε</span> (Hom. <span lang="grc">αι᾽ γάρ, αἴθε</span>), or by <span lang="grc">εἰ, ὡς</span> (both poetical): <span lang="grc">εἰ γὰρ γένοιτο</span> _would that it might happen_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 6.1.38">X. C. 6.1.38</cite>, <span lang="grc">ὡς ὄλοιτο</span> _may he perish_ <cite data-ref="Soph. El. 126">S. El. 126</cite>. (<span lang="grc">ὡς</span> is properly an exclamation: _how_.)

##### <a id="s1816"></a> §1816

The optative introduced by <span lang="grc">εἰ γάρ</span>, etc. is sometimes explained as a protasis with the conclusion omitted: <span lang="grc">εἴθε φίλος ἡμῖν γένοιο</span> _oh_, _if you would become our friend_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Hell. 4.1.38">X. H. 4.1.38</cite>. Cp. [2352 e](../29-adverbial-complex-sentences-2193-2487/10-classification-of-conditional-sentences.md#s2352).

##### <a id="s1817"></a> §1817

An unattainable wish, referring to the present, may be expressed by the present optative in Homer: <span lang="grc">εἴθ᾽ ἡβώοιμι</span> _would that I were young again_ H 157.

##### <a id="s1818"></a> §1818

Unattainable wishes, when they refer to the future, may be expressed by the optative: <span lang="grc">εἴ μοι γένοιτο φθόλλος ἐν βραχί̄οσι</span> _would that I had a voice in my arms_ <cite data-ref="Eur. Hec. 836">E. Hec. 836</cite>. Wishes represented as hopeless are expressed in the postHomeric language by the past tenses of the indicative ([1780](#s1780)) or by <span lang="grc">ὤφελον</span> ([1781](#s1781)).

##### <a id="s1819"></a> §1819

Hom. often uses the optative with a concessive or permissive force: <span lang="grc">ἔπειτα δὲ καί τι πάθοιμι</span> _after that I may_ (lit. _may I_) _suffer come what will_ <cite data-ref="Hom. Il. 21.274"><span lang="grc">Φ</span> 274</cite>.

##### <a id="s1820"></a> §1820

**Imperative Optative**.—The optative may express a command or exhortation with a force nearly akin to the imperative: <span lang="grc">Χειρίσοφος ἡγοῖτο</span> _let Chirisophus lead_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 3.2.37">X. A. 3.2.37</cite>.

##### <a id="s1821"></a> §1821

Potential Optative.—The potential optative, which in Attic regu<!-- page 407 --> larly takes <span lang="grc">ἄν</span> ([1824](#s1824)), is occasionally found in Homer and later poetry in an earlier form, without that particle: <span lang="grc">ῥεῖα θεός γ᾽ ἐθέλων καὶ τηλόθεν ἄνδρα σαώσαι</span> _easily might a god, if he so willed, bring a man safe even from afar_ <cite data-ref="Hom. Od. 3.231"><span lang="grc">γ</span> 231</cite>, <span lang="grc">θᾶσσον ἢ λέγοι τις</span> _quicker than a man could speak_ <cite data-ref="Eur. Hipp. 1186">E. Hipp. 1186</cite>. This construction is suspected in prose.

**a.** Usually in negative sentences or in questions expecting a negative answer (with <span lang="grc">οὐ</span>): <span lang="grc">οὐ μὲν γάρ τι κακώτερον ἄλλο πάθοιμι</span> _for I could not_ (conceivably) _suffer anything worse_ T 321, <span lang="grc">τεά̄ν, Ζεῦ, δύνασιν τίς ἀνδρῶν ὑπερβασίᾱ κατάσχοι</span>; _thy power, oh Zeus_, _what trespass of man can check?_ <cite data-ref="Soph. Ant. 604">S. Ant. 604</cite>.

##### <a id="s1822"></a> §1822

The optative after <span lang="grc">οὐκ ἔστιν ὅστις</span> (<span lang="grc">ὅπως, ὅποι</span>) in the dramatists is probably potential: <span lang="grc">οὐκ ἔσθ᾽ ὅπως λέξαιμι τὰ ψευδῆ καλά</span> _I could not call false tidings fair_ <cite data-ref="Aesch. Ag. 620">A. Ag. 620</cite>. <span lang="grc">ἄν</span> is usually employed in this construction.

##### <a id="s1823"></a> §1823

The optative without <span lang="grc">ἄν</span> (<span lang="grc">κέν</span>) is also used elsewhere, as in purpose clauses ([2196](../29-adverbial-complex-sentences-2193-2487/02-purpose-clauses-final-clauses.md#s2196)) and clauses of _fearing_ ([2225](../29-adverbial-complex-sentences-2193-2487/03-object-clauses.md#s2225)) after a secondary tense; in the apodosis of conditional sentences ([2300 d](../29-adverbial-complex-sentences-2193-2487/10-classification-of-conditional-sentences.md#s2300), [2326](../29-adverbial-complex-sentences-2193-2487/10-classification-of-conditional-sentences.md#s2326) d, [2333](../29-adverbial-complex-sentences-2193-2487/10-classification-of-conditional-sentences.md#s2333)), in relative sentences ([2566](../30-adjective-clauses-relative-clauses-2488-2573.md#s2566), [2568](../30-adjective-clauses-relative-clauses-2488-2573.md#s2568)); and as the representative of the indicative ([2615](../31-dependent-substantive-clauses-2574-2635.md#s2615)) or subjunctive ([2619](../31-dependent-substantive-clauses-2574-2635.md#s2619)) in indirect discourse after secondary tenses.

## OPTATIVE WITH <span lang="grc">ἄν</span>

##### <a id="s1824"></a> §1824

**Potential Optative**.—The potential optative with <span lang="grc">ἄν</span> states a future possibility, propriety, or likelihood, as an _opinion_ of the speaker; and may be translated by _may_, _might_, _can_ (especially with a negative), _must_, _would_, _should_ (rarely _will_, _shall_). So in Latin _velim_, _videas_, _cognoscas_, _credas_.

<span lang="grc">γνοίης δ᾽ ἂν ὅτι τοῦθ᾽ οὕτως ἔχει</span> _you may see that this is so_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 1.6.21">X. C. 1.6.21</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἅπαντες ἂν ὁμολογήσειαν</span> _all would agree_ <cite data-ref="Isoc. 11.5">I. 11.5</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἡδέως ἂν ἐροίμην</span> _I_ (would gladly ask) _should like to ask_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 18.64">D. 18.64</cite>, <span lang="grc">οὐκ ἂν λάβοις</span> _thou canst not take_ <cite data-ref="Soph. Phil. 103">S. Ph. 103</cite>, <span lang="grc">λέγοιμ᾽ ἂν τάδε</span> _I will tell this_ <cite data-ref="Aesch. Supp. 928">A. Supp. 928</cite>. The second person singular is often indefinite (_one_), as <span lang="grc">γνοίης ἄν</span> (_cognoscas_) = <span lang="grc">γνοίη τις ἄν</span>.

**a.** The potential optative ranges from possibility to fixed resolve. The aorist optative with <span lang="grc">ἄν</span> and a negative is very common.

**b.** When stress is laid on the idea of possibility and power, necessity and obligation, Greek uses <span lang="grc">δύναμαι, δεῖ</span> or <span lang="grc">χρή</span> with the infinitive (statement of _fact_).

**c.** The potential optative with <span lang="grc">ἄν</span> is also used in dependent sentences; in purpose clauses ([2202 b](../29-adverbial-complex-sentences-2193-2487/02-purpose-clauses-final-clauses.md#s2202)), in object clauses after _verbs of effort_ ([2216](../29-adverbial-complex-sentences-2193-2487/03-object-clauses.md#s2216)) and _verbs of fearing_ ([2232](../29-adverbial-complex-sentences-2193-2487/03-object-clauses.md#s2232)), in causal clauses ([2243](../29-adverbial-complex-sentences-2193-2487/04-causal-clauses.md#s2243)), in result clauses ([2278](../29-adverbial-complex-sentences-2193-2487/07-omegasigmatauepsilon-omegasigma-with-a-finite-verb.md#s2278)), in the apodosis of conditional (see [2356](../29-adverbial-complex-sentences-2193-2487/10-classification-of-conditional-sentences.md#s2356)) and conditional relative sentences ([2566](../30-adjective-clauses-relative-clauses-2488-2573.md#s2566)). In indirect discourse the infinitive with <span lang="grc">ἄν</span> or the participle with <span lang="grc">ἄν</span> may represent the optative with <span lang="grc">ἄν</span> ([1845 f](09-infinitive-and-participle-with-alphanu.md#s1845)f.).

##### <a id="s1825"></a> §1825

Usually these optatives are not limited by any definite condition present to the mind, and it is unnecessary to supply any protasis in thought. In some cases a protasis is dormant in a word of the sentence (such as <span lang="grc">δικαίως, εἰκότως</span>). Thus, in <span lang="grc">οὓς ἀχαρίστους εἶναι δικαίως ἂν ὑπολαμβάνοιτε</span> _whom you would justly consider to be ungrateful_ <cite data-ref="Aeschin. 3.196">Aes. 3.196</cite>, <span lang="grc">δικαίως</span> may stand for <span lang="grc">εἰ δικαίως ὑπολαμβάνοιτε</span>: _if you should consider_ the matter _justly_. So <span lang="grc">οὔτε ἐσθίουσι πλείω ἢ</span> <!-- page 408 --> <span lang="grc">δύνανται φέρειν· διαρραγεῖεν γὰρ ἄν κτλ</span>. _they neither eat more than they can bear_, _for_ otherwise (_if they should eat more:_ <span lang="grc">εἰ ἐσθίοιεν πλείω</span>) _they would burst_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 8.2.21">X. C. 8.2.21</cite>. The potential optative is also used as the main clause of less vivid conditions ([2329](../29-adverbial-complex-sentences-2193-2487/10-classification-of-conditional-sentences.md#s2329)) in which the protasis has the optative by assimilation to the mood of the apodosis.

##### <a id="s1826"></a> §1826

The potential optative with <span lang="grc">ἄν</span> is used to soften the statement of an opinion or fact, or to express irony: <span lang="grc">ἕτερόν τι τοῦτ᾽ ἂν εἴη</span> _this is_ (would be) _another matter_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 20.116">D. 20.116</cite>, <span lang="grc">νοσοῖμ᾽ ἄν, εἰ νόσημα τοὺς ἐχθροὺς στυγεῖν</span> _I must be mad_, _if it is madness to hate one's foes_ <cite data-ref="Aesch. PB 978">A. Pr. 978</cite>. So often with <span lang="grc">ἴσως</span> or <span lang="grc">τάχα</span> _perhaps_.

**a.** With a negative, the potential optative may have the force of a strong assertion: <span lang="grc">οὐ γὰρ ἂν ἀπέλθοιμ᾽, ἀλλὰ κόψω τὴν θύρᾱν</span> _for I will not go away, but I will knock at the door_ <cite data-ref="Aristoph. Ach. 236">Ar. Ach. 236</cite>.

##### <a id="s1827"></a> §1827

<span lang="grc">βουλοίμην ἄν</span> (_velim_) is often used as a softened optative of wish: <span lang="grc">βουλοίμην ἂν τοῦτο οὕτω γενέσθαι</span> _I could wish that this might be the result_ (<span lang="grc">οὕτω γένοιτο</span> _may it result thus_) <cite data-ref="Plat. Apol. 19a">P. A. 19a</cite>. For <span lang="grc">ἐβουλόμην ἄν</span> see [1789](#s1789).

##### <a id="s1828"></a> §1828

The present and aorist are used of what will be, or what will prove to be, true (future realization of a present fact): <span lang="grc">ἀρετὴ ἄρα, ὡς ἔοικεν, ὑγίειά τις ἂν εἴη</span> _virtue then, it seems, will_ (prove to) _be a kind of health_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Rep. 444d">P. R. 444d</cite>. The perfect is used of what will prove to be the case as regards a completed action: <span lang="grc">πῶς ἂν λελήθοι</span>; _how can it have escaped my knowledge?_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Sym. 3">X. S. 3</cite>. 6. Usually the perfect is here equivalent to the present.

##### <a id="s1829"></a> §1829

The present and aorist are rarely used of the past: (**a**) in Hom. of past possibility: <span lang="grc">καί νύ κεν ἔνθ᾽ ἀπόλοιτο</span> _and now he might have perished_ E 311 (Attic <span lang="grc">ἀπώλετο ἄν</span>, [1784](#s1784)), <span lang="grc">ἀλλὰ τί κεν ῥέξαιμι</span>; _but what could I do?_ T 90. (**b**) in Hdt. of a mild assertion: <span lang="grc">ταῦτα μὲν καὶ φθόνῳ ἂν εἴποιεν</span> _they may have said this out of envy_ 9. 71, <span lang="grc">εἴησαν δ᾽ ἂν οὖτοι Κρῆτες</span> _these would prove to be_ (might be, must have been) _Cretans_ 1. 2. Both uses are doubtful in Attic prose.

##### <a id="s1830"></a> §1830

The potential optative with <span lang="grc">ἄν</span> may be used, in a sense akin to that of the imperative, to express a command, exhortation, or request: <span lang="grc">λέγοις ἂν τὴν δέησιν</span> _tell me_ (you may tell) _your request_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Parm. 126a">P. Par. 126a</cite>, <span lang="grc">προάγοις ἄν</span> _move on_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Phaedrus 229b">P. Phae. 229b</cite>. This courteous formula is used even where a harsh command might be expected: <span lang="grc">χωροῖς ἂν εἴσω σὺν τάχει</span> _go within with all speed_ <cite data-ref="Soph. El. 1491">S. El. 1491</cite>.

**a.** In <span lang="grc">ποῖ δῆτ᾽ ἂν τραποίμην</span>; _whither pray shall I turn?_ <cite data-ref="Aristoph. Frogs 296">Ar. Ran. 296</cite> the use is akin to the deliberative subjunctive ([1805](#s1805)) or deliberative future ([1916](10-the-tenses.md#s1916)).

##### <a id="s1831"></a> §1831

The potential optative with <span lang="grc">ἄν</span> is used in questions: <span lang="grc">τίς οὐκ ἂν ὁμολογήσειεν</span>; _who would not agree?_ (<span lang="grc">οὐδείς</span>: _scil_. <span lang="grc">οὐκ ἂν ὁμολογήσειε</span>) <cite data-ref="Xen. Mem. 1.1.5">X. M. 1.1.5</cite>. So even the optative of wish: <span lang="grc">τί δ᾽ ὅρκῳ τῷδε μὴ ᾿μμένων πάθοις</span>; _but if thou dost not abide by thy oath what dost thou invoke upon thyself?_ <cite data-ref="Eur. Med. 754">E. Med. 754</cite> (lit. _mayest thou suffer what?_).

##### <a id="s1832"></a> §1832

<span lang="grc">πῶς ἄν, τίς ἄν</span> with the potential optative may be used to express a wish (especially in the tragic poets): <span lang="grc">πῶς ἂν ὀλοίμᾱν</span> _oh_, _would that I might die_ <cite data-ref="Eur. Med. 97">E. Med. 97</cite>, <span lang="grc">τίς ἂν ἐν τάχει μόλοι μοῖρα</span> _oh_, _that some fate would speedily come_ <cite data-ref="Aesch. Ag. 1448">A. Ag. 1448</cite>. Properly this usage is not a wish, but is simply a question _how_ the wish may be fulfilled.

##### <a id="s1833"></a> §1833

The potential optative with <span lang="grc">ἄν</span> (especially with negatives) may ex<!-- page 409 --> change with the indicative: <span lang="grc">φημὶ καὶ οὐκ ἂν ἀρνηθείην</span> _I assert and cannot deny_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 21.191">D. 21.191</cite>. It is often stronger, though more courteous, than the future indicative: <span lang="grc">οὐκ ἂν πέρᾱ φράσαιμι</span> _I will speak no more_ <cite data-ref="Soph. OT 343">S. O. T. 343</cite>.

##### <a id="s1834"></a> §1834

The future optative with <span lang="grc">ἄν</span> occurs only in a few suspected passages.
