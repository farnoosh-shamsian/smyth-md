# ADVERBIAL COMPLEX SENTENCES (2193-2487): CONDITIONAL CLAUSES

##### <a id="s2280"></a> §2280

A condition is a supposition on which a statement is based. A conditional sentence commonly consists of two clauses:

The protasis: the conditional, or subordinate, clause, expressing a supposed or assumed case (_if_).

The apodosis: the conclusion, or principal clause, expressing what follows if the condition is realized. The truth or fulfilment of the conclusion depends on the truth or fulfilment of the conditional clause.

**a.** The protasis has its name from <span lang="grc">πρότασις</span>, lit. _stretching forward, that which is put forward_ (in logic, a premiss); the apodosis, from <span lang="grc">ἀπόδοσις</span>, lit. _giving back_, _return; i.e._ the _resuming_ or _answering_ clause.

##### <a id="s2281"></a> §2281

The protasis usually precedes, but may follow, the apodosis.

##### <a id="s2282"></a> §2282

The protasis is introduced by <span lang="grc">εἰ</span> _if_.

**a.** Homer has also <span lang="grc">αι᾽</span>, which is an Aeolic (and Doric' form.

##### <a id="s2283"></a> §2283

With the subjunctive mood, <span lang="grc"> **ει᾽** </span> commonly takes <span lang="grc"> **ἄν** </span> (Epic <span lang="grc"> **εἴ κε** </span> or <span lang="grc"> **εἴ κεν** </span>, not <span lang="grc"> **ἐά̄ν** </span>).

**a.** There are three forms, <span lang="grc">**ἐά̄ν**, **ἤν**, **ἄ̄ν**. ἐά̄ν</span> is the ordinary form in Attic prose and inscriptions; <span lang="grc">ἤν</span> appears in Ionic and in the older Attic writers (the tragic poets and Thucydides); <span lang="grc">ἄ̄ν</span>, generally in the later writers (sometimes together with <span lang="grc">ἐά̄ν</span>), very rarely in Attic inscriptions. In Plato <span lang="grc">ἄ̄ν</span> is commoner than <span lang="grc">ἐά̄ν</span>. Xenophon has all three forms.

**b.** <span lang="grc">ἤν</span> is from <span lang="grc">εἰ ¨ ἄν, ἄ̄ν</span> from <span lang="grc">η᾽</span> (another form of <span lang="grc">εἰ</span>) + <span lang="grc">ἄν</span>. The etymology of <span lang="grc">ἐά̄ν</span> is uncertain: either from <span lang="grc">η᾽ ¨ ἄν</span> or from <span lang="grc">εἰ ¨ ἄ̄ν</span>. <!-- page 513 -->

##### <a id="s2284"></a> §2284

The particle <span lang="grc">ἄν</span> is used in the apodosis: (1) with the optative, to denote possibility (cp. [1824](../20-the-verb-voices/07-the-moods-in-simple-sentences.md#s1824)); (2) with the past tenses of the indicative, to denote either the non-fulfilment of the condition ([1786](../20-the-verb-voices/07-the-moods-in-simple-sentences.md#s1786)) or, occasionally, repetition ([1790](../20-the-verb-voices/07-the-moods-in-simple-sentences.md#s1790)).

##### <a id="s2285"></a> §2285

The apodosis may be introduced by <span lang="grc">δέ</span> or <span lang="grc">ἀλλά</span>, less often by <span lang="grc">αὐτάρ</span>. See under Particles. <span lang="grc">νῦν δέ</span> _as it is, as it was_ corrects a supposition contrary to fact. The apodosis sometimes has <span lang="grc">τότε, τότε δή, οὕτως</span> (Hom. <span lang="grc">τῷ</span>) comparable to Eng. _then_, _in that case_ in the conclusion of conditional sentences.

##### <a id="s2286"></a> §2286

The negative of the protasis is <span lang="grc">μή</span> because the subordinate clause expresses something that is _conceived_ or _imagined_. <span lang="grc">μή</span> negatives the conditional clause _as a whole_. On <span lang="grc">οὐ</span> adherescent in protasis, see [2698](../35-negative-sentences.md#s2698).

The negative of the apodosis is <span lang="grc">οὐ</span>, in case the principal clause states the conclusion as a _fact_ on the supposition that the protasis is true; <span lang="grc">μή</span>, when the construction requires that negative ([2689](../35-negative-sentences.md#s2689)).

##### <a id="s2287"></a> §2287

The indicative, subjunctive, and optative moods, and the participle may stand in protasis and apodosis. The imperative and infinitive may be used in the apodosis. The future optative is not used in conditional sentences except in indirect discourse. The tenses in conditional sentences, except unreal conditions, have the same force as in simple sentences.

##### <a id="s2288"></a> §2288

Instead of a formal conditional sentence the two members may be simply coördinated, the protasis having the form of an independent clause.

<span lang="grc">σμῑκρὸν λαβὲ παράδειγμα, καὶ πάντα εἴσει ἃ βούλομαι</span> _take an insignificant example_, _and you will know what I mean_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Theaet. 154c">P. Th. 154c</cite>, <span lang="grc">πρά̄ττεταί τι τῶν ὑ̄μῖν δοκούντων συμφέρειν· ἄφωνος Αἰσχίνης</span> _something is going on_ (_of a kind_) _that seems to be to your advantage. Aeschines is dumb_. <cite data-ref="Dem. 18.198">D. 18.198</cite>. Cp. “Take with you this great truth, and you have the key to Paul's writings” (Channing); “Petition me, perhaps I may forgive” (Dryden). Cp. [1839](../20-the-verb-voices/08-imperative.md#s1839).
