# ADVERBIAL COMPLEX SENTENCES (2193-2487): PURPOSE CLAUSES (FINAL CLAUSES)

##### <a id="s2193"></a> §2193

Final clauses denote purpose and are introduced by <span lang="grc">ἵνα, ὅπως, ὡς</span> _in order that, that_ (Lat. _ut_); negative <span lang="grc">ἵνα μή, ὅπως μή, ὡς μή</span>, and <span lang="grc">μή</span> alone, _lest_ (Lat. _ne_).

**a.** Also by <span lang="grc"> **ὄφρα** </span>, strictly _while_, _until_, in Epic and Lyric; and <span lang="grc"> **ἕως** </span> in Epic ([2418](12-temporal-clauses.md#s2418)). <span lang="grc"> **ἵνα** </span> is the chief final conjunction in Aristophanes, Herodotus, Plato, and the orators. It is the only purely final conjunction in that it does not limit the idea of purpose by the idea of time (like <span lang="grc">ὄφρα</span> and <span lang="grc">ἕως</span>), or of manner (like <span lang="grc">ὅπως</span> and <span lang="grc">ὡς</span>); and therefore never takes <span lang="grc">ἄν</span> (<span lang="grc">κέν</span>), since the purpose is regarded as free from all conditions ([2201 b](#s2201)). <span lang="grc"> **ὅπως** </span> is the chief final conjunction in Thucydides, and in Xenophon (slightly more common than <span lang="grc">ἵνα</span>). <span lang="grc"> **ὡς** </span> often shows the original meaning _in which way, how_, _as_ (cp. [2578](../31-dependent-substantive-clauses-2574-2635.md#s2578), [2989](../36-particles/35-omegasigma-omegasigma.md#s2989)). It is rare in prose, except in Xenophon, and does not occur on inscriptions; rare in Aristophanes, but common in tragedy, especially in Euripides. <span lang="grc"> **μή** </span> is very rare in prose, except in Xenophon and Plato (<span lang="grc"> **μὴ οὐ** </span> is very rare in Homer and in Attic: <cite data-ref="Xen. Mem. 2.2.14">X. M. 2.2.14</cite>).

**b.** _In order that no one_ is <span lang="grc">ἵνα</span> (etc.) <span lang="grc">μηδείς</span> or <span lang="grc">μή τις</span>, _in order that . . . never_ is <span lang="grc">ἵνα</span> (etc.) <span lang="grc">μήποτε</span> or <span lang="grc">μή ποτε</span>, _and in order that . . . not_ is <span lang="grc">μηδέ</span> after <span lang="grc">μή</span>.

##### <a id="s2194"></a> §2194

Final clauses were developed from original coördination.

<span lang="grc">θάπτε με ὅττι τάχιστα· πύλᾱς Ἀίδᾱο περήσω</span> _bury me with all speed; let me pass the gates of Hades_ <cite data-ref="Hom. Il. 23.71"><span lang="grc">Ψ</span> 71</cite>, where we have a sentence of will added without any connective; and (negative) <span lang="grc">ἀπόστιχε μή τι νοήσῃ Ἣρη</span> _depart lest Hera observe aught_ A 522 (originally _let Hera not observe anything_, [1802](../20-the-verb-voices/07-the-moods-in-simple-sentences.md#s1802)). Even in Attic, where subordination is regular, the original form of coördination can be (theoretically) restored, as in <span lang="grc">καί σε πρὸς . . . θεῶν ἱκνοῦμαι μὴ προδοὺς ἡμᾶς γένῃ</span> _and I entreat thee by the gods | do not forsake us_ <cite data-ref="Soph. Aj. 588">S. Aj. 588</cite>. We can no longer trace the original coördination with <span lang="grc">ἵνα</span> and <span lang="grc">ὡς</span>.

##### <a id="s2195"></a> §2195

A final clause stands in apposition to <span lang="grc">τούτου ἕνεκα</span> or <span lang="grc">διὰ τοῦτο</span> expressed or understood. Thus, <span lang="grc">ἐκκλησίᾱν τούτου ἕνεκα ξυνήγαγον ὅπως ὑπομνήσω</span> _I have convened an assembly for this reason that I may remind_ you <cite data-ref="Thuc. 2.60">T. 2.60</cite>. Here <span lang="grc">τούτου ἕνεκα</span> might be omitted.

##### <a id="s2196"></a> §2196

The verb of a final clause stands in the subjunctive after an introductory primary tense, in the optative (sometimes in the subjunctive, [2197](#s2197)) after a secondary tense.

<span lang="grc">γράφω ἵνα ἐκμάθῃς</span> _I write_ (on this account) _that you may learn_. <!-- page 494 -->

<span lang="grc">γράφω ἵνα μὴ ἐκμάθῃς</span> _I write_ (on this account) _that you may not learn_.

<span lang="grc">ἔγραψα ἵνα ἐκμάθοις</span> (or <span lang="grc">ἐκμάθῃς</span>) _I wrote_ (on this account) _that you might learn_.

<span lang="grc">ἔγραψα ἵνα μὴ ἐκμάθοις</span> (or <span lang="grc">ἐκμάθῃς</span>) _I wrote_ (on this account) _that you might not learn_.

<span lang="grc">κατάμενε ἵνα καὶ περὶ σοῦ βουλευσώμεθα</span> _remain behind that we may consider your case also_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 6.6.28">X. A. 6.6.28</cite>, <span lang="grc">βασιλεὺς αἱρεῖται οὐχ ἵνα ἑαυτοῦ καλῶς ἐπιμελῆται, ἀλλ᾽ ἵνα καὶ οι᾽ ἑλόμενοι δι᾽ αὐτὸν εὖ πρά̄ττωσι</span> _a king is chosen, not that he may care for his own interest however nobly, but that those who choose him may prosper through him_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Mem. 3.2.3">X. M. 3.2.3</cite>, <span lang="grc">παρακαλεῖς ἰ̄ᾱτροὺς ὅπως μὴ ἀποθάνῃ</span> _you call in physicians in order that he may not die_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Mem. 2.10.2">X. M. 2.10.2</cite>. <span lang="grc">φύλακας συμπέμπει</span> (hist. pres., [1883](../20-the-verb-voices/10-the-tenses.md#s1883)) . . . <span lang="grc">ὅπως ἀπὸ τῶν δυσχωριῶν φυλάττοιεν αὐτόν</span> _he sent guards along in order that they might guard him from the rough parts of the country_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 1.4.7">X. C. 1.4.7</cite>. <span lang="grc">καὶ ἅμα ταῦτ᾽ εἰπὼν ἀνέστη ὡς μὴ μέλλοιτο ἀλλὰ περαίνοιτο τὰ δέοντα</span> _and with these words on his lips he stood up in order that what was needful might not be delayed but be done at once_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 3.1.47">X. A. 3.1.47</cite>, <span lang="grc">μὴ σπεῦδε πλουτεῖν μὴ ταχὺς πένης γε´νῃ</span> _haste not to be rich lest thou soon become poor_ Men. Sent. 358. For the optative after an optative, see [2186 c](../27-syntax-of-the-complex-sentence.md#s2186).

##### <a id="s2197"></a> §2197

After a secondary tense, the subjunctive may be used in place of the optative.

**a.** In the narration of past events, the subjunctive sets forth a person's previous purpose in the form in which he conceived his purpose. Thus (<span lang="grc">τὰ πλοῖα</span>) <span lang="grc">Ἀβροκόμᾱς . . . κατέκαυσεν ἵνα μὴ Κῦρος διαβῇ</span> _Abrocomas burned the boats in order that Cyrus might_ (may) _not cross_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 1.4.18">X. A. 1.4.18</cite>. Here the thought of A. was ‘I will burn the boats that Cyrus may not cross’ (<span lang="grc">ἵνα μὴ διαβῇ</span>), and is given in a kind of quotation.

N.—Thucydides and Herodotus prefer this vivid subjunctive; the poets, Plato, and Xenophon, the optative. In Demosthenes, the subjunctive and optative are equally common.

**b.** When the purpose (or its effect) is represented as still continuing in the present. See the example in 2195. This use is closely connected with **a.**

**c.** After <span lang="grc">**τί οὐ**, **τί οὖν οὐ**</span>, and the aorist indicative: <span lang="grc">τί οὖν οὐχὶ τὰ μὲν τείχη φυλακῇ ἐχυρὰ ἐποιήσαμεν ὅπως ἄν</span> ([2201](#s2201)) <span lang="grc">σοι σᾶ ᾖ κτλ.</span>; _why then do we not make your walls strong by a garrison that they may be safe for you_, etc.? <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 5.4.37">X. C. 5.4.37</cite>. Here the sentence with <span lang="grc">ἐποιήσαμεν</span> is practically equivalent to one with <span lang="grc">ποιήσωμεν</span>.

##### <a id="s2198"></a> §2198

The alternative construction of final clauses with subjunctive or optative is that of implicit indirect discourse ([2622](../31-dependent-substantive-clauses-2574-2635.md#s2622)). The subjunctive is always possible instead of the optative. Observe that the subjunctive for the optative is relatively past, since the leading verb is past.

##### <a id="s2199"></a> §2199

After a secondary tense both subjunctive and optative may be used in the same sentence.

<span lang="grc">ναῦς οἱ Κορίνθιοι . . . ἐπλήρουν ὅπως ναυμαχίᾱς τε ἀποπειρἁ̄σωσι . . ., καὶ τὰ̄ς ὁλκάδας αὐτῶν ἦσσον οι&lt;\*&gt; ἐν τῇ Ναυπάκτῳ Ἀθηναῖοι κωλύ̄οιεν ἀπαίρειν</span> _the Corinthians_ <!-- page 495 --> _manned . . . ships both to try a naval battle and that the Athenians at Naupactus might be less able to prevent their transports from putting out to sea_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 7.17">T. 7.17</cite>.

**a.** In some cases, especially when the subjunctive precedes, the subjunctive may express the immediate purpose, the realization of which is expected; while the optative expresses the less immediate purpose conceived as a consequence of the action of the subjunctive or as a mere possibility.

##### <a id="s2200"></a> §2200

The optative is very rare after a primary tense except when that tense implies a reference to the past as well as to the present.

<span lang="grc">οἴχονται ἵνα μὴ δοῖεν δίκην</span> _they have gone away that they might not suffer punishment_ <cite data-ref="Lys. 20.21">L. 20.21</cite>. Here <span lang="grc">οἴχονται</span> is practically equivalent to <span lang="grc">ἔφυγον</span>, and the optative <span lang="grc">δοῖεν</span> shows that the purpose was conceived in the past. On the optative (without <span lang="grc">ἄν</span>) by assimilation after an optative, see [2186 c](../27-syntax-of-the-complex-sentence.md#s2186).

##### <a id="s2201"></a> §2201

<span lang="grc">ὅπως</span> with the subjunctive sometimes takes <span lang="grc">ἄν</span> in positive clauses.

<span lang="grc">τοῦτ᾽ αὐτὸ νῦν δίδασχ᾽, ὅπως ἂν ἐκμάθω</span> _tell me now this very thing, that I may learn_ <cite data-ref="Soph. OC 575">S. O. C. 575</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἄξεις ἡμᾶς ὅπως ἂν εἰδῶμεν</span> _you will guide us in order that we may know_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 5.2.21">X. C. 5.2.21</cite>.

**a.** <span lang="grc"> **ὡς** </span> and <span lang="grc"> **ὄφρα** </span> with <span lang="grc">ἄν</span> or <span lang="grc">κέ</span> occur in poetry, especially in Homer. <span lang="grc"> **ὡς ἄν** </span> (first in Aeschylus) is very rare in Attic prose, but occurs eight times in Xenophon; as <span lang="grc">ὡς δ᾽ ἂν μάθῃς . . ., ἀντάκουσον</span> _but that you may learn, hear me in turn_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 2.5.16">X. A. 2.5.16</cite>. This use must not be confused with <span lang="grc">ὡς ἄν</span> in conditional relative clauses ([2565](../30-adjective-clauses-relative-clauses-2488-2573.md#s2565)).—<span lang="grc"> **ὅπως ἄν** </span> is more common than simple <span lang="grc">ὅπως</span> in Aristophanes and Plato, far less common in Xenophon. It is regular in official and legal language. —<span lang="grc"> **ἵνα ἄν** </span> is not final, but local (_wherever_, [2567](../30-adjective-clauses-relative-clauses-2488-2573.md#s2567)). The original meaning of <span lang="grc">ἵνα</span> was local and denoted the end to be reached.

**b.** <span lang="grc">ἄν</span> (<span lang="grc">κέ</span>) does not appreciably affect the meaning. Originally these particles seem to have had a limiting and conditional force ([1762](../20-the-verb-voices/06-the-particle-alphanu.md#s1762)): <span lang="grc">ὡς ἄν</span> _in whatever way_, _that so_ (cp. _so = in order that so_) as in “Teach me to die that so I may Rise glorious at the awful day” (Bishop Ken), and cp. <span lang="grc">ὡς</span> with <span lang="grc">ὅτῳ τρόπῳ</span> in <span lang="grc">ἱ̄κόμην τὸ Πῡθικὸν μαντεῖον, ὡς μάθοιμ᾽ ὅτῳ τρόπῳ πατρὶ δίκᾱς ἀροίμην</span> _I came to the Pythian shrine that I might learn in what way I might avenge my father_ <cite data-ref="Soph. El. 33">S. El. 33</cite>. With <span lang="grc">ὅπως ἄν</span> cp. <span lang="grc">ἐά̄ν πως</span>. Both <span lang="grc">ὅπως</span> and <span lang="grc">ὡς</span> were originally relative adverbs denoting _manner_ (_how_, cp. [2578](../31-dependent-substantive-clauses-2574-2635.md#s2578)), but when they became conjunctions (_in order that_), their limitation by <span lang="grc">ἄν</span> ceased to be felt.

##### <a id="s2202"></a> §2202

<span lang="grc">ὡς ἄν</span> and <span lang="grc">ὅπως ἄν</span> with the optative occur very rarely in Attic prose (in Xenophon especially), and more frequently after secondary than after primary tenses.

<span lang="grc">ἔδωκε χρήματα Ἀνταλκίδᾳ ὅπως ἂν πληρωθέντος ναυτικοῦ . . . οἵ τε Ἀθηναῖοι . . . μᾶλλον τῆς εἰρήνης προσδέοιντο</span> _he gave money to Antalcidas in order that, if a fleet were manned, the Athenians might be more disposed to peace_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Hell. 4.8.16">X. H. 4.8.16</cite>. <span lang="grc">ὡς ἄν</span> final must be distinguished from <span lang="grc">ὡς ἄν</span> consecutive ([2278](07-omegasigmatauepsilon-omegasigma-with-a-finite-verb.md#s2278)).

**a.** Homer has a few cases of <span lang="grc">ὡς ἄν</span> (<span lang="grc">κέ</span>) and <span lang="grc">ὄφρ᾽ ἄν</span> (<span lang="grc">κέ</span>); <span lang="grc">ἵνα κεν</span> once (<cite data-ref="Hom. Od. 12.156"><span lang="grc">μ</span> 156</cite>). Hdt. has <span lang="grc">ὡς ἄν, ὅκως ἄν</span> rarely.

**b.** After primary tenses the optative with <span lang="grc">ἄν</span> is certainly, after secondary tenses probably, _potential_. Its combination with the final conjunction produces <!-- page 496 --> a conditional relative clause in which the relative and interrogative force of <span lang="grc">ὅπως</span> and <span lang="grc">ὡς</span> comes to light. With <span lang="grc">ὅπως ἄν</span> the final force is stronger than with <span lang="grc">ὡς ἄν</span>. In the example quoted above, <span lang="grc">πληρωθέντος ναυτικοῦ</span> represents the protasis (<span lang="grc">εἰ ναυτικὸν πληρωθείη</span>) to <span lang="grc">ἂν προσδέοιντο</span>.

##### <a id="s2203"></a> §2203

The future indicative is used, especially in poetry, after <span lang="grc">ὅπως</span> (rarely after <span lang="grc">ὡς, ὄφρα</span>, and <span lang="grc">μή</span>) in the same sense as the subjunctive.

<span lang="grc">οὐδὲ δι᾽ ἓν ἄλλο τρέφονται ἢ ὅπως μαχοῦνται</span> _nor are they maintained for any other single purpose than for fighting_ (lit. _how they shall fight_) <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 2.1.21">X. C. 2.1.21</cite>, <span lang="grc">σῑγᾶθ᾽, ὅπως μὴ πεύσεταί</span> (fut.) <span lang="grc">τις . . . γλώσσης χάριν δὲ πάντ᾽ ἀπαγγείλῃ</span> (subj.) <span lang="grc">τάδε</span> _keep silence, lest some one hear and report all this for the sake of talk_ <cite data-ref="Aesch. Lib. 265">A. Ch. 265</cite>. In prose the future occurs with <span lang="grc">ὅπως</span> in Xenophon and Andocides. This usage is an extension of that after _verbs of effort_ ([2211](03-object-clauses.md#s2211)).

##### <a id="s2204"></a> §2204

The principal clause is sometimes omitted.

<span lang="grc">ἵν᾽ ἐκ τούτων ἄρξωμαι</span> _to begin with this_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 21.43">D. 21.43</cite>. <span lang="grc">ἵνα τί</span>, originally _to what end_ (cp. [946](../09-the-predicate.md#s946)), and <span lang="grc">ὡς τί</span> are also used colloquially: <span lang="grc">ἵνα τί ταῦτα λέγεις</span>; _why do you say this?_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Apol. 26d">P. A. 26d</cite>.

##### <a id="s2205"></a> §2205

By assimilation of mood, final clauses may take a past tense of the indicative without <span lang="grc">ἄν</span> ([2185 c](../27-syntax-of-the-complex-sentence.md#s2185)) or the optative without <span lang="grc">ἄν</span> ([2186 c](../27-syntax-of-the-complex-sentence.md#s2186).)

##### <a id="s2206"></a> §2206

**Equivalents of a Final Clause**.—The common methods of expressing purpose may be illustrated by the translations (in Attic) of _they sent a herald to announce:_

<span lang="grc">ἔπεμψαν κήρῡκα ἵνα</span> (<span lang="grc">ὅπως</span>) <span lang="grc">ἀπαγγέλλοιτο</span> ([2196](#s2196)).

<span lang="grc">ἔπεμψαν κήρῡκα ὅστις</span> (<span lang="grc">ὃς</span>) <span lang="grc">ἀπαγγελεῖται</span> ([2554](../30-adjective-clauses-relative-clauses-2488-2573.md#s2554)).

<span lang="grc">ἔπεμψαν κήρῡκα ἀπαγγελοῦντα</span> ([2065](../22-the-participle.md#s2065)), <span lang="grc">ἀπαγγέλλοντα</span> (rare, [2065](../22-the-participle.md#s2065)).

<span lang="grc">ἔπεμψαν κήρῡκα ὡς ἀπαγγελοῦντα</span> ([2086 c](../22-the-participle.md#s2086)).

<span lang="grc">ἔπεμψαν κήρῡκα ἀπαγγέλλειν</span> (rare in prose, [2009](../21-verbal-nouns.md#s2009)).

<span lang="grc">ἔπεμψαν κήρῡκα τοῦ ἀπαγγέλλειν</span> ([2032 e](../21-verbal-nouns.md#s2032), often in Thucydides).

<span lang="grc">ἔπεμψαν κήρῡκα ὑπὲρ</span> (<span lang="grc">ἕνεκα</span>) <span lang="grc">τοῦ ἀπαγγέλλειν</span> ([2032 g](../21-verbal-nouns.md#s2032)).

For <span lang="grc">ὥστε</span> denoting an intended result, see [2267](06-omegasigmatauepsilon-rarely-omegasigma-with-the-infinitive.md#s2267).
