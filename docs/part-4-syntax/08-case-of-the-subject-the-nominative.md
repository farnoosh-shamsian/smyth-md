# CASE OF THE SUBJECT: THE NOMINATIVE

##### <a id="s938"></a> §938

The nominative is the case of the subject; the oblique cases, with the exception of the adnominal genitive ([1290 f](18-the-cases/03-genitive.md#s1290)f.) and adnominal dative ([1502](18-the-cases/06-dative.md#s1502)), are complements of the predicate.

##### <a id="s939"></a> §939

The nominative is the case of the subject of a finite verb and of a predicate noun in agreement with the subject. <span lang="grc">Πρόξενος παρῆν</span> _Proxenus was present_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 1.2.3">X. A. 1.2.3</cite>, <span lang="grc">Κλέαρχος φυγὰς ἦν</span> _Clearchus was an exile_ 1. 1. 9.

**a.** On the nominative subject of the infinitive, see [1973](21-verbal-nouns.md#s1973); in exclamations, 1288.

##### <a id="s940"></a> §940

**Independent Nominative**.—The nominative may be used independently in citing the names of persons and things: <span lang="grc">προσείληφε τὴν τῶν πονηρῶν κοινὴν ἐπωνυμίᾱν σῡκοφάντης</span> _he received the common appellation of the vile_, i.e. ‘_informer_’ <cite data-ref="Aeschin. 2.99">Aes. 2.99</cite>, <span lang="grc">τὸ δ᾽ ὑ̄μεῖς ὅταν λέγω, λέγω τὴν πόλιν</span> _when I say You_, _I mean the State_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 18.88">D. 18.88</cite>. Cp. [908](02-syntax-of-the-simple-sentence.md#s908). (The accus. is also possible.) So in lists (cp. [904](01-definitions.md#s904) c): <span lang="grc">τίθημι δύο ποιητικῆς εἴδη· θείᾱ μὲν καὶ ἀνθρωπίνη</span> _I assume two kinds of poetry: the divine and the human_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Soph. 266d">P. Soph. 266d</cite>.

##### <a id="s941"></a> §941

A sentence may begin with the nominative as the subject of the thought in place of an oblique case: <span lang="grc">οἱ δὲ φίλοι, ἄ̄ν τις ἐπίστηται αὐτοῖς χρῆσθαι, τί φήσομεν αὐτοὺς εἶναι</span>; _but as for friends, if one knows how to treat them, what shall we call them?_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Ec. 1.14">X. O. 1.14</cite> (for <span lang="grc">τοὺς δὲ φίλους . . . τί φήσομεν εἶναι</span>).

**a.** On the nominative in suspense see under Anacoluthon (Index).

##### <a id="s942"></a> §942

In referring to himself in letters a man may use his own name in the nominative, either in apposition to the first person contained in the verb ([976](10-concord-of-subject-and-predicate.md#s976)), or as subject of a verb in the third person: <span lang="grc">Θεμιστοκλῆς ἥκω παρὰ σέ</span> _I_, _Themistocles_, _have come to you_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 1.137">T. 1.137</cite>, <span lang="grc">Ἀρταξέρξης νομίζει</span> _Artaxerxes thinks_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Hell. 5.1.31">X. H. 5.1.31</cite>.

**a.** A speaker referring to himself in the third person usually soon reverts to the first person (<cite data-ref="Dem. 18.79">D. 18.79</cite>).

##### <a id="s943"></a> §943

When there is no danger of obscurity, the subject may shift without warning: <span lang="grc">μίαν μὲν ναῦν λαμβάνουσιν, τὰ̄ς δ᾽ ἄλλᾱς οὐκ ἐδυνήθησαν, ἀλλ᾽ ἀποφεύγουσιν</span> _they captured one ship; the rest they were unable to capture; but they_ (the ships) _escaped_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 7.25">T. 7.25</cite>, <span lang="grc">τῶν νόμων αὐτῶν ἀκούετε τί κελεύουσι καὶ τί παραβεβήκᾱσιν</span> _hear what the laws themselves command and what transgressions they_ (my opponents) _have committed_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 59.115">D. 59.115</cite>.
