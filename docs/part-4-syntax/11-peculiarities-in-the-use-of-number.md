# PECULIARITIES IN THE USE OF NUMBER

##### <a id="s996"></a> §996

**Collective Singular**.—A noun in the singular may denote a number of persons or things: <span lang="grc">ὁ Μῆδος</span> _the Medes_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 1.69">T. 1.69</cite>, <span lang="grc">τὸ Ἑλληνικόν</span> _the Greeks_ 1. 1, <span lang="grc">τὸ βαρβαρικόν</span> _the barbarians_ 7. 29, <span lang="grc">ἡ πλίνθος</span> _the bricks_ 3. 20, <span lang="grc">ἵππον ἔχω εἰς χῑλίᾱν</span> _I have about a thousand horse_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 4.6.2">X. C. 4.6.2</cite>, <span lang="grc">μῡρίᾱ ἀσπίς</span> _ten thousand heavy armed_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 1.7.10">X. A. 1.7.10</cite>. On the plural verb with collectives, see [950](10-concord-of-subject-and-predicate.md#s950). Cp. [1024](14-adjectives.md#s1024), [1044](14-adjectives.md#s1044).

**a.** So with the neuter participle: <span lang="grc">τὸ μαχόμενον</span> almost = <span lang="grc">οἱ μαχόμενοι</span> _the combatants_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 4.96">T. 4.96</cite>.

**b.** The name of a nation with the article may denote one person as the representative (King, etc.) of a class: <span lang="grc">ὁ Μακεδών</span> _the Macedonian_ (Philip) <cite data-ref="Dem. 7.6">D. 7.6</cite>.

##### <a id="s997"></a> §997

The inhabitants of a place may be implied in the name of the place: <span lang="grc">Λέσβος ἀπέστη βουληθέντες καὶ πρὸ τοῦ πολέμου</span> _Lesbos revolted, having wished to do so even before the war_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 3.2">T. 3.2</cite>.

##### <a id="s998"></a> §998

**Distributive Singular**.—The singular of abstract nouns may be used distributively (rarely with concrete substantives): <span lang="grc">ὅσοι δίκαιοι ἐγένοντο ἐν τῷ ἑαυτῶν βίῳ</span> _all who proved themselves just in their lives_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Apol. 41a">P. A. 41a</cite>, <span lang="grc">διάφοροι τὸν τρόοπον</span> _different in character_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 8.96">T. 8.96</cite>. The distributive plural ([1004](#s1004)) is more common than the distributive singular: cp. <span lang="grc">νεᾱνίαι τὰ̄ς ὄψεις</span> _youths in appearance_ <cite data-ref="Lys. 10.29">L. 10.29</cite> with <span lang="grc">ἡδεῖς τὴν ὄψιν</span> _pleasing in appearance_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Rep. 452b">P. R. 452b</cite>.

##### <a id="s999"></a> §999

**Dual**.—The dual is chiefly employed of two persons or things which, by nature or association, form a pair: <span lang="grc">ὀφθαλμώ</span> _the eyes_ (_both eyes_), <span lang="grc">χεῖρε</span> _the hands_, <span lang="grc">ἵππω</span> _a span of horses_. The addition of <span lang="grc">ἄμφω</span> _both_ indicates that the two things belong together: <span lang="grc">δύο</span> emphasizes the number. Both <span lang="grc">ἄμφω</span> and <span lang="grc">δύο</span> were early used with the plural. The dual died out in the living speech of Attica by 300 B.C. Aeolic has no dual, and Ionic lost it very early. In Hom. the dual is used freely, and often in conjunction with the plural. <!-- page 270 -->

##### <a id="s1000"></a> §1000

**Plural**.—The plural of proper names, of materials, and of abstracts is used to denote a class. (1) _of proper names:_ <span lang="grc">Θησέες</span> _men like Theseus_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Theaet. 169b">P. Th. 169b</cite>. (2) _of materials:_ here the plural denotes the parts, the different kinds of a thing, a mass, etc.: <span lang="grc">τόξα</span> _bow_ <cite data-ref="Hdt. 3.78">Hdt. 3.78</cite>, <span lang="grc">πῡροί, κρῑθαί</span> _wheat_, _barley_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 4.5.26">X. A. 4.5.26</cite>, <span lang="grc">οἶνοι</span> _wines_ 4. 4. 9, <span lang="grc">κρέᾱ</span> _meat_ <cite data-ref="Aristoph. Frogs 553">Ar. Ran. 553</cite> (<span lang="grc">κρέας</span> _piece of meat_), <span lang="grc">ἥλιοι</span> _hot days_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 7.87">T. 7.87</cite>, <span lang="grc">ξύλα</span> _timber_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 7.25">T. 7.25</cite>. (3) _of abstracts:_ here the plural refers to the single kinds, cases, occasions, manifestations of the idea expressed by the abstract substantive; or is referred to several persons: <span lang="grc">ἀγνωμοσύναι</span> _misunderstandings_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 2.5.6">X. A. 2.5.6</cite>, <span lang="grc">θάλπη</span> _degrees of heat_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Mem. 1.4.13">X. M. 1.4.13</cite>. Used in the plural, abstract nouns may become concrete, as <span lang="grc">ταφαί</span> _funeral_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 2.34">T. 2.34</cite> (<span lang="grc">ταφή</span> _sepulture_), <span lang="grc">εὐφροσύναι</span> _good cheer_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 7.2.28">X. C. 7.2.28</cite> (<span lang="grc">εὐφροσύνη</span> _mirth_), <span lang="grc">χάριτες</span> _proofs of good will, presents_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 8.53">D. 8.53</cite>, <span lang="grc">εὔνοιαι</span> _cases of benevolence, presents_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 8.25">D. 8.25</cite>.

**a.** Many concrete substantives are commonly used only in the plural: <span lang="grc">πύλαι</span> _gate_, <span lang="grc">θύραι</span> _door_, <span lang="grc">τὰ Ὀλύμπια</span> _the Olympic festival;_ and in poetry <span lang="grc">δώματα</span> _house_, <span lang="grc">κλί̄μακες</span> _ladder_, <span lang="grc">λέκτρα</span> _bed;_ cp. [1006](#s1006).

**b.** The plural, especially in poetry, may correspond to the English indefinite singular: <span lang="grc">ἐπὶ ναυσί</span> _by ship_.

##### <a id="s1001"></a> §1001

In Homer the plural denotes the various forms in which a quality is manifested: <span lang="grc">τεκτοσύναι</span> _the arts of the carpenter_ <cite data-ref="Hom. Od. 5.250"><span lang="grc">ε</span> 250</cite>. In poetry, often of feelings, emotions, etc.: <span lang="grc">μανίαι</span> (attacks of) _madness_ <cite data-ref="Aesch. PB 879">A. Pr. 879</cite>.

##### <a id="s1002"></a> §1002

<span lang="grc">οὐδένες</span> (<span lang="grc">μηδένες</span>) denotes classes of men, states, nations (<cite data-ref="Dem. 5.15">D. 5.15</cite>).

##### <a id="s1003"></a> §1003

The neuter plural is often used even in reference to a single idea or thought in order to represent it in its entirety or in its details, as <span lang="grc">τὰ ἀληθῆ</span> _the truth_. This is very common with neuter pronouns: <span lang="grc">ἐχειρονόμουν δέ· ταῦτα γὰρ ἠπιστάμην</span> _but I waved my arms_, _for I knew how to do this_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Sym. 2">X. S. 2</cite>. 19, <span lang="grc">διὰ ταχέων</span> _quickly_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Apol. 32d">P. A. 32d</cite>.

**a.** Thucydides is fond of the neuter plural of verbal adjectives used impersonally: <span lang="grc">ἐψηφίσαντο πολεμητέα εἶναι</span> _they voted that it was necessary to make war_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 1.88">T. 1.88</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἀδύνατα ἦν</span> _it was impossible_ 4. 1. Cp. [1052](14-adjectives.md#s1052).

##### <a id="s1004"></a> §1004

**Distributive Plural**.—Abstract substantives are often used distributively in the plural: <span lang="grc">σῑγαὶ τῶν νεωτέρων παρὰ πρεσβυτέροις</span> _the silence of the younger men in the presence of their elders_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Rep. 425a">P. R. 425a</cite>.

##### <a id="s1005"></a> §1005

Names of towns and parts of the body are sometimes plural: <span lang="grc">Ἀθῆναι</span> _Athens_, <span lang="grc">Θῆβαι</span> _Thebes_, <span lang="grc">στήθη</span> and <span lang="grc">στέρνα</span> _breast_ (chiefly poetic). The name of the inhabitants is often used for the name of a city: <span lang="grc">Δελφοί</span> <cite data-ref="Dem. 5.25">D. 5.25</cite>.

##### <a id="s1006"></a> §1006

**Plural of Majesty** (poetic).—The plural may be used to lend dignity: <span lang="grc">θρόνοι</span> _throne_ <cite data-ref="Soph. Ant. 1041">S. Ant. 1041</cite>, <span lang="grc">σκῆπτρα</span> _scepter_ <cite data-ref="Aesch. Ag. 1265">A. Ag. 1265</cite>, <span lang="grc">δώματα</span> _dwelling_ <cite data-ref="Hom. Od. 5.6"><span lang="grc">ε</span> 6</cite>; <span lang="grc">παιδικά</span> _favourite_ in prose (only in the plural form).

##### <a id="s1007"></a> §1007

Here belongs the _allusive plural_ by which one person is alluded to in the plural number: <span lang="grc">δεσποτῶν θανάτοισι</span> _by the death of_ <!-- page 271 --> _our lord_ <cite data-ref="Aesch. Lib. 52">A. Ch. 52</cite>, <span lang="grc">παθοῦσα πρὸς τῶν φιλτάτων</span> _I_ (Clytaemnestra) _having suffered at the hands of my dearest ones_ (Orestes) <cite data-ref="Aesch. Eum. 100">A. Eum. 100</cite>.

##### <a id="s1008"></a> §1008

**Plural of Modesty**.—A speaker in referring to himself may use the first person plural as a modest form of statement. In prose, of an author: <span lang="grc">ἔννοιά ποθ᾽ ἡμῖν ἐγένετο</span> _the reflection once occurred to me_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 1.1.1">X. C. 1.1.1</cite>. In tragedy, often with interchange of plural and singular: <span lang="grc">εἰ κωλῡόμεσθα μὴ μαθεῖν ἃ βούλομαι</span> _if I_ (Creusa) _am prevented from learning what I wish_ <cite data-ref="Eur. Ion 391">E. Ion 391</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἱκετεύομεν ἀμφὶ σὰ̄ν γενειάδα . . . προσπίτνων</span> _I entreat thee, as I grasp thy beard_ <cite data-ref="Eur. Her. 1206">E. H. F. 1206</cite>. See [1009](#s1009).

##### <a id="s1009"></a> §1009

In tragedy, if a woman, speaking of herself, uses the plural verb ([1008](#s1008)), an adjective or participle, in agreement with the subject, is feminine singular or masculine plural: <span lang="grc">ἥλιον μαρτῡρόμεσθα, δρῶσ᾽ ἃ δρᾶν οὐ βούλομαι</span> _I call the sun to witness, that I am acting against my will_ <cite data-ref="Eur. Her. 858">E. H. F. 858</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἀρκοῦμεν ἡμεῖς οἱ προθνῄσκοντες σέθεν</span> _it is enough that I_ (Alcestis) _die in thy stead_ <cite data-ref="Eur. Alc. 383">E. Alc. 383</cite>.

##### <a id="s1010"></a> §1010

<span lang="grc">εἰπέ, φέρε, ἄγε</span> may be used as stereotyped formulas, without regard to the number of persons addressed: <span lang="grc">εἰπέ μοι, ὦ Σώκρατές τε καὶ ὑ̄μεῖς οἱ ἄλλοι</span> _tell me_, _Socrates and the rest of you_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Euthyd. 283b">P. Eu. 283b</cite>.

##### <a id="s1011"></a> §1011

One person may be addressed as the representative of two or more who are present, or of his family: <span lang="grc">Ἀντίνο᾽, οὔ πως ἔστιν . . . μεθ᾽ ὑ̄μῖν δαίνυσθαι</span> _Antinous_, _it is in no wise possible to feast with you_ <cite data-ref="Hom. Od. 2.310"><span lang="grc">β</span> 310</cite>, <span lang="grc">ὦ τέκνον, ἦ πάρεστον;</span> _my children, are ye here?_ <cite data-ref="Soph. OC 1102">S. O. C. 1102</cite>. So in dramatic poetry, the coryphaeus may be regarded as the representative of the whole chorus, as <span lang="grc">ὦ ξένοι, μή μ᾽ ἀνέρῃ τίς εἰμι</span> _strangers [^1] do not ask [^2] me who I am_ <cite data-ref="Soph. OC 207">S. O. C. 207</cite>.

[^1]: (addressed to the whole chorus)

[^2]: (the singular of the coryphaeus)

##### <a id="s1012"></a> §1012

Greek writers often shift from a particular to a general statement and _vice versa_, thus permitting a free transition from singular to plural, and from plural to singular: <span lang="grc">οὐδὲ τότε συγχαίρει ὁ τύραννος· ἐνδεεστέροις γὰρ οὖσι ταπεινοτέροις αὐτοῖς οἴονται χρῆσθαι</span> _not even then does the despot rejoice with the rest; for the more they are in want, the more submissive he thinks to find them_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Hiero 5.4">X. Hi. 5.4</cite>.
