# SYNTAX OF THE COMPOUND SENTENCE

##### <a id="s2162"></a> §2162

A compound sentence consists of two or more simple sentences, grammatically independent of one another and generally united by a coördinating conjunction. Thus, <span lang="grc">τῇ δὲ ὑστεραίᾳ ἐπορεύοντο διὰ τοῦ πεδίου ι καὶ ι Τισσαφέρνης εἵπετο</span> _but on the next day they proceeded through the plain and Tissaphernes kept following them_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 3.4.18">X. A. 3.4.18</cite>.

**a.** Abbreviated compound sentences, i.e. sentences containing a compound subject with a single verbal predicate or a single subject with a compound verbal predicate, are treated in this book as expanded simple sentences ([923](04-expansion-of-the-simple-sentence.md#s923), [924](04-expansion-of-the-simple-sentence.md#s924)).

##### <a id="s2163"></a> §2163

Greek has, among others, the following coördinating conjunctions, the uses of which in connecting sentences, clauses, phrases, and single words are described under Particles.

A. Copulative conjunctions: <span lang="grc">**τ**έ</span> (enclitic), <span lang="grc">**κ**αί</span> _and_, <span lang="grc">**τὲ**. **τέ**, **τὲ**</span> . . <!-- page 484 --> <span lang="grc">καί, καὶ . . . καί</span> _both . . . and_, <span lang="grc">οὐδέ</span> (<span lang="grc">μηδέ</span>) _and not, nor_, <span lang="grc">οὔτε . . . οὔτε</span> (<span lang="grc">μήτε . . . μήτε</span>) _neither . . . nor_.

B. Adversative conjunctions: <span lang="grc">ἀλλά</span> _but_, <span lang="grc">δέ</span> (postpositive, often with <span lang="grc">μέν</span> in the preceding clause) _but_, _and_, <span lang="grc">ἀτάρ</span> _but_, _yet_, _however_, <span lang="grc">μέντοι</span> (postpositive) _however_, _yet_, <span lang="grc">καίτοι</span> _and yet_.

C. Disjunctive conjunctions: <span lang="grc">ἤ</span> _or_, <span lang="grc">ἢ . . . ἤ</span> _either . . . or_, <span lang="grc">εἴτε . . . εἴτε</span> (without a verb) _either . . . or_.

D. Inferential conjunctions: <span lang="grc">ἄρα</span> _then_, _accordingly_, <span lang="grc">οὖν</span> _therefore_, _then_, <span lang="grc">νῦν</span> (in the poetic and enclitic forms <span lang="grc">νυν</span> and <span lang="grc">νῡν</span>) _then_, _therefore_, <span lang="grc">τοίνυν</span> _now_, _then_, <span lang="grc">τοιγάρ</span> (poetic), <span lang="grc">τοιγάρτοι, τοιγαροῦν</span> _so then, therefore_.

E. Causal conjunction: <span lang="grc">γάρ</span> _for_.

##### <a id="s2164"></a> §2164

Compound sentences are divided into Copulative, Adversative, Disjunctive, Inferential, and Causal sentences.

## ASYNDETON

##### <a id="s2165"></a> §2165

Two or more sentences (or words) independent in form and thought, but juxtaposed, i.e. coördinated without any connective, are _asyndetic_ (from <span lang="grc">ἀσύνδετον</span> _not bound together_), and such absence of connectives is called _asyndeton_.

**a.** The absence of connectives in a language so rich in means of coördination as is Greek is more striking than in other languages. _Grammatical_ asyndeton cannot always be separated from _rhetorical_ asyndeton. Grammatical asyndeton is the absence of a conjunction where a connective might have been used without marked influence on the character of the thought; as especially in explanatory sentences (often after a preparatory word, usually a demonstrative) which take up the matter just introduced; also where, in place of a conjunction, a resumptive word, such as <span lang="grc">οὗτος, τοιοῦτος, τοσοῦτος, ἐνταῦθα, οὕτω</span>, etc., is employed. Rhetorical asyndeton is the absence of a conjunction where the following sentence contains a distinct advance in the thought and not a mere formal explanation appended to the foregoing sentence. Rhetorical asyndeton generally expresses emotion of some sort, and is the mark of liveliness, rapidity, passion, or impressiveness, of thought, each idea being set forth separately and distinctly. Thus, <span lang="grc">οὐκ ἀσεβής; οὐκ ὠμός; οὐκ ἀκάθαρτος; οὐ σῡκοφάντης</span>; _is he not impious? is he not brutal? is he not impure? is he not a pettifogger?_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 25.63">D. 25.63</cite>.

##### <a id="s2166"></a> §2166

Asyndeton is frequent in rapid and lively descriptions.

<span lang="grc">συμβαλόντες τὰ̄ς ἀσπίδας ἐωθοῦντο, ἐμάχοντο, ἀπέκτεινον, ἀπέθνῃσκον</span> _interlocking their shields, they shoved, they fought, they slew, they were slain_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Hell. 4.3.19">X. H. 4.3.19</cite>, <span lang="grc">προσπεσόντες ἐμάχοντο, ἐώθουν ἐωθοῦντο, ἔπαιον ἐπαίοντο</span> _falling upon them, they fought; pushed_ (and) _were pushed; struck_ (and) _were struck_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 7.1.38">X. C. 7.1.38</cite>. Also with _anaphora_ ([2167 c](#s2167)), as in <span lang="grc">ἔχεις πόλιν, ἔχεις τριήρεις, ἔχεις χρήματα, ἔχεις ἄνδρας τοσούτους</span> _you have a city, you have triremes, you have money, you have so many men_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 7.1.21">X. A. 7.1.21</cite>. Cp. <cite data-ref="Thuc. 7.71">T. 7.71</cite>, <cite data-ref="Dem. 19.76">D. 19.76</cite>, 19. 215, <cite data-ref="Plat. Sym. 197d">P. S. 197d</cite>.

##### <a id="s2167"></a> §2167

Asyndeton also appears when the unconnected sentence

**a.** Summarizes the main contents, or expresses the result, of the preceding. <!-- page 485 --> Thus, <span lang="grc">πάντ᾽ ἔχεις λόγον</span> _you have the whole story_ <cite data-ref="Aesch. Ag. 582">A. Ag. 582</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἀκηκόατε, ἑωρἁ̄κατε, πεπόνθατε, ἔχετε· δικάζετε</span> _you have heard, you have seen, you have suffered, you have the evidence; pronounce your judgment_ <cite data-ref="Lys. 12.100">L. 12.100</cite>, <span lang="grc">φυλακῇ μέντοι πρὸ τῶν πυλῶν ἐντευξόμεθα· ἔστι γὰρ ἀεὶ τεταγμένη. οὐκ ἂν μέλλειν δέοι, ἔφη ὁ Κῦρος, ἀλλ᾽ ἰέναι</span> _however_, _we shall meet with a guard in front of the gates, for one is always stationed there_. _We must not delay, but advance, said Cyrus_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 7.5.25">X. C. 7.5.25</cite>. This is often the case when a demonstrative takes up the foregoing thought (as <span lang="grc">ἔδοξε ταῦτα</span> <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 1.3.20">X. A. 1.3.20</cite>) or continues the narrative, as in <span lang="grc">ἀκούσᾱσι τοῖς στρατηγοῖς ταῦτα ἔδοξε τὸ στράτευμα συναγαγεῖν</span> 4. 4. 19 (cp. [2061](22-the-participle.md#s2061)).

**b.** Expresses a reason or explains the preceding. Thus, <span lang="grc">μῑκρὸν δ᾽ ὕπνου λαχὼν εἶδεν ὄναρ· ἔδοξεν αὐτῷ . . . σκηπτὸς πεσεῖν κτλ</span>. _when he had snatched a little sleep_, _he saw a vision; a bolt of lightning seemed to him to fall_, etc. <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 3.1.11">X. A. 3.1.11</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἱκοῦ πρὸς οἴκους· πᾶς σε Καδμείων λεὼς καλεῖ</span> _come home; all the Cadmean folk calls thee_ <cite data-ref="Soph. OC 741">S. O. C. 741</cite>. Here <span lang="grc">γάρ</span> or <span lang="grc">ἄρα</span> might have been used. So often after a preparatory word (often a demonstrative); as <span lang="grc">ταὐτὸν δή μοι δοκεῖ τοῦτ᾽ ἄρα καὶ περὶ τὴν ψῡχὴν εἶναι· ἔνδηλα πάντα ἐστὶν ἐν τῇ ψῡχῇ ἐπειδὰν γυμνωθῇ τοῦ σώματος κτλ</span>. _now it seems to me that this is the same with regard to the soul too; everything in the soul is open to view when a_ man _is stripped of his body_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Gorg. 524d">P. G. 524d</cite>. <span lang="grc">ἑνὶ μόνῳ προέχουσιν οἱ ἱππεῖς ἡμᾶς· φεύγειν αὐτοῖς ἀσφαλέστερόν ἐστιν ἢ ἡμῖν</span> _in one point alone has the cavalry the advantage of us: it is safer for them to run away than for us_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 3.2.19">X. A. 3.2.19</cite>, and so when <span lang="grc">ὥσπερ</span> is followed by <span lang="grc">οὕτω καί</span> (<cite data-ref="Plat. Rep. 557c">P. R. 557c</cite>). Also when <span lang="grc">μέν γε . . . δέ</span> take up what precedes, as <span lang="grc">ὅμοιός γε Σόλων νομοθέτης καί Τῑμοκράτης· ὁ μέν γε . . . ὁ δέ</span> <cite data-ref="Dem. 24.106">D. 24.106</cite>. Furthermore after <span lang="grc">τεκμήριον δέ</span> ([994](10-concord-of-subject-and-predicate.md#s994)), as <cite data-ref="Thuc. 2.50">T. 2.50</cite>.

**c.** Repeats a significant word or phrase of the earlier sentence (_anaphora_). Thus, <span lang="grc">καὶ ὅτῳ δοκεῖ ταῦτα, ἀνατεινάτω τὴν χεῖρα· ἀνέτειναν ἅπαντες</span> _and let him who approves this, hold up his hand; they all held up their hands_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 3.2.33">X. A. 3.2.33</cite>. In poetry a thought is often repeated in a different form by means of a juxtaposed sentence (<cite data-ref="Soph. Trach. 1082">S. Tr. 1082</cite>).

**d.** Sets forth a contrast in thought to the preceding. This is commoner in poetry than in prose. Thus, <span lang="grc">μέλλοντα ταῦτα· τῶν προκειμένων τι χρὴ πρά̄σσειν</span> _this lies in the future; the present must be thy care_ <cite data-ref="Soph. Ant. 1334">S. Ant. 1334</cite>.

**e.** Introduces a new thought or indicates a change to a new form of expression. Thus, <span lang="grc">ἀλλ᾽ ἰτέον, ἔφη. πρῶτόν με ὑπομνήσατε ἃ ἐλέγετε</span> _but we must proceed_, _said he_. _First recall to my mind what you were saying_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Phaedo 91c">P. Ph. 91c</cite>.

**f.** Is introduced by a word stressed by emotion, as <span lang="grc">ταῦτα</span> <cite data-ref="Dem. 3.32">D. 3.32</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἐγώ</span> 4. 29.

On juxtaposition of participles, see [2147](22-the-participle.md#s2147).

## COÖRDINATION IN PLACE OF SUBORDINATION—PARATAXIS

##### <a id="s2168"></a> §2168

The term _parataxis_ (<span lang="grc">παράταξις</span> _arranging side by side_), as here employed, is restricted to the arrangement of two independent sentences side by side, though one is _in thought_ subordinate to the other.

**a.** In Greek, <span lang="grc">παράταξις</span> means simply _coördination_ in general, as <span lang="grc">ὑπόταξις</span> means _subordination_.

##### <a id="s2169"></a> §2169

In many cases parataxis is a common form of expression <!-- page 486 --> not only in the earlier language of Homer, but also in Attic prose and poetry.

So frequently in Attic prose with <span lang="grc">καί, τὲ . . . καί, ἅμα . . . καί, εὐθὺς . . . καί</span>, and with <span lang="grc">δέ</span> meaning _for_. Thus, <span lang="grc">ἤδη δὲ ἦν ὀψὲ . . . καὶ οἱ Κορίνθιοι πρύμναν ἐκρούοντο</span> _it was already late and_ (for _when_) _the Corinthians started to row astern_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 1.50">T. 1.50</cite>, <span lang="grc">καὶ ἤδη τε ἦν περὶ πλήθουσαν ἀγορὰ̄ν καὶ ἔρχονται . . . κήρῡκες</span> _and it was already about the time when the market-place fills and_ ( = _when_) _heralds arrived_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 2.1.7">X. A. 2.1.7</cite>, <span lang="grc">καὶ ἅμα ταῦτ᾽ ἔλεγε καὶ ἀπῄει</span> _and as soon as he said this, he departed_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Hell. 7.1.28">X. H. 7.1.28</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἐπίστασθε μόνοι τῶν Ἑλλήνων τοὺς ἀγαθοὺς ἄνδρας τῑμᾶν· εὑρήσετε δὲ . . . παρ᾽ ὑ̄μῖν στρατηγοὺς ἀγαθοὺς</span> (<span lang="grc">ἀνακειμένους</span>) _you alone among the Greeks know how to honour men of merit; for you will find statues of brave generals set up among you_ Lyc. 51. Cp. <span lang="grc">σκέψασθε δέ</span> <cite data-ref="Thuc. 1.143">T. 1.143</cite>.

**a.** Temporal conjunctions, as <span lang="grc">ἡνίκα</span>, are rarely used to introduce such clauses, which often indicate a sudden or decisive occurrence or simultaneous action.

**b.** Thucydides is especially fond of <span lang="grc">καί</span> or <span lang="grc">τέ</span> to coördinate two ideas, one of which is subordinate to the other.

##### <a id="s2170"></a> §2170

Parataxis often occurs when a thought naturally subordinate is made independent for the sake of emphasis or liveliness. Such rhetorical parataxis occurs chiefly in the orators and in Pindar. So especially when <span lang="grc">μέν</span> and <span lang="grc">δέ</span> are used to coördinate two contrasted clauses, the former of which is logically subordinate and inserted to heighten the force of the latter. Here English uses _whereas_, _while_. Thus, <span lang="grc">αἰσχρόν ἐστι, εἰ ἐγὼ μὲν τὰ ἔργα τῶν ὑπὲρ ὑ̄μῶν πόνων ὑπέμεινα, ὑ̄μεῖς δὲ μηδὲ τοὺς λόγους αὐτῶν ἀνέξεσθε</span> _it is a shame that, whereas I have undergone the toil of exertions in your cause, you will not endure even their recital_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 18.160">D. 18.160</cite>.

##### <a id="s2171"></a> §2171

There exist many traces in Greek of the use of the older coördination in place of which some form of subordination was adopted, either entirely or in part, in the later language.

**a.** Thus several relative pronouns and adverbs were originally demonstrative, and as such pointed either to the earlier or the later clause. So <span lang="grc">ὁ, ἡ, τό</span> ([1105](16-the-article-origin-and-development.md#s1105), cp. [1114](16-the-article-origin-and-development.md#s1114)): <span lang="grc">τεύχεα δ᾽ ἐξενάριζε, τά οἱ πόρε χάλκεος αρης</span> (H [146](../part-1-letters-sounds-syllables-accent/06-syllables-and-their-quantity.md#s146)) meant originally _he stripped him of his arms; these brazen Ares had given him_. <span lang="grc">τέως</span> _so long_ is properly demonstrative, but has acquired a relative function in <span lang="grc">καὶ τέως ἐστὶ καιρός, ἀντιλάβεσθε τῶν πρᾱγμάτων</span> _and while there is time, take our policy in hand_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 1.20">D. 1.20</cite>.

##### <a id="s2172"></a> §2172

Homer often places two thoughts in juxtaposition without any regard for logical connection. This is especially common with <span lang="grc">δέ, τέ, καί, αὐτάρ, ἀλλά</span>. Thus, <span lang="grc">πολὺς δ᾽ ὀρυμαγδὸς ἐπ᾽ αὐτῷ ἀνδρῶν ἠδὲ κυνῶν, ἀπό τέ σφισιν</span> (for <span lang="grc">οἶς</span>) <span lang="grc">ὕπνος ὄλωλεν</span> _and there is loud clamour around him of men and of dogs, and sleep is gone from them_ <cite data-ref="Hom. Il. 10.185"><span lang="grc">Κ</span> 185</cite>.

**a.** So also in clauses preceded by a relative word; as <span lang="grc">εἷος ὁ ταῦθ᾽ ὥρμαινε . . ., ἐκ δ᾽ Ἑλένη θαλάμοιο . . . ἤλυθεν</span> _while he was pondering on this_, (but) _Helen came forth from her chamber_ <cite data-ref="Hom. Od. 4.120"><span lang="grc">δ</span> 120</cite>, <span lang="grc">ὅς κε θεοῖς ἐπιπείθηται, μάλα τ᾽ ἔκλυον αὐτοῦ</span> _whoever obeys the gods_, (and) _him they hear_ <cite data-ref="Hom. Il. 1.218"><span lang="grc">Α</span> 218</cite>.

**b.** This use appears even in Attic prose; as <span lang="grc">οἰκου̂ͅσι δ᾽ ἐν μιᾷ τῶν νήσων οὐ</span> <!-- page 487 --> <span lang="grc">μεγάλῃ, καλεῖται δὲ</span> (for <span lang="grc">ἣ καλεῖται</span>) <span lang="grc">Λιπάρᾱ</span> _they dwell in one of the islands that is not large, and it_ (which) _is called Lipara_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 3.88">T. 3.88</cite>. Cp. also 2837.
