# DEPENDENT SUBSTANTIVE CLAUSES (2574-2635)

##### <a id="s2574"></a> §2574

A subordinate clause may play the part of a substantive in relation to the main clause. Such clauses are generally the object, sometimes the subject, of the verb of the main clause.

<span lang="grc">εἶπεν ὅτι οὐ πόλεμον ποιησόμενοι ἥκοιεν</span> _he said that they had not come to wage war_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 5.5.24">X. A. 5.5.24</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἔπρᾱσσον ὅπως τις βοήθεια ἥξει</span> _they were managing how some reinforcements should come_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 3.4">T. 3.4</cite>, <span lang="grc">δέδοικα μὴ . . . ἐπιλαθώμεθα τῆς οἴκαδε ὁδοῦ</span> _I am afraid lest we may forget the way home_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 3.2.25">X. A. 3.2.25</cite>; <span lang="grc">ἐλέγετο ὅτι . . . Πῶλος ὅσον οὐ παρείη</span> _it was said that Polus had all but arrived_ 7. 2. 5.

##### <a id="s2575"></a> §2575

There are four main divisions of substantive clauses.

\1. **Dependent Statements:** subordinate clauses stating that something _is;_ as <span lang="grc">λέγει ὡς οὐδέν ἐστιν ἀδικώτερον φήμης</span> _he says that nothing is more unjust than talk about a man's character_ <cite data-ref="Aeschin. 1.125">Aes. 1.125</cite>. <!-- page 581 -->

\2. **Dependent Clauses of _will_ or _desire:_** subordinate clauses denoting that something _should be_ or _should be done_. These clauses have been treated under the following divisions:

**a.** Dependent clauses after _verbs of effort_ ([2209](29-adverbial-complex-sentences-2193-2487/03-object-clauses.md#s2209)).

**b.** Dependent clauses after _verbs of fearing_ ([2221](29-adverbial-complex-sentences-2193-2487/03-object-clauses.md#s2221)).

N.—On dependent voluntative clauses with the accusative and infinitive (indirect petition), see [1991](21-verbal-nouns.md#s1991) ff.

\3. **Dependent Questions:** subordinate clauses asking a question; both parts of the sentence together forming a _statement;_ as <span lang="grc">ἠρώτων ὅ τι ἐστὶ τὸ πρᾶγμα</span> _I asked what the matter was_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 5.7.23">X. A. 5.7.23</cite>.

\4. **Dependent Exclamations:** subordinate clauses setting forth an exclamation; both parts of the sentence together forming a _statement;_ as <span lang="grc">διαθεώμενος αὐτῶν ὅσην μὲν χώρᾱν καὶ οἵᾱν ἔχοιεν</span> _observing how great the extent of their territory was and how excellent its quality_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 3.1.19">X. A. 3.1.19</cite>.

## DEPENDENT STATEMENTS

##### <a id="s2576"></a> §2576

Dependent statements, or subordinate clauses stating _that_ something _is_, are expressed in various ways:

\1. By an infinitive, with or without an accusative (explained in 1972 ff., 2016 ff.). Thus, <span lang="grc">νομίζω γὰρ ὑ̄μᾶς ἐμοὶ εἶναι καὶ πατρίδα καὶ φίλους</span> _for I think that you are both fatherland and friends to me_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 1.3.6">X. C. 1.3.6</cite>, <span lang="grc">οἶμαι εἰδέναι</span> _I think that I know_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Prot. 312c">P. Pr. 312c</cite>.

\2. By a participle, with or without an accusative (explained in 2106 ff.). Thus, <span lang="grc">οὐ γὰρ ᾔδεσαν αὐτὸν τεθνηκότα</span> _for they did not know that he was dead_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 1.10.16">X. A. 1.10.16</cite>, <span lang="grc">μέμνημαι ἀκούσᾱς</span> _I remember that I heard_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 1.6.6">X. C. 1.6.6</cite>.

\3. By <span lang="grc">ὅτι</span> or <span lang="grc">ὡς</span> (and some other conjunctions) with the indicative or optative. On this form of dependent statement see [2577](#s2577) ff., and under Indirect Discourse.

**a.** In any form of substantive clause the subject of the subordinate verb may be made the object of the principal verb ([2182](27-syntax-of-the-complex-sentence.md#s2182)).

**b.** A clause with <span lang="grc">ὅτι</span> (<span lang="grc">ὡς</span>) may precede the principal clause. Cp. [2586](#s2586).

### DEPENDENT STATEMENTS INTRODUCED BY <span lang="grc">ὅτι</span> OR <span lang="grc">ὡς</span>

##### <a id="s2577"></a> §2577

The conjunctions <span lang="grc">ὅτι</span> or <span lang="grc">ὡς</span> _that_ introduce dependent statements in the indicative and optative

After _verbs of saying, knowing, perceiving, showing_, etc.

After _verbs of emotion_ (_rejoicing, grieving, wondering_), etc.

Or such dependent statements contain an explanation of the main clause or of a word in that clause, no special verb introducing the conjunction.

<span lang="grc">τοῦτο ἄξιον ἐπαινεῖν τῶν ἀνδρῶν τῶν τότε ναυμαχησάντων, ὅτι τὸν . . . φόβον διέλῡσαν τῶν Ἑλλήνων</span> _it is right to praise this in the men who engaged in the sea-fight of those days_, (namely) _that they dispelled the fear felt by the Greeks_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Menex. 241b">P. Menex. 241b</cite>. <!-- page 582 -->

##### <a id="s2578"></a> §2578

The conjunctions introducing dependent statements are <span lang="grc">ὅτ&lt;\*&gt;</span> (Homeric also <span lang="grc">ὅττι, ὅ</span> and <span lang="grc">ὅτε</span>), <span lang="grc">ὡς, διότι, ὅπως</span> (rarely), <span lang="grc">οὕνεκα</span> and <span lang="grc">ὁθούνεκα</span> (both poetic).

**a. <span lang="grc">ὅτι</span>** meaning _that_ was originally, like Hom. <span lang="grc">ὅ</span>, perhaps an accusative of the inner object (cognate): <span lang="grc">ὁρῶ ς` νοσεῖς</span> lit. _I see what_ sickness _you are sick_ (= <span lang="grc">ἣν νόσον νοσεῖς</span>). But by the time of Homer both <span lang="grc">ὅ</span> and <span lang="grc">ὅτι</span> had become mere formal conjunctions. Hom. <span lang="grc">ὅτε</span> _that_ seems to be a weakened <span lang="grc">ὅτε</span> _when;_ but this is disputed.

**b. <span lang="grc">διότι</span>** originally = <span lang="grc">διὰ τοῦτο, ὅτι</span> _on account of this, that_ = _because_ (as <cite data-ref="Thuc. 1.52">T. 1.52</cite>); then = <span lang="grc">ὅτι</span> _that_ in Hdt. and in Attic after Isocrates, who uses <span lang="grc">διότι</span> for <span lang="grc">ὅτι</span> to avoid hiatus.

**c. <span lang="grc">ὡς</span>** strictly an old ablative of <span lang="grc">ὅς</span> ([2989](36-particles/35-omegasigma-omegasigma.md#s2989)) meaning _how_, _in what way_, as in exclamatory clauses and indirect questions. The meaning _how_ (cp. _how that_) may be seen in <span lang="grc">οἶδα γὰρ ὥς μοι ὀδώδυσται κλυτὸς ἐννοσίγαιος</span> _for I know how_ (_that_) _the famed earth-shaker has been wroth against me_ <cite data-ref="Hom. Od. 5.423"><span lang="grc">ε</span> 423</cite>, and also in Attic (<cite data-ref="Andoc. 2.14">And. 2.14</cite>; <cite data-ref="Isoc. 2.3">I. 2.3</cite>, 3. 10, 16. 11, 16. 15; <cite data-ref="Aeschin. 2.35">Aes. 2.35</cite>; <cite data-ref="Dem. 24.139">D. 24.139</cite>). The development of <span lang="grc">ὡς</span> _how_ to <span lang="grc">ὡς</span> _that_ followed from the use of <span lang="grc">ὡς</span> after verbs signifying _to see, perceive_, _know_, and the like. Cp. “he sayed how there was a knight.”

**d. <span lang="grc">ὅπως</span>** ([2929](36-particles/26-omicronpiomegasigma.md#s2929)) _that_ is common in Herodotus (<span lang="grc">ὅκως</span>), rare in Attic, most used in poetry and Xenophon. From its original use in indirect questions <span lang="grc">ὅπως</span> _how_ gradually acquired the meaning _that_. Thus, <span lang="grc">ἀλλ᾽ ὅπως μὲν . . . ἐγὼ ἄχθομαι ὑ̄μᾶς τρέφων, μηδ᾽ ὑπονοεῖτε</span> _do not even entertain the thought that I am annoyed at maintaining you_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 3.3.20">X. C. 3.3.20</cite>.

**e. <span lang="grc">οὕνεκα</span>** = <span lang="grc">οὖ ἕνεκα</span>, for <span lang="grc">τούτου ἕνεκα, ὅ</span>, properly causal: _on account of_ (_as regards_) _this_, _that_, and then = _that_, even in Homer (_Odyssey_ and <cite data-ref="Hom. Il. 11.21"><span lang="grc">Λ</span> 21</cite>) and later in poetry. Thus, <span lang="grc">ἐξάγγελλε . . . οὕνεκ᾽ Οἰδίπους τοιαῦτ᾽ ἔνειμε παισὶ τοῖς αὑτοῦ γέρᾱ</span> _announce that Oedipus has distributed such honours to his sons_ <cite data-ref="Soph. OC 1393">S. O. C. 1393</cite>.

**f. <span lang="grc">ὁθούνεκα</span>** = <span lang="grc">ὅτου ἕνεκα</span>, for <span lang="grc">τούτου ἕνεκα, ὅτι</span>; and then = _that_. It is found only in tragedy, as <span lang="grc">ἄγγελλε . . . ὁθούνεκα τέθνηκ᾽ Ὀρέστης</span> _report that Orestes is dead_ <cite data-ref="Soph. El. 47">S. El. 47</cite>.

##### <a id="s2579"></a> §2579

Some _verbs of saying_ are followed either by <span lang="grc">ὅτι</span> or <span lang="grc">ὡς</span> or by an infinitive ([2017](21-verbal-nouns.md#s2017)). In most cases the choice is optional with the writer. Affirmative clauses usually take the infinitive or <span lang="grc">ὅτι</span>; but <span lang="grc">ὡς</span> is apparently preferred to <span lang="grc">ὅτι</span> when a writer wishes to mark a statement as an opinion, a pretext, as untrue, and so when the main clause is negative, or when the subordinate clause is negative (or both are negative). Thus, <span lang="grc">νομίζουσιν οἱ ἐκείνῃ ἄνθρωποι . . . ὡς ὁ Ἥφαιστος χαλκεύει</span> _the local belief is that Hephaestus is working at his forge_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 3.88">T. 3.88</cite>, <span lang="grc">διαβαλὼν αὐτοὺς ὡς οὐδὲν ἀληθὲς ἐν νῷ ἔχουσιν</span> _slanderously attacking them on the score that their intentions were not sincere_ 5. 45, <span lang="grc">πολλάκις ἐθαύμασα τίσι ποτὲ λόγοις Ἀθηναίους ἔπεισαν οἱ γραψάμενοι Σωκράτην ὡς ἄξιος εἴη θανάτου</span> _I have often wondered with what possible arguments the accusers of Socrates succeeded in convincing the Athenians that he deserved death_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Mem. 1.1.1">X. M. 1.1.1</cite>, <span lang="grc">οὐ τοῦτο λέγω ὡς οὐ δεῖ ποτε καὶ ἐλά̄ττονι ἔτι μορίῳ ἰέναι</span> _I do not say_ (_this_) _that it is not ever necessary to attack_ the enemy _with a still smaller detachment_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 5.4.20">X. C. 5.4.20</cite>. <span lang="grc">ὅτι</span> may be used of an untrue statement designed to create belief (<cite data-ref="Soph. El. 43">S. El. 43</cite>). <!-- page 583 -->

**a.** Dependent statements in the optative in indirect discourse after _verbs of saying_ are chiefly post-Homeric.

##### <a id="s2580"></a> §2580

_Verbs of thinking_ almost always take the infinitive ([2018](21-verbal-nouns.md#s2018)) but <span lang="grc">ὡς</span> occurs; as with <span lang="grc">νομίζω</span> <cite data-ref="Thuc. 3.88">T. 3.88</cite> ([2579](#s2579)), <span lang="grc">ἐλπίζω</span> 5. 9, <span lang="grc">οἴομαι</span> <cite data-ref="Xen. Hell. 6.3.12">X. H. 6.3.12</cite>, <span lang="grc">ὑπολαμβάνω</span> <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 8.3.40">X. C. 8.3.40</cite>. <span lang="grc">ὅτι</span> is very rare (with <span lang="grc">οἴομαι</span> in <cite data-ref="Plat. Phaedo 87c">P. Ph. 87c</cite>). <span lang="grc">λογίζομαι</span> (<span lang="grc">ὅτι</span>) is a _verb of saying_.

**a.** <span lang="grc">μαρτυρῶ</span> with <span lang="grc">ὅτι</span> (<span lang="grc">ὡς</span>) expresses reality; with the infinitive it denotes uncertainty.

##### <a id="s2581"></a> §2581

Verbs of intellectual perception usually take <span lang="grc">ὅτι</span> (<span lang="grc">ὡς</span>); less often the participle, which is normal after verbs of physical perception. A verb of physical perception, if followed by <span lang="grc">ὅτι</span> (<span lang="grc">ὡς</span>), virtually becomes a verb of intellectual perception.

##### <a id="s2582"></a> §2582

Many verbs take <span lang="grc">ὅτι</span> (<span lang="grc">ὡς</span>) or the participle either in indirect discourse or not in indirect discourse ([2106](22-the-participle.md#s2106)-[2115](22-the-participle.md#s2115)). Here the construction with the finite verb is less dependent than that with the participle; but the meaning is essentially the same in Attic. Many verbs take <span lang="grc">ὅτι</span> (<span lang="grc">ὡς</span>), the infinitive, or the participle, often without great difference in meaning in Attic ([2123](22-the-participle.md#s2123)-[2145](22-the-participle.md#s2145)).

##### <a id="s2583"></a> §2583

<span lang="grc">ὅτι</span> (<span lang="grc">ὡς</span>), when separated from its clause by another clause, may be repeated. Thus, <span lang="grc">ἔλεγεν ὅτι, εἰ μὴ καταβήσονται . . . , ὅτι κατακαύσει . . . τὰ̄ς κώμᾱς</span> _he said that, if they did not descend, he would burn their villages to the ground_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 7.4.5">X. A. 7.4.5</cite>.

##### <a id="s2584"></a> §2584

The personal <span lang="grc"> **δῆλός εἰμι ὅτι, λανθάνω ὅτι** </span>, etc. are often used instead of the impersonal <span lang="grc">δῆλόν ἐστιν ὅτι, λανθάνει ὅτι</span>, etc. Thus, <span lang="grc">ὅτι πονηρότατοί εἰσιν οὐδὲ σὲ λανθάνουσιν</span> _not even you fail to perceive that they are the very worst_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Ec. 1.19">X. O. 1.19</cite>.

##### <a id="s2585"></a> §2585

<span lang="grc"> **δῆλον ὅτι** </span> (<span lang="grc">δηλονότι</span>) _evidently_, <span lang="grc"> **οἶδ᾽ ὅτι** </span> (<span lang="grc"> **εὖ οἶδ᾽ ὅτι** </span>) _surely_, <span lang="grc"> **εὖ ἴσθι ὅτι** </span> _be assured_ are so often used parenthetically and elliptically as to become mere formal expressions requiring no verb. <span lang="grc">ὅτι</span> here loses all conjunctive force. Thus, <span lang="grc">ἔχει δὴ οὑτωσὶ̄ δῆλον ὅτι τούτων πέρι</span> _the case then stands clearly thus about these matters_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Gorg. 487d">P. G. 487d</cite>, <span lang="grc">οὔτ᾽ ἂν ὑ̄μεῖς οἶδ᾽ ὅτι ἐπαύσασθε</span> _nor assuredly would you have ceased_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 6.29">D. 6.29</cite>, <span lang="grc">καὶ πάντων οἶδ᾽ ὅτι φησάντων γ᾽ ἄν</span> (for <span lang="grc">καὶ οἶδ᾽ ὅτι πάντες φήσαιέν γ᾽ ἄν</span>) _and all assuredly would say_ 9. 1.

**a.** Plato (_Sophistes_ and _Leges_) uses <span lang="grc">δῆλον</span> (<span lang="grc">ἐστίν</span>) <span lang="grc">ὡς</span> for <span lang="grc">δῆλον ὅτι</span>.

##### <a id="s2586"></a> §2586

<span lang="grc">ὅτι</span> (and by analogy <span lang="grc">ὡς</span>) are often attached loosely to the main clause with the meaning _as a proof_ (_in support_) _of the fact that_. Thus, <span lang="grc">ὅτι δ᾽ οὕτω ταῦτ᾽ ἔχει, λέγε μοι τὸ τοῦ Καλλισθένους ψήφισμα</span> _as a proof of the fact that this is so_, _read me the bill of Callisthenes_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 18.37">D. 18.37</cite>.

##### <a id="s2587"></a> §2587

_Verbs of emotion_ (_to rejoice, grieve_, _be angry, wonder_, etc.) take <span lang="grc">ὅτι</span> (<span lang="grc">ὡς</span>) with a finite verb (negative <span lang="grc">οὐ</span>), but more commonly the participle ([2100](22-the-participle.md#s2100)) when the subject is not changed.

**a.** Hom. prefers <span lang="grc">ὅτι, ὡς</span> to the participle or infinitive.

**b.** The accusative and infinitive with _verbs of emotion_ are rare; as with <span lang="grc">θαυμάζω</span> <cite data-ref="Eur. Alc. 1130">E. Alc. 1130</cite>. (<span lang="grc">θαυμάζω</span> may be followed by a dependent question: <cite data-ref="Dem. 37.44">D. 37.44</cite>).

**c.** On _verbs of emotion_ with <span lang="grc">εἰ</span> instead of <span lang="grc">ὅτι, ὡς</span> (negative, generally <span lang="grc">μή</span>), see [2247](29-adverbial-complex-sentences-2193-2487/04-causal-clauses.md#s2247). On the use in dependent exclamations, see [2687](34-exclamatory-sentences.md#s2687).

##### <a id="s2588"></a> §2588

<span lang="grc"> **μέμνημαι, οἶδα, ἀκούω** </span> and like verbs, may take <span lang="grc">ὅτε</span> instead of <span lang="grc">ὅτι</span> ([2395](29-adverbial-complex-sentences-2193-2487/12-temporal-clauses.md#s2395) A. N.). Cp., in Homer, <cite data-ref="Hom. Il. 21.396"><span lang="grc">Φ</span> 396</cite>, <cite data-ref="Hom. Od. 16.424"><span lang="grc">π</span> 424</cite>.

## INDIRECT DISCOURSE

<!-- page 584 -->

##### <a id="s2589"></a> §2589

The words or thoughts of a person may be quoted in direct or indirect form after verbs, or other expressions, of _saying_ or _thinking_.

**a.** In reporting a speech, in making a quotation, or in dialogue, a _verb of saying_ is often repeated (<cite data-ref="Plat. Prot. 310b">P. Pr. 310b</cite>, 345 c, <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 7.6.5">X. A. 7.6.5</cite>-6). So also in such cases as <span lang="grc">Πάνθεια εἶπεν, ἀλλὰ θάρρει, ἔφη, ὦ Κῦρε</span> <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 7.3.13">X. C. 7.3.13</cite>.

##### <a id="s2590"></a> §2590

(I) **Direct Discourse** (**Oratio Recta**).—In a _direct_ quotation the words or thoughts quoted are given at first hand in the exact form used by the original speaker or thinker.

<span lang="grc">Μεγαρέες ἔπεμπον ἐπὶ τοὺς στρατηγοὺς τῶν Ἑλλήνων κήρῡκα, ἀπικόμενος δὲ ὁ κῆρυξ πρὸς αὐτοὺς ἔλεγε τάδε· “Μεγαρέες λέγουσι· ‘ἡμεῖς, ἄνδρες σύμμαχοι, οὐ δυνατοί εἰμεν τὴν Περσέων ἵππον δέκεσθαι μοῦνοι</span>’” _the Megarians sent a herald to the generals of the Greeks_, _and on his arrival the herald spoke as follows:_ “_The Megarians say:_ ‘_we_, _oh allies, are not able to sustain the attack of the Persian cavalry by ourselves_’” <cite data-ref="Hdt. 9.21">Hdt. 9.21</cite>; and often in Hdt. (cp. [3](../part-1-letters-sounds-syllables-accent/01-the-letters.md#s3). 40, 3. 122, 5. 24, 7. 150, 8. [140](../part-1-letters-sounds-syllables-accent/06-syllables-and-their-quantity.md#s140)).

**a.** Direct quotation may, in prose, be introduced by <span lang="grc">ὅτι</span>, which has the value of quotation marks. Thus, <span lang="grc">οἱ δὲ εἶπον ὅτι ἱκανοί ἐσμεν</span> _but they said_ (_that_) “_we are ready_” <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 5.4.10">X. A. 5.4.10</cite>. So usually when the finite verb is omitted; as <span lang="grc">ἀπεκρί̄νατο ὅτι οὔ</span> _he answered_ (_that_) “_no_” 1. 6. 7. The use of direct speech introduced by <span lang="grc">ὅτι</span> is, in general, that of familiar style. The first example is <cite data-ref="Hdt. 2.115">Hdt. 2.115</cite>. <span lang="grc">ὡς</span> for <span lang="grc">ὅτι</span> is very rare (Dinarchus 1. 12, 1. [102](../part-1-letters-sounds-syllables-accent/03-euphony-of-consonants.md#s102)). Cp. “the emperor sends thee this word _that_, if thou love thy sons, let Marcus . . ., or any one of you, chop off your hand” Shakesp. _Tit. Andr._ 3. 1. 151.

##### <a id="s2591"></a> §2591

(II) **Indirect Discourse** (**Oratio Obliqua**). In an _indirect_ quotation the words or thoughts are given at second hand with certain modifications to indicate that the words or thoughts are reported.

**a.** The original form may be preserved except that there is a change from the first or second person to the third person: so <span lang="grc">πάντ᾽ ἐθέλει δόμεναι</span> H 391 reporting <span lang="grc">πάντ᾽ ἐθέλω δόμεναι</span> H 364. In such cases there is no grammatical dependence.

**b.** The narrator may report in dependent form the words or thoughts of a person from the point of view of that person. This is the common form of indirect discourse.

**c.** The narrator may report in dependent form the words or thoughts of a person from his own point of view. See [2624](#s2624).

##### <a id="s2592"></a> §2592

The constructions of indirect discourse are regulated by the character of the leading verb or expression.

**a.** _Verbs of saying_ take either <span lang="grc">ὅτι</span> or <span lang="grc">ὡς</span> and a finite verb or the infinitive ([2017](21-verbal-nouns.md#s2017), [2579](#s2579)).

**b.** Most _verbs of thinking_ and _believing_ take the infinitive ([2018](21-verbal-nouns.md#s2018), cp. [2580](#s2580)).

**c.** Most _verbs of knowing_, _perceiving_, _hearing_, _showing_ take the participle ([2106](22-the-participle.md#s2106), [2110](22-the-participle.md#s2110)), but admit the construction with <span lang="grc">ὅτι</span> or <span lang="grc">ὡς</span>. Some are followed by the infinitive ([2123 f](22-the-participle.md#s2123)f.). <!-- page 585 -->

**d.** On the construction of _verbs of hoping_, _promising_ , and _swearing_, see [1868](20-the-verb-voices/10-the-tenses.md#s1868), [1999](21-verbal-nouns.md#s1999), 2024.

##### <a id="s2593"></a> §2593

Indirect discourse is said to be _implied_ in subordinate clauses dependent on verbs which involve an idea of _saying_ or _thinking_ ([2622](#s2622)).

##### <a id="s2594"></a> §2594

A speaker may state his own words or thoughts in the form of indirect discourse. Cp. [2614](#s2614), [2615](#s2615), etc.

##### <a id="s2595"></a> §2595

Clauses standing in indirect discourse are substantive clauses, and usually _object_ of the leading verb; its _subject_, when that verb is passive or intransitive. The infinitive in substantive clauses after _verbs of saying_ and _thinking_ retains the _time_ of the corresponding finite verb of direct discourse.

##### <a id="s2596"></a> §2596

Indirect questions ([2677](33-indirect-dependent-questions.md#s2677)) have the constructions of indirect discourse.

## GENERAL PRINCIPLES OF INDIRECT DISCOURSE

##### <a id="s2597"></a> §2597

Simple and compound sentences, and **principal** clauses **of** complex sentences, introduced by <span lang="grc">ὅτι</span> or <span lang="grc">ὡς</span> are treated as follows:

##### <a id="s2598"></a> §2598

(I) After _primary_ tenses, the original mood and tense are retained, except that the person of the verb may be changed.

##### <a id="s2599"></a> §2599

(II) After _secondary_ tenses, primary tenses of the indicative and all subjunctives _may_ be changed to the same tense of the optative; but an indicative denoting unreality (with or without <span lang="grc">ἄν</span>) is retained. Imperfects and pluperfects are generally retained ([2623 b](#s2623)).

##### <a id="s2600"></a> §2600

The verb of simple and compound sentences, and of principal clauses of complex sentences, when introduced by a verb taking the infinitive or participle, passes into the infinitive or participle in the corresponding tense. <span lang="grc">ἄν</span> is kept, if it was used in the direct form.

##### <a id="s2601"></a> §2601

**Subordinate** clauses of complex sentences introduced by <span lang="grc">ὅτι</span> or <span lang="grc">ὡς</span> are treated as follows:

##### <a id="s2602"></a> §2602

(I) Subordinate clauses of a sentence introduced by a leading verb in a _primary_ tense, _must_ remain unchanged in mood and tense.

##### <a id="s2603"></a> §2603

(II) If subordinate clauses are introduced by a leading verb in a _secondary_ tense, all primary tenses of the indicative and all subjunctives (with or without <span lang="grc">ἄν</span>) _may_ be changed to the corresponding tenses of the optative without <span lang="grc">ἄν</span>. All secondary tenses of the indicative (with or without <span lang="grc">ἄν</span>) remain unchanged.

##### <a id="s2604"></a> §2604

Verbs standing in subordinate clauses of sentences introduced by a leading verb requiring the participle or the infinitive, follow the rules of 2602, 2603. <!-- page 586 -->

##### <a id="s2605"></a> §2605

The principal and subordinate clauses of the direct form retain the names _principal_ and _subordinate_ in indirect discourse though the whole clause in which they stand itself depends on the verb introducing the indirect discourse (the _leading_ verb).

##### <a id="s2606"></a> §2606

The change from direct to indirect discourse is almost always a change of _mood_, not of _tense_. The time of a participle introducing indirect discourse is determined by that of the leading verb. The _person_ of the verb is often changed.

##### <a id="s2607"></a> §2607

<span lang="grc">ἄν</span> of the direct form is retained in indirect discourse except when a dependent subjunctive with <span lang="grc">ἄν</span> becomes optative after a secondary tense. Here <span lang="grc">ἐά̄ν, ὅταν, ἐπειδάν, ἕως ἄν</span>, etc., become <span lang="grc">εἰ, ὅτε, ἐπειδή, ἕως</span>, etc.

##### <a id="s2608"></a> §2608

The same negative (<span lang="grc">οὐ</span> or <span lang="grc">μή</span>) used in the direct discourse is commonly kept in the indirect form. But in some cases with the infinitive and participle <span lang="grc">μή</span> takes the place of <span lang="grc">οὐ</span> ([2723 f](35-negative-sentences.md#s2723)f., 2730, [2737](35-negative-sentences.md#s2737)).

##### <a id="s2609"></a> §2609

No verb ever _becomes_ subjunctive by reason of indirect discourse. The subjunctive (with or without <span lang="grc">ἄν</span>) may, after a secondary tense, become optative without <span lang="grc">ἄν</span>.

##### <a id="s2610"></a> §2610

No verb can be changed to the optative in indirect discourse except after a secondary tense, and since, even after a secondary tense, indicatives or subjunctives may be retained for vividness, no verb _must_ become optative by reason of indirect discourse.

##### <a id="s2611"></a> §2611

All optatives with or without <span lang="grc">ἄν</span> in the direct form are retained (with or without <span lang="grc">ἄν</span>) in indirect discourse introduced by <span lang="grc">ὅτι</span> or <span lang="grc">ὡς</span>. After verbs requiring the participle or infinitive, such optatives in principal clauses become participles or infinitives (with or without <span lang="grc">ἄν</span>), but remain unchanged in subordinate clauses.

**a.** The optative in indirect discourse may represent either the indicative or the subjunctive after a secondary tense.

**b.** A present optative in indirect discourse may represent (1) the present indicative; (2) the imperfect ([2623 b](#s2623)) indicative; (3) the present subjunctive with or without <span lang="grc">ἄν</span>; (4) the present optative.

##### <a id="s2612"></a> §2612

The imperative is commonly replaced in indirect discourse by a periphrasis with <span lang="grc">χρῆναι</span>. Cp. [2633 b](#s2633).

##### <a id="s2613"></a> §2613

The retention of the mood of direct discourse, where either the direct or indirect form is possible, lies solely in the option of the writer or speaker. The vivid form reproduces the time and situation in which the quoted words were used. The vivid form is preferred by some writers, as Thucydides; the indirect form by others, as the orators, Plato, and Xenophon.

## SIMPLE SENTENCES IN INDIRECT DISCOURSE

<!-- page 587 -->

### 1. _Indicative and Optative after_ <span lang="grc"> **ὅτι** </span> _or_ <span lang="grc"> **ὡς** </span>

##### <a id="s2614"></a> §2614

After primary tenses, the verb of the direct form remains unchanged in mood and tense.

<span lang="grc">λέγει δ᾽ ὡς ὑβριστής εἰμι</span> _he says that I am an insolent person_ <cite data-ref="Lys. 24.15">L. 24.15</cite> ( = <span lang="grc">ὑβριστὴς εἶ</span>), <span lang="grc">ἀλλ᾽ ἐννοεῖν χρὴ τοῦτο μέν, γυναῖχ᾽ ὅτι ἔφῡμεν</span> _but we must remember on the one hand that we were born women_ <cite data-ref="Soph. Ant. 61">S. Ant. 61</cite>, <span lang="grc">οἶδ᾽ ὅτι οὐδ᾽ ἂν τοῦτό μοι ἐμέμφου</span> _I know that you would not blame me even for this_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Ec. 2.15">X. O. 2.15</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἀπεκρί̄νατο ὅτι οὐδὲν ἂν τούτων εἴποι</span> _he replied that he would say nothing of this_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 5.6.37">X. A. 5.6.37</cite> ( = <span lang="grc">ἂν εἴποιμι</span>).

##### <a id="s2615"></a> §2615

After secondary tenses, an indicative without <span lang="grc">ἄν</span> usually becomes optative, but may be retained unchanged. An indicative with <span lang="grc">ἄν</span> and an optative with <span lang="grc">ἄν</span> are retained.

**a. Optative for Indicative**.—<span lang="grc">ἔγνωσαν ὅτι κενὸς ὁ φόβος εἴη</span> _they recognized that their fear was groundless_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 2.2.21">X. A. 2.2.21</cite> ( = <span lang="grc">ἐστί</span>), <span lang="grc">ἔλεξαν ὅτι πέμψειε σφᾶς ὁ Ἰνδῶν βασιλεύς</span> _they said that the king of the Indians had sent them_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 2.4.7">X. C. 2.4.7</cite> ( = <span lang="grc">ἔπεμψεν ἡμᾶς</span>), <span lang="grc">ἠγγέλθη ὅτι ἡττημένοι εἶεν οἱ Λακεδαιμόνιοι . . . καὶ Πείσανδρος τεθναίη</span> _it was reported that the Lacedaemonians had been defeated and that Peisander was dead_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Hell. 4.3.10">X. H. 4.3.10</cite> ( = <span lang="grc">ἡττημένοι εἰσι</span> and <span lang="grc">τέθνηκε</span>).

N.—The first example of the optative in indirect discourse is later than Homer (_Hymn to Aphrodite_ [214](../part-2-inflection/04-substantives/01-first-declension-stems-in-alpha.md#s214)). Aeschylus has four cases. See [2624 c](#s2624).

**b. Direct Form Retained**.—<span lang="grc">διῆλθε λόγος ὅτι διώκει αὐτοὺς Κῦρος</span> _a report spread that Cyrus was pursuing them_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 1.4.7">X. A. 1.4.7</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἀποκρῑνάμενοι ὅτι πέμψουσι πρέσβεις, εὐθὺς ἀπήλλαξαν</span> _they withdrew immediately on answering that they would send envoys_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 1.90">T. 1.90</cite> ( = <span lang="grc">πέμψομεν</span>). See also [2623](#s2623), [2625](#s2625).

### 2. _Infinitive and Participle_

##### <a id="s2616"></a> §2616

The infinitive and participle are used in indirect discourse to represent the finite verb of direct discourse.

<span lang="grc">ὑπώπτευον ἐπὶ βασιλέᾱ ἰέναι</span> _they suspected that they were to go against the king_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 5.1.8">X. A. 5.1.8</cite> ( = <span lang="grc">ἴμεν</span>), <span lang="grc">ἔφη ἢ ἄξειν Λακεδαιμονίους ἢ αὐτοῦ ἀποκτενεῖν</span> _he said that he would either bring the Lacedaemonians or kill them on the spot_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 4.28">T. 4.28</cite> ( = <span lang="grc">ἄξω, ἀποκτενῶ</span>), <span lang="grc">οὐ γὰρ ᾔδεσαν αὐτὸν τεθνηκότα</span> _for they did not know that he was dead_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 1.10.16">X. A. 1.10.16</cite> ( = <span lang="grc">ὅτι τέθνηκε</span>).

For examples of the infinitive, see [1846](20-the-verb-voices/09-infinitive-and-participle-with-alphanu.md#s1846), [1848](20-the-verb-voices/09-infinitive-and-participle-with-alphanu.md#s1848), [1849](20-the-verb-voices/09-infinitive-and-participle-with-alphanu.md#s1849), [1867](20-the-verb-voices/10-the-tenses.md#s1867), 2022; for examples of the participle, see [1846](20-the-verb-voices/09-infinitive-and-participle-with-alphanu.md#s1846), [1848](20-the-verb-voices/09-infinitive-and-participle-with-alphanu.md#s1848), [1874](20-the-verb-voices/10-the-tenses.md#s1874), [2106](22-the-participle.md#s2106), 2112 b.

## COMPLEX SENTENCES IN INDIRECT DISCOURSE

##### <a id="s2617"></a> §2617

When a complex sentence passes into indirect discourse, its principal verb is treated like the verb of a simple sentence and stands either in a finite mood (after <span lang="grc">ὅτι</span> or <span lang="grc">ὡς</span>) or in the infinitive or in the participle. <!-- page 588 -->

##### <a id="s2618"></a> §2618

After primary tenses, all subordinate verbs retain the original mood and tense.

<span lang="grc">λέγουσιν ὡς, ἐπειδάν τις ἀγαθὸς ὤν τελευτήσῃ, μεγάλην μοῖραν καὶ τῑμὴν ἔχει</span> _they say that, when a good man dies, he enjoys great esteem and honour_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Crat. 398b">P. Crat. 398b</cite>, <span lang="grc">προλέγω ὅτι, ὁπότερ᾽ ἂν ἀποκρί̄νηται, ἐξελεγχθήσεται</span> _I tell you in advance that_, _whichever answer he makes, he will be confuted_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Euthyd. 275e">P. Eu. 275e</cite>, <span lang="grc">παράδειγμα σαφὲς καταστήσατε, δ̀ς ἂν ἀφιστῆται θανάτῳ ζημιωσόμενον</span> _give plain warning that whoever revolts shall be punished with death_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 3.40">T. 3.40</cite> ( = <span lang="grc">ὅτι ζημιω´σεται</span>).

##### <a id="s2619"></a> §2619

After secondary tenses, all subordinate verbs in the present, future, or perfect indicative, and all subjunctives, are usually changed to the corresponding tenses of the optative, or they are retained. Subjunctives with <span lang="grc">ἄν</span> lose <span lang="grc">ἄν</span> on passing into the optative.

**a. Optative for Indicative and Indicative Retained**.—<span lang="grc">εἶπε . . . ὅτι ἄνδρα ἄγοι . . . δ̀ν εἶρξαι δέοι</span> _he said that he was bringing a man whom it was necessary to lock up_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 1.8.12">X. A. 1.8.12</cite> ( = <span lang="grc">ἐστί</span>), <span lang="grc">εὖ δὲ εἰδέναι ἔφασαν ὅτι παρέσοιντο</span> _for they said that they knew well that they would come_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Hell. 6.5.19">X. H. 6.5.19</cite> ( = <span lang="grc">ἴσμεν ὅτι παρέσονται</span>), <span lang="grc">ἔλεγεν ὅτι ἕτοιμος εἴη ἡγεῖσθαι αὐτοῖς . . . εἰς τὸ Δέλτα . . ., ἔνθα πολλὰ κἀ̄γαθὰ λήψοιντο</span> _he said that he was ready to be their leader to the Delta_, _where they would obtain an abundance of good things_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 7.1.33">X. A. 7.1.33</cite> ( = <span lang="grc">ἕτοιμός εἰμι, λήψεσθε</span>), <span lang="grc">ἔλεγον ὅτι . . . ἥκοιεν ἡγεμόνας ἔχοντες, οἳ αὐτούς, ἐὰ̄ν σπονδαὶ γένωνται, ἄξουσιν ἔνθεν ἕξουσι τὰ ἐπιτήδεια</span> _they said that they had come with guides who would lead them, should a truce be made_, to a place _where they would get their supplies_ 2. 3. 6 ( = <span lang="grc">ἥκομεν, ὑ̄μᾶς, ἕξετε</span>), <span lang="grc">ἀγαπήσειν με ἔφασκεν, εἰ τὸ σῶμα σώσω</span> _he said I might think myself well off if I saved my life_ <cite data-ref="Lys. 12.11">L. 12.11</cite> ( = <span lang="grc">ἀγαπήσεις, εἰ σώσεις</span>).

N.—Except in the future the change to the optative of the indicative after <span lang="grc">εἰ</span> is rare: as <span lang="grc">προσῆλθον λέγων ὅτι . . . ἕτοιμός εἰμι, εἴ τινα βούλοιτο</span> ( = <span lang="grc">βούλει</span>), <span lang="grc">παραδοῦναι βασανίζειν</span> _I went and said that I was ready to give up_ the slaves _to be tortured_, _if he wished any one of them_ <cite data-ref="Lys. 7.34">L. 7.34</cite>, <span lang="grc">εἶπεν ὅτι Δέξιππον μὲν οὐκ ἐπαινοίη, εἰ ταῦτα πεποιηκὼς εἴη</span> _he said that he did not commend Dexippus_, _if he had done this_ <cite data-ref="Xen. **b. Optative for Subjunctive and Subjunctive Retained**.—<span lang="grc">εἶπεν ὅτι οἰμώξοιτο, εἰ μὴ σιωπήσειεν</span> _he said that he would smart for it unless he kept quiet_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Subordinate verbs in the imperfect, aorist (but see [2623 c](#s2623), N. 3), or pluperfect indicative, and all optatives, remain unchanged.

## IMPLIED INDIRECT DISCOURSE

##### <a id="s2622"></a> §2622

Indirect discourse is _implied_ in the case of any subordinate clause, which, though not depending formally on a _verb of saying_ or _thinking_, contains the _past thought of another person_ and not a statement of the writer or speaker. Implied indirect discourse appears only after secondary tenses, and in various kinds of dependent clauses.

**a.** Conditional clauses, the conclusion being implied in the leading verb. Thus, after a _verb of emotion_, <span lang="grc">οἱ δ᾽ ᾤκτῑρον εἰ ἁλώσοιντο</span> _others pitied them if they should be captured_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 1.4.7">X. A. 1.4.7</cite>. The original form was ‘we pity them thinking what they will suffer <span lang="grc">εἰ ἁλώσονται</span> _if they shall be captured_.’ In other <span lang="grc">εἰ</span> clauses, as <span lang="grc">τὰ χρήματα τῷ δήμῳ ἔδωκεν, εἴ πως τελευτήσειεν ἄπαις</span> _he gave his property to the people in case he died childless_ <cite data-ref="Andoc. 4.15">And. 4.15</cite> (i.e. that the people might have it, in case he should die: direct <span lang="grc">ἐὰ̄ν τελευτήσω</span>, and here <span lang="grc">ἐὰ̄ν τελευτήσῃ</span> might have been used). <!-- page 590 -->

**b.** Temporal clauses implying purpose, expectation, or the like (cp. [2420](29-adverbial-complex-sentences-2193-2487/12-temporal-clauses.md#s2420)). Thus, <span lang="grc">σπονδὰ̄ς ἐποιήσαντο, ἕως ἀπαγγελθείη τὰ λεχθέντα</span> _they made a truce_ (which they agreed should continue) _until what had been said should have been reported_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Hell. 3.2.20">X. H. 3.2.20</cite> (<span lang="grc">ἕως ἂν ἀπαγγελθῇ</span> would be the direct form). Cp. <span lang="grc">ἕως δ᾽ ἂν ταῦτα διαπρά̄ξωνται, φυλακὴν . . . κατέλιπε</span> _he left behind a guard_ (which he intended should remain) _until they should settle these matters_ 5. 3. 25.

**c.** Causal clauses. See [2242](29-adverbial-complex-sentences-2193-2487/04-causal-clauses.md#s2242).

**d.** Ordinary relative clauses. Thus, <span lang="grc">εἴρετο παῖδα, τὸν Εὐάδνᾱ τέκοι</span> _he asked for the child which Evadna had borne_ Pindar, Ol. 6. 49. Here relative and interrogative are not sharply distinguished.

**e.** Clauses depending on an infinitive especially when introduced by a _verb of will_ or _desire_, e.g. _command_, _advise_, _plan_, _ask_, _wish_ ([1991](21-verbal-nouns.md#s1991), [1992](21-verbal-nouns.md#s1992)). Here the infinitive expressing command, warning, wish, is not itself in indirect discourse. The negative is <span lang="grc">μή</span>. Thus, <span lang="grc">ἀφικνοῦνται</span> (historical present) <span lang="grc">ὡς Σιτάλκην . . . βουλόμενοι πεῖσαι αὐτόν, εἰ δύναιντο, . . . στρατεῦσαι ἐπὶ τὴν Ποτει´δαιαν</span> _they came to Sitalces with the desire of persuading him_ (_if they could_) _to make an expedition against Potidaea_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 2.67">T. 2.67</cite> ( = <span lang="grc">ἐὰ̄ν δυνώμεθα</span>), cp. [2633 a](#s2633).

**f.** Clauses of purpose and object clauses after _verbs of effort_ admit the alternative constructions of indirect discourse.

## REMARKS ON THE CONSTRUCTIONS OF INDIRECT DISCOURSE

##### <a id="s2623"></a> §2623

**Past Tenses in Indirect Discourse**.—The following rules govern _past tenses_ in indirect discourse.

**a.** The potential indicative with <span lang="grc">ἄν</span>, the indicative in a condition denoting unreality with <span lang="grc">ἄν</span> or without <span lang="grc">ἄν</span> (as <span lang="grc">ἐχρῆν, ἔδει</span>, etc.), always remain unchanged in order to prevent confusion with the optative of the direct form.

<span lang="grc">ἀπελογοῦντο ὡς οὐκ ἄν ποτε οὕτω μῶροι ἦσαν . . . εἰ ᾔδεσαν</span> _they pleaded that they never would have been so foolish, if they had known_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Hell. 5.4.22">X. H. 5.4.22</cite> ( = <span lang="grc">οὐκ ἂν ἦμεν, εἰ ᾖσμεν</span>), (<span lang="grc">ἔλεγεν</span>) <span lang="grc">ὅτι κρεῖττον ἦν αὐτῷ τότε ἀποθανεῖν</span> _he said that it would have been better for him to die then_ <cite data-ref="Lys. 10.25">L. 10.25</cite> ( = <span lang="grc">κρεῖττον ἦν μοι</span>).

**b.** The imperfect and pluperfect in simple sentences usually remain unchanged after secondary tenses to prevent ambiguity; but when there is no doubt that a past tense stood in the direct form, the imperfect passes into the present optative, the pluperfect into the perfect optative. In subordinate clauses both tenses are retained unaltered.

<span lang="grc">ἤκουσεν ὅτι πολλάκις πρὸς τὸν Ἰνδὸν οἱ Χαλδαῖοι ἐπορεύοντο</span> _he heard that the Chaldaeans often went to the Indian king_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 3.2.27">X. C. 3.2.27</cite>, <span lang="grc">εἶχε γὰρ λέγειν καὶ ὅτι μόνοι τῶν Ἑλλήνων βασιλεῖ συνεμάχοντο ἐν Πλαταιαῖς, καὶ ὅτι ὕστερον οὐδεπώποτε στρατεύσαιντο</span> (cp. c) <span lang="grc">ἐπὶ βασιλέᾱ</span> _for he was able to say both that alone of the Greeks they had fought on the side of the king at Plataea and that later they had never at any time taken the field against the king_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Hell. 7.1.34">X. H. 7.1.34</cite> ( = <span lang="grc">συνεμαχόμεθα, ἐστρατευσάμεθα</span>), <span lang="grc">τὰ πεπρᾱγμένα διηγοῦντο, ὅτι αὐτοὶ μὲν . . . πλέοιεν, τὴν δὲ ἀναίρεσιν τῶν ναυᾱγῶν προστάξαιεν</span> _they related what had occurred to the effect that they were themselves sailing_ against the enemy _and that they had given orāers for the rescue of the men on the wrecks_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Hell. 1.7.5">X. H. 1.7.5</cite> ( = <span lang="grc">ἐπλέομεν, προσετάξαμεν</span>). <!-- page 591 -->

N.—The change to the optative is not made when the time of the action of imperfect (and pluperfect) is earlier than that of a coőrdinated verb in the same quoted sentence; as <span lang="grc">ἔλεγέν τ᾽ ὡς φιλαθήναιος ἦν καὶ τἀ̄ν Σάμῳ πρῶτος κατείποι</span> _he said that he both had been a lover of Athens and that_ (afterwards) _he was the first to tell what had happened at Samos_ <cite data-ref="Aristoph. Wasps 282">Ar. Vesp. 282</cite>.

**c.** The aorist indicative without <span lang="grc">ἄν</span> in a simple sentence or in a principal clause may be changed to the aorist optative after a secondary tense; but in subordinate clauses (except those denoting cause, N. 3) it remains unchanged to avoid ambiguity with the aorist optative, which usually represents the aorist subjunctive.

<span lang="grc">ἀπεκρῑνάμην αὐτῷ ὅτι . . . οὐ λάβοιμι</span> _I answered him that I did not take_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 50.36">D. 50.36</cite> ( = <span lang="grc">οὐκ ἔλαβον</span>), <span lang="grc">τοῖς ἰδίοις χρήσεσθαι ἔφη, ἃ ὁ πατὴρ αὐτῷ ἔδωκεν</span> _he said that he would use his own money that his father had given him_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Hell. 1.5.3">X. H. 1.5.3</cite> ( = <span lang="grc">χρήσομαι, ἔδωκεν</span>).

N. 1.—The retention of the aorist indicative is here the essential point of difference between subordinate clauses and principal clauses or simple sentences.

N. 2.—In a subordinate clause the time of the aorist usually expresses an action prior to that of the leading verb.

N. 3.—In causal clauses with <span lang="grc">ὅτι</span> or <span lang="grc">ὡς</span> a dependent aorist indicative may become aorist optative; as <span lang="grc">εἶχε γὰρ λέγειν . . . ὡς Λακεδαιμόνιοι διὰ τοῦτο πολεμήσειαν αὐτοῖς, ὅτι οὐκ ἐθελήσαιεν μετ᾽ Ἀ̄γησιλά̄ου ἐλθεῖν ἐπ᾽ αὐτόν</span> _for he was able to say that the Lacedaemonians had gone to war with them_ (the Thebans) _for the reason that they_ (the Thebans) _had not been willing to attack him_ (the Persian king) _in company with Agesilaus_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Hell. 7.1.34">X. H. 7.1.34</cite> (direct <span lang="grc">ἐπολέμησαν ἡμῖν, ὅτι οὐκ ἠθελήσαμεν</span>). Rarely in temporal clauses with <span lang="grc">ἐπεί</span> (<cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 5.3.26">X. C. 5.3.26</cite>).

##### <a id="s2624"></a> §2624

**Inserted Statement of Fact**.—When the present or perfect indicative would have stood in the direct discourse, a past tense of historical narration is often used as a _statement of fact_ by the writer from his own point of view, though the rest of the sentence may be given in indirect discourse after a secondary tense from the point of view of the subject of the leading verb.

<span lang="grc">ᾔδει ὅτι οὐχ οἷόν τ᾽ ἦν αὐτῇ σωθῆναι</span> _she knew that it was not possible for her to be saved_ <cite data-ref="Antiph. 1.8">Ant. 1.8</cite> ( = <span lang="grc">οὐχ οἷόν τ᾽ ἐστὶ ἐμοὶ σωθῆναι</span>. With <span lang="grc">ἦν</span> the sentence virtually has the force of <span lang="grc">οὐχ οἷόν τ᾽ ἧν σωθῆναι καὶ ᾔδει</span> _she could not be saved and she knew it_). So <span lang="grc">ἔλεγον οὐ καλῶς τὴν Ἑλλάσα ἐλευθεροῦν αὐτόν, εἰ ἄνδρας διέφθειρεν</span> _they said that he was not freeing Greece in the right way if he put men to death_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 3.32">T. 3.32</cite> ( = <span lang="grc">ἐλευθεροῖς, διαφθείρεις</span>), <span lang="grc">τοὺς φυγάδας ἐκέλευσε σὺν αὐτῷ στρατεύεσθαι, ὑποσχόμενος αὐτοῖς, εἰ καλῶς καταπρά̄ξειεν ἐφ᾽ ἃ ἐστρατεύετο, μὴ πρόσθεν παύσεσθαι πρὶν αὐτοὺς καταγάγοι οἴκαδε</span> _he urged the exiles to make the expedition with him_, _promising them that, if he should succeed in accomplishing the purposes of his campaign_, _he would not cease until he had brought them back to their homes_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 1.2.2">X. A. 1.2.2</cite> ( = <span lang="grc">ἢν καταπρά̄ξω ἐφ᾽ ἃ στρατεύομαι, οὐ παύσομαι πρὶν ἂν καταγάγω</span>), <span lang="grc">ἀποθανὼν ἐδήλωσεν ὅτι οὐκ ἀληθῆ ταῦτα ἦν</span> _he showed by his death that this was not true_ <cite data-ref="Lys. 19.52">L. 19.52</cite> ( = <span lang="grc">ἐστί</span>), <span lang="grc">ἔφη εἶναι παρ᾽ ἑαυτῷ ὅσον μὴ ἦν ἀνηλωμένον</span> _he said that he had in his possession all that had not been expended_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 48.16">D. 48.16</cite> ( = <span lang="grc">παρ᾽ ἐμοί ἐστιν ὅσον μὴ ἔστιν ἀνηλωμένον</span>), <span lang="grc">ἐν πολλῇ δὴ ἀπορίᾳ ἦσαν οἱ Ἕλληνες</span>, <!-- page 592 --> <span lang="grc">ἐννοούμενοι μὲν ὅτι ἐπὶ ταῖς βασιλέως θύραις ἦσαν</span> _the Greeks were accordingly in great perplexity on reflecting that they were at the king's gates_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 3.1.2">X. A. 3.1.2</cite> (i.e. they were there in fact and they knew it).

**a.** The use of past tenses of historical narration instead of present tenses of direct discourse occurs, in simple sentences, especially after _verbs of knowing_, _perceiving_, _showing_, and _verbs of emotion_ (rarely after _verbs of saying_ w. <span lang="grc">ὅτι</span>).

**b.** Such inserted statements of fact are often difficult to distinguish from indicatives in indirect discourse; and the two forms of expression may occur in the same sentence (<cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 4.2.35">X. C. 4.2.35</cite>-[36](../part-1-letters-sounds-syllables-accent/01-the-letters.md#s36)). The common explanation of the use of the imperfect and pluperfect for the present and perfect is that Greek had the same assimilation of tense as English.

**c.** Except in indirect questions, the optative of indirect discourse is unknown to Homer. (<span lang="grc">εἰπεῖν ὡς ἔλθοι ω</span> 237 may be considered as interrogative.) After primary or secondary tenses Homer employs, in the dependent clause, the same past tense that would have been used in an independent clause, from the point of view of the speaker, and not the tense which would have been used in direct discourse from the point of view of the subject of the main clause. Thus, <span lang="grc">γίγνωσκον δ̀</span> ( = <span lang="grc">ὅτι</span>) <span lang="grc">δὴ κακὰ μήδετο</span> _I knew that he was planning evil_ <cite data-ref="Hom. Od. 3.166"><span lang="grc">γ</span> 166</cite> (_i.e_ <span lang="grc">κακὰ ἐμήδετο καὶ ἐγίγνωσκον</span> _he was planning evil and I knew it_). In Attic we should commonly have <span lang="grc">μήδεται</span> or <span lang="grc">μήδοιτο</span>. After secondary tenses the future is usually expressed in Homer by <span lang="grc">ἔμελλον</span> and the infinitive, as <span lang="grc">οὐδὲ τὸ ᾔδη, ὃ οὐ πείσεσθαι ἔμελλεν</span> _nor did he know this, that she had no thought to comply_ <cite data-ref="Hom. Od. 3.146"><span lang="grc">γ</span> 146</cite>.

**d.** That this use of statements of fact standing outside indirect discourse is optional only, is seen from a comparison of the first example in 2624 with <span lang="grc">καλῶς γὰρ ᾔδειν ὡς ἐγὼ ταύτῃ κράτιστός εἰμι</span> _for he knew full well that I am first-rate in this line_ <cite data-ref="Aristoph. Wasps 635">Ar. Vesp. 635</cite> and with <span lang="grc">ᾔδει αὐτὸν ὅτι μέσον ἔχοι τοῦ Περσικοῦ στρατεύματος</span> _he knew that he held the centre of the Persian army_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 1.8.21">X. A. 1.8.21</cite>.

##### <a id="s2625"></a> §2625

An optative with or without <span lang="grc">ἄν</span> is regularly retained after <span lang="grc">ὅτι</span> (<span lang="grc">ὡς</span>).

<span lang="grc">ἐδίδασκον ὡς . . . συνεστρατεύοντο ὅποι ἡγοῖντο</span> _they showed that they always followed them in their campaigns wherever they led_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Hell. 5.2.8">X. H. 5.2.8</cite> ( = <span lang="grc">συνεστρατευόμεθα, ὅποι ἡγοῖσθε</span>, cp. [2568](30-adjective-clauses-relative-clauses-2488-2573.md#s2568)), <span lang="grc">ἀπεκρί̄νατο . . . ὅτι πρόσθεν ἂν ἀποθάνοιεν ἢ τὰ ὅπλα παραδοίησαν</span> _he replied that they would sooner die than surrender their arms_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 2.1.10">X. A. 2.1.10</cite> ( = <span lang="grc">ἂν ἀποθάνοιμεν, παραδοῖμεν</span>).

##### <a id="s2626"></a> §2626

In some cases the optative _with_ <span lang="grc">ἄν</span> in temporal and relative sentences is used to represent the subjunctive with <span lang="grc">ἄν</span>; but many scholars expel <span lang="grc">ἄν</span>.

<span lang="grc">παρήγγειλαν αὐτοῖς μὴ πρότερον ἐπιτίθεσθαι πρὶν ἂν τῶν σφετέρων ἢ πέσοι τις ἢ τρωθείη</span> _they gave orders to them that they should not attack before some one of their number had either fallen or been wounded_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Hell. 2.4.18">X. H. 2.4.18</cite>. Cp. [2421](29-adverbial-complex-sentences-2193-2487/12-temporal-clauses.md#s2421).

##### <a id="s2627"></a> §2627

An optative occasioned by indirect discourse may stand after a _primary_ tense when it is implied that the thought quoted has been expressed in the past.

<span lang="grc">λέγει ὁ λόγος ὅτι Νεοπτόλεμος Νέστορα ἔροιτο</span> _the story goes that Neoptolemus asked Nestor_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Hipp. Maj. 286b">P. Hipp. M. 286b</cite>. This may be expressed by <span lang="grc">λέγεται εἰπεῖν ὅτι</span>. Cp. <span lang="grc">λέγεται εἰπεῖν ὅτι βούλοιτο</span> _it is reported that he said that he wished_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 1.4.25">X. C. 1.4.25</cite>. <!-- page 593 -->

**a.** The historical present is a secondary tense: <span lang="grc">οἱ δὲ πεμφθέντες λέγουσι Κύ̄ρῳ ὅτι μῑσοῖεν τοὺς Ἀσσυρίους</span> _and those who had been sent told Cyrus that they hated the Assyrians_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 4.2.4">X. C. 4.2.4</cite>.

##### <a id="s2628"></a> §2628

Indirect discourse may be introduced by <span lang="grc">ὅτι</span> (<span lang="grc">ὡς</span>) and then pass into the infinitive as if the introductory verb had required the infinitive.

<span lang="grc">ἡ δὲ ἀπεκρί̄νατο ὅτι βούλοιτο μὲν ἅπαντα τῷ πατρὶ χαρίζεσθαι, ἄ̄κοντα μέντοι τὸν παῖδα χαλεπὸν εἶναι νομίζειν</span> ( = <span lang="grc">νομίζοι</span>) <span lang="grc">καταλιπεῖν</span> _she answered that she wished to do everything to oblige her father, but that she considered it unkind to leave the child behind against his inclination_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 1.3.13">X. C. 1.3.13</cite>.

**a.** It is unusual to have the infinitive first, and then <span lang="grc">ὅτι</span> (<cite data-ref="Thuc. 5.65">T. 5.65</cite>).

**b.** One and the same clause may even begin with <span lang="grc">ὅτι</span> (<span lang="grc">ὡς</span>) and then (sometimes after a parenthesis) be continued by an infinitive, less often by a participle. Thus, <span lang="grc">ἀκούω ὅτι</span> (omitted in one Ms.) <span lang="grc">καὶ συνθηρευτά̄ς τινας τῶν παίδων σοι γενέσθαι αὐτοῦ</span> _I hear too that some of his sons became your companions in the chase_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 2.4.15">X. C. 2.4.15</cite>. Continuation with a participle in <cite data-ref="Thuc. 4.37">T. 4.37</cite>.

##### <a id="s2629"></a> §2629

An optative dependent on <span lang="grc">ὅτι</span> (<span lang="grc">ὡς</span>) may be followed, in a parenthetical or appended clause (often introduced by <span lang="grc">γάρ</span> or <span lang="grc">οὖν</span>), by an _independent_ optative, which is used as if it itself directly depended on <span lang="grc">ὅτι</span> (<span lang="grc">ὡς</span>).

<span lang="grc">ἔλεγον πολλοὶ . . . ὅτι παντὸς ἄξια λέγοι Σεύθης· χειμὼν γὰρ εἴη καὶ οὔτε οἴκαδε ἀποπλεῖν τῷ ταῦτα βουλομένῳ δυνατὸν εἴη κτλ</span>. _many said that what Seuthes said was of much value; for it was winter and neither was it possible for any one who so desired to sail home_, etc. <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 7.3.13">X. A. 7.3.13</cite> (here we might have had <span lang="grc">χειμῶνα γὰρ εἶναι</span> by [2628](#s2628)).

**a.** Such an independent optative may also follow an infinitive in indirect discourse (<cite data-ref="Lys. 13.78">L. 13.78</cite>), an indicative after <span lang="grc">ὅτι</span> (<cite data-ref="Isaeus 8.22">Is. 8.22</cite>), or a participle (<cite data-ref="Isaeus 9.5">Is. 9.5</cite>). After an optative in indirect discourse the appended clause may contain an indicative (<cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 6.2.10">X. A. 6.2.10</cite>, <cite data-ref="Isoc. 17.21">I. 17.21</cite>).

##### <a id="s2630"></a> §2630

An infinitive in indirect discourse may follow a sentence which merely _involves_ the idea of indirect statement.

<span lang="grc">ὁ δὲ αὐτοὺς εἰς Λακεδαίμονα ἐκέλευεν ἰέναι· οὐ γὰρ εἶναι κύ̄ριος αὐτός</span> _he recommended them to go to Lacedaemon; for_ (he said that) _he was not himself empowered to act_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Hell. 2.2.12">X. H. 2.2.12</cite>.

##### <a id="s2631"></a> §2631

In subordinate temporal and relative clauses the infinitive is often used for the indicative or optative by attraction to an infinitive standing in the principal clause after a _verb of saying_. In some cases <span lang="grc">ἔφη</span> may be mentally inserted.

<span lang="grc">ἔφη . . . ἐπειδὴ δὲ γενέσθαι ἐπὶ τῇ οἰκίᾳ τῇ Ἀγάθωνος, ἀνεῳγμένην καταλαμβάνειν τὴν θύραν</span> _he said that, when he arrived at the house of Agathon_, _he found the door open_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Sym. 174d">P. S. 174d</cite> ( = <span lang="grc">ἐπειδὴ ἐγενόμην, καταλαμβάνω</span>). See also the sentence quoted in 1228 b, end. So <span lang="grc">οὗτοι δὲ ἔλεγον ὅτι πολλοὺς φαίη Ἀριαῖος εἶναι Πέρσᾱς ἑαυτοῦ βελτί̄ους, οὓς οὐκ ἂν ἀνασχέσθαι αὐτοῦ βασιλεύοντος</span> _and they said that Ariaeus said that there were many Persians better than himself, who would not endure_ <!-- page 594 --> _his being king_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 2.2.1">X. A. 2.2.1</cite> ( = <span lang="grc">πολλοί εἰσι ἐμαυτοῦ βελτί̄ους, οἳ οὐκ. ἂν ἀνάσχοιντο ἐμοῦ β</span>.). Here the relative is equivalent, in sense, to <span lang="grc">καὶ τούτους</span>. The infinitive occurs even in clauses with <span lang="grc">εἰ</span> (<cite data-ref="Thuc. 4.98">T. 4.98</cite>, and often in Hdt.), and with <span lang="grc">διότι</span> (<cite data-ref="Hdt. 3.55">Hdt. 3.55</cite>).

**a.** The infinitive is rare in such relative clauses as <span lang="grc">διορίζουσι σαφῶς ἐν οἷς ἐξεῖναι ἀποκτιννύναι</span> _they make a clear distinction in cases where it is permitted to kill_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 23.74">D. 23.74</cite>.

##### <a id="s2632"></a> §2632

For the sake of variation, a mood of the direct form may be used in the same sentence with a mood of the indirect. The main verb may be kept in the direct form, while the subordinate verb becomes optative, or, less often, the subordinate verb may be retained in the direct form though the main verb becomes optative.

<span lang="grc">οὗτοι ἔλεγον ὅτι Κῦρος μὲν τέθνηκεν, Ἀριαῖος δὲ πεφευγὼς . . . εἴη</span> _these said that Cyrus was dead but that Ariaeus had fled_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 2.1.3">X. A. 2.1.3</cite> (here we might have had <span lang="grc">τεθνήκοι</span> or <span lang="grc">πέφευγε</span>), <span lang="grc">αἱ δὲ ἀπεκρί̄ναντο ὅτι οὐκ ἐνταῦθα εἴη, ἀλλ᾽ ἀπέχει ὅσον παρασάγγην</span> _and they replied that he was not there but was a parasang distant_ 4. 5. 10 (here we might have <span lang="grc">ἐστί</span> or <span lang="grc">ἀπέχοι</span>), <span lang="grc">ἐδόκει δῆλον εἶναι ὅτι αἱρήσονται αὐτὸν εἴ τις ἐπιψηφίζοι</span> _it seemed to be clear that they would elect him if any one should put it to vote_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 6.1.25">X. A. 6.1.25</cite> (here we might have <span lang="grc">αἱρήσοιντο</span> or <span lang="grc">ἐὰ̄ν ε&lt;\*&gt;πιψηφίζᾐ, ἔλεξας . . . ὅτι μέγιστον εἴη μαθεῖν ὅπως δεῖ ἐξεργάζεσθαι ἕκαστα</span> _you said that it was essential to learn how it is necessary to conduct each process_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Ec. 15.2">X. O. 15.2</cite> (here <span lang="grc">ἐστί</span> or <span lang="grc">δέοι</span> might have been used), <span lang="grc">παρήγγειλαν, ἐπειδὴ δειπνήσαιεν, . . . ἀναπαύεσθαι καὶ ἕπεσθαι, ἡνίκ᾽ ἄν τις παραγγέλλῃ</span> _they gave orders that, when they had supped, they should rest and follow when any one gave the command_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 3.5.18">X. A. 3.5.18</cite> (here we might have had <span lang="grc">ἐπειδὰν δειπνήσωσι</span> or <span lang="grc">ἡνίκα παραγγέλλοι</span>). Other examples 2619. Subjunctive (in some Mss.), then optative: <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 7.7.57">X. A. 7.7.57</cite>.

##### <a id="s2633"></a> §2633

The idea conveyed by an imperative or a hortatory (or even deliberative) subjunctive of direct discourse may be set forth in the infinitive by a _statement_ as to what _ought to be_.

**a.** In an infinitive dependent on a _verb of will_ or _desire_ (such as _ask_, _command_, _advise_, _forbid_, etc. [1992](21-verbal-nouns.md#s1992)) which does not properly take the construction of indirect discourse.

<span lang="grc">εἷς δὲ δὴ εἶπε</span> ([1997](21-verbal-nouns.md#s1997)) . . . <span lang="grc">στρατηγοὺς μὲν ἑλέσθαι ἄλλους</span> _and some one urged that they choose other generals_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 1.3.14">X. A. 1.3.14</cite> (cp. <span lang="grc">ἕλεσθε</span> or <span lang="grc">ἕλωμεν</span>), <span lang="grc">ἀπηγόρευε μηδένα βάλλειν</span> _he forbade any one to shoot_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 1.4.14">X. C. 1.4.14</cite> (cp. <span lang="grc">μηδεὶς βαλλέτω</span>).

N.—Here may be placed the infinitive after <span lang="grc">ἡγοῦμαι, νομίζω, οἴομαι</span> in the sense of <span lang="grc">δοκῶ</span> _I think it proper_ (or _necessary_); as <span lang="grc">ᾤοντο ἀπιέναι</span> _they thought that they should retire_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Hell. 4.7.4">X. H. 4.7.4</cite> (cp. <span lang="grc">ἀπίωμεν</span>).

**b.** In an infinitive dependent on <span lang="grc">ἔφη χρῆναι</span> (<span lang="grc">δεῖν</span>), as <span lang="grc">ἔφη . . . χρῆναι πλεῖν ἐπὶ Συρᾱκούσᾱς</span> _he said that they ought to sail to Syracuse_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 4.69">T. 4.69</cite>.

**c.** In the simple infinitive, as <span lang="grc">τὰ̄ς μὲν ἐπιστολὰ̄ς . . . ἀνέγνωσαν, ἐν αἷς πολλῶν ἄλλων γεγραμμένων κεφάλαιον ἦν πρὸς Λακεδαιμονίους οὐ γιγνώσκειν ὅ τι βούλονται . . . εἰ οὖν τι βούλονται σαφὲς λέγειν, πέμψαι μετὰ τοῦ Πέρσου ἄνδρας ὡς αὐτόν</span> _they read the dispatches, in which of much besides therein written to the Lacedaemonians the substance was that_ the king _did not understand what they wanted; if therefore_ <!-- page 595 --> _they wished to make explicit statements, let them send men to him in company with the Persian_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 4.50">T. 4.50</cite>. Cp. <cite data-ref="Thuc. 1.27">T. 1.27</cite>. 1 <span lang="grc">μένειν ῀ μενέτω</span>.

##### <a id="s2634"></a> §2634

Long sentences (and even some short complex sentences), or a series of sentences, in indirect discourse depending on a single _verb of saying_ or _thinking_, are uncongenial to the animated character of Greek, which resists the formal regularity of Latin. Some long speeches in indirect discourse do, however, appear, e.g. Andoc. 1. 3842, Thuc. 6. 49, Xen. C. 8. 1. 10-11, Plato R. 614 b (the entire _Symposium_ is given in reported form). To effect variety and to ensure clearness by relieving the strain on the leading verb, Greek has various devices.

**a.** <span lang="grc">ἔφη</span> (<span lang="grc">ἔλεξε, εἶπεν, ἤρετο</span>) is repeated, e.g. <cite data-ref="Thuc. 7.48">T. 7.48</cite>.

**b.** The indirect form is abandoned for the direct form, e.g. <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 1.3.14">X. A. 1.3.14</cite>, 1. 9. 25, 4. 8. 10; often with a change, or repetition, of the _verb of saying_ (<cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 5.6.37">X. A. 5.6.37</cite>, <cite data-ref="Xen. Hell. 2.1.25">X. H. 2.1.25</cite>).

**c.** <span lang="grc">ἔφη χρῆναι</span> (<span lang="grc">δεῖν</span>) or <span lang="grc">ἐκέλευσε</span> is inserted or repeated (<cite data-ref="Thuc. 6.49">T. 6.49</cite>. 4).

N. 1.—Transition from direct to indirect discourse is rare (<cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 7.1.39">X. A. 7.1.39</cite>, cp. <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 3.2.25">X. C. 3.2.25</cite>).

N. 2.—An interrogative clause always depends immediately on the introductory verb, hence such clauses do not occur in the course of a long sentence in indirect discourse.

| <span lang="grc">ἔφη γὰρ εἶναι μὲν ἀνδράποδόν οἱ ἐπὶ Λαυρίῳ, δεῖν δὲ κομίσασθαι ἀποφορά̄ν. ἀναστὰ̄ς δὲ πρῲ ψευσθεὶς τῆς ὥρᾱς βαδίζειν· εἶναι δὲ πανσέληνον. ἐπεὶ δὲ παρὰ τὸ προπύλαιον τοῦ Διονύ̄σου ἦν, ὁρᾶν ἀνθρώπους πολλοὺς ἀπὸ τοῦ Ὠιδείου καταβαίνοντας εἰς τὴν ὀρχήστρᾱν· δείσᾱς δὲ αὐτούς, εἰσελθὼν ὑπὸ τὴν σκιὰ̄ν καθέζεσθαι μεταξὺ τοῦ κίονος καὶ τῆς στήλης ἐφ᾽ ᾗ ὁ στρατηγός ἐστιν ὁ χαλκοῦς. ὁρᾶν δὲ ἀνθρώπους τὸν μὲν ἀριθμὸν μάλιστα τριᾱκοσίους, ἑστάναι δὲ κύκλῳ ἀνὰ πέντε καὶ δέκα ἄνδρας, τοὺς δὲ ἀνὰ εἴκοσιν· ὁρῶν δὲ αὐτ ῶν πρὸς τὴν σελήνην τὰ πρόσωπα τῶν πλείστων γιγνώσκειν. καὶ πρῶτον μέν, ὦ ἄνδρες, τοῦθ᾽ ὑπέθετο δεινότατον πρᾶγμα, οἶμαι, ὅπως ἐν ἐκείνῳ εἴη ὅντινα βούλοιτο Ἀθηναίων φάναι τῶν ἀνδρῶν τούτων εἶναι, ὅντινα δὲ μὴ βούλοιτο, λέγειν ὅτι οὐκ ἦν. ἰδὼν δὲ ταῦτ᾽ ἔφη ἐπὶ Λαύριον ἰέναι, καὶ τῇ ὑστεραίᾳ ἀκούειν ὅτι οἱ Ἑρμαῖ εἶεν περικεκομμένοι· γνῶναι οὖν εὐθὺς ὅτι τούτων εἴη τῶν ἀνδρῶν τὸ ἔργον. ἥκων δὲ εἰς ἄστυ ζητητά̄ς τε ἤδη ᾑρημένους καταλαμβάνειν καὶ μήνῡτρα κεκηρῡγμένα ἑκατὸν μνᾶς</span>.—Andocides 1. 38. | For Dioclides said that he had a slave at Laurium, and that he had to fetch a payment due him. Rising early he mistook the time and set out, and there was a full moon. When he was by the gateway of the sanctuary of Dionysus, he saw a body of men coming down from the Odeum into the orchestra, and through fear of them he betook himself into the shade and sat down between the column and the block on which the Bronze General stands. He saw about three hundred men, some standing round about in groups of fifteen, others in groups of twenty. On seeing them in the moonlight he recognized the faces of most. In the first place, gentlemen, he has concocted this most extraordinary tale, in order, as I believe, that it might be in his power to include among these men any Athenian he wished, or to exclude any he did not wish. On seeing this he said he went to Laurium, and on the day after heard that the statues of Hermes had been mutilated. So he knew forthwith that it was the work of these men. On his return to the city he found that commissioners of inquiry had already been appointed and that a hundred minae had been offered as a reward. |
