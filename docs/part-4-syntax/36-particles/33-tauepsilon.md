# PARTICLES: τέ

##### <a id="s2967"></a> §2967

<span lang="grc"> **τέ** </span> _and_ (postpositive, and enclitic as _-que_) is generally used with a correlative conjunction.

##### <a id="s2968"></a> §2968

<span lang="grc">τέ</span> alone sometimes in prose links whole clauses or sentences which serve to explain, amplify, supplement, or to denote a consequence of, what precedes (_and thus, and therefore, and as a result_). Thus, <span lang="grc">ὁ δ᾽ ἐχαλέπαινεν . . ., ἐκέλευσέ τ᾽ αὐτὸν ἐκ τοῦ μέσου ἐξίστασθαι</span> _but he was angry and_ (_therefore_) _ordered him to get out of the way_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 1.5.14">X. A. 1.5.14</cite>. Cp. [2978](#s2978).

**a.** This use of <span lang="grc">τέ</span> (<span lang="grc">τέ</span> _consequential_) is quite common in Herodotus and Thucydides, rather rare in Xenophon, and infrequent in other prose writers. It occurs also in poetry.

N.—In poetry <span lang="grc">τέ</span> alone (cp. _-que_) often connects single parallel nouns and pronouns so that the two connected ideas form a whole; as <span lang="grc">σκῆπτρον τῑμά̄ς τε</span> _sceptre and prerogatives_ <cite data-ref="Aesch. PB 171">A. Pr. 171</cite>. In prose, participles and infinitives are occasionally linked by <span lang="grc">τέ</span>; as <span lang="grc">καθαρωτέρᾱ οὖσα πρεπόντως τε μᾶλλον ἠμφιεσμένη</span> _being fairer and dressed more becomingly_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Ec. 10.12">X. O. 10.12</cite>. <!-- page 667 -->

##### <a id="s2969"></a> §2969

<span lang="grc">τέ</span> (or <span lang="grc">καί</span>) meaning _both_ may be followed by asyndeton (<cite data-ref="Soph. Ant. 296">S. Ant. 296</cite>).

##### <a id="s2970"></a> §2970

Homer often, and Herodotus sometimes, adds <span lang="grc">τέ</span> to relative pronouns and conjunctions introducing subordinate clauses, which are usually postpositive. So after <span lang="grc">ὅς, ὅσος, οἷος, ὡς, ὅτε, ἐπεί, ἔνθα, ὅθι</span>, etc. Thus, <span lang="grc">φίληθεν ἐκ Διός, ὅς τε θεοῖσι . . . ἀνάσσει</span> _they were loved by Zeus_, _who rules over the gods_ B 669. This untranslatable <span lang="grc">τέ</span> is probably connective (not indefinite), and belongs to the whole clause. It has the effect of showing that its clause corresponds in some way to the preceding clause. <span lang="grc">ὅς τε</span> is found in lyric poetry and in the lyric parts of tragedy (rarely in dialogue parts). <span lang="grc">ὥστε, οἷός τε</span> became common.

##### <a id="s2971"></a> §2971

This connective force is also seen when <span lang="grc">τέ</span> stands in the principal clause, sometimes both in the principal and in the subordinate clause, e.g. <span lang="grc">ὅς κε θεοῖς ἐπιπείθηται, μάλα τ᾽ ἔκλυον αὐτοῦ</span> _whosoever obeys the gods, him especially they hear_ A 218, <span lang="grc">ὅππῃ τ᾽ ἰ̄θύ̄σῃ, τῇ τ᾽ εἴκουσι στίχες ἀνδρῶν</span> _wheresoever he rushes_, _there the ranks of men give way_ M 48.

##### <a id="s2972"></a> §2972

Homer has <span lang="grc">τέ</span> after the coördinating conjunctions <span lang="grc">καί, δέ, οὐδέ, ἀλλά, ἤ</span>; after <span lang="grc">ἦ, μέν, πέρ, γάρ</span>, and before <span lang="grc">ἄρα</span> in questions.

##### <a id="s2973"></a> §2973

<span lang="grc"> **τὲ . . . τέ** </span> usually serves to connect clauses, less frequently single words. In English _and_ often suffices, but _as . . . so_ is often in place. <span lang="grc">τὲ . . . τέ</span> is more common in poetry than in prose, but in prose more common than <span lang="grc">τέ</span> standing alone. Thus, <span lang="grc">πατὴρ ἀνδρῶν τε θεῶν τε</span> _father of men and gods_ A 544, <span lang="grc">ἐμοί τε γὰρ πολέμιοι Ἀσσύριοι, σοί τε νῦν ἐχθί̄ονές εἰσιν ἢ ἐμοί</span> _for the Assyrians are enemies to me, and they are now more hostile to you than to me_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 4.5.23">X. C. 4.5.23</cite>, <span lang="grc">περὶ ὧν εἰδέναι τε κάλλιστον μὴ εἰδέναι τε αἴσχιστον</span> _knowledge of which is most excellent and ignorance most disgraceful_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Gorg. 472c">P. G. 472c</cite>.

**a.** One clause may be negative, the other affirmative (<cite data-ref="Thuc. 2.22">T. 2.22</cite>); but we usually have <span lang="grc">οὔτε</span> instead of <span lang="grc">τὲ οὐ</span>.

##### <a id="s2974"></a> §2974

<span lang="grc"> **τὲ καί** </span> or <span lang="grc"> **τὲ . . . καί** </span> often serves to unite complements, both similars and opposites. <span lang="grc">τὲ . . . καί</span> is not used when one clause is subordinate to another. The two words or clauses thus united may show a contrast, or the second may be stronger than the first. <span lang="grc">τέ</span> is commonly separated from <span lang="grc">καί</span> by one or more words. <span lang="grc">τὲ . . . καί</span> is weaker than <span lang="grc">καὶ . . . καί</span>, and will not easily bear the translation _both . . . and_. It is rare in colloquial Attic. Thus, <span lang="grc">ἄρχειν τε καὶ ἄρχεσθαι</span> _to rule and be ruled_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 1.9.4">X. A. 1.9.4</cite>, <span lang="grc">κάλλιστόν τε καὶ ἄριστον</span> _fairest and best_ 2. 1. 9, <span lang="grc">τό τ᾽ ἄρχειν καὶ τὸ δουλεύειν</span> _to rule and to be a slave_ <cite data-ref="Aesch. PB 927">A. Pr. 927</cite>, <span lang="grc">βίᾳ τε κοὐχ ἑκών</span> _by force and not willingly_ <cite data-ref="Soph. OC 935">S. O. C. 935</cite>, <span lang="grc">γυμνάσαι . . . ἑαυτόν τε καὶ τοὺς ἵππους</span> _to exercise himself and his horses_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 1.2.7">X. A. 1.2.7</cite>. Clauses dissimilar in form may be linked by <span lang="grc">τὲ . . . καί</span>; as <span lang="grc">ἀπεκρί̄νατο διὰ βραχέων τε καὶ αὐτὰ τὰ ἐρωτώμενα</span> _he answered briefly and only the questions put to him_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Prot. 336a">P. Pr. 336a</cite>.

##### <a id="s2975"></a> §2975

<span lang="grc">τὲ . . . καί</span> is often used of actions coincident in time, or of actions standing in a causal relation to each other; as <span lang="grc">ἡμέρᾱ τε σχεδὸν ὑπέφαινε καὶ εἰς τὸ μέσον ἧκον οἱ ἄρχοντες</span> _day was just breaking and_ (= _when_) _the officers came into the centre_ of the camp <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 3.3.1">X. A. 3.3.1</cite> (temporal parataxis; cp. [2169](../26-syntax-of-the-compound-sentence.md#s2169)).

##### <a id="s2976"></a> §2976

<span lang="grc">τὲ . . . καί</span> is sometimes used of alternatives (for <span lang="grc">εἴτε . . . εἴτε</span>). Thus, <span lang="grc">θεοῦ τε γὰρ θέλοντος . . . καὶ μὴ θέλοντος</span> _whether God wills or not_ <cite data-ref="Aesch. Seven 427">A. Sept. 427</cite>. Here <span lang="grc">καὶ . . . καί</span> is more common ([2877](17-kappaalphaiota.md#s2877)). <!-- page 668 -->

##### <a id="s2977"></a> §2977

We find <span lang="grc"> **τὲ . . . καὶ . . . τέ, τὲ . . . καὶ . . . τὲ . . . τέ . . . ῾τἔ, τὲ . . . τὲ . . . καί, τὲ . . . τὲ . . . καὶ . . . τέ, τὲ . . . καὶ . . . καὶ . . . τέ** </span>. But in prose <span lang="grc">τέ</span> before and after <span lang="grc">καί</span> is rare.

##### <a id="s2978"></a> §2978

When <span lang="grc">τέ</span> follows <span lang="grc">τὲ . . . καί, τέ</span> does not point back to <span lang="grc">καί</span>, but denotes an addition to the preceding member (_and besides_). Thus, <span lang="grc">τείχη τε περιελόντες καὶ ναῦς παραδόντες φόρον τε ταξάμενοι</span> _both destroying their walls and surrendering their ships and besides assessing tribute on themselves_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 1.108">T. 1.108</cite>. Cp. [2968](#s2968).

##### <a id="s2979"></a> §2979

<span lang="grc"> **καί τε** </span> is Epic; elsewhere the <span lang="grc">καί</span> of <span lang="grc">καὶ . . . τε</span> belongs to the whole clause (<cite data-ref="Aesch. Lib. 252">A. Ch. 252</cite>).

##### <a id="s2980"></a> §2980

<span lang="grc"> **ἄλλως τε καί** </span> _both in other ways and especially, on other grounds and particularly_, or simply _especially_. This combination usually stands before conditional clauses (or clauses with a conditional participle), causal, and temporal clauses. Thus, <span lang="grc">χαλεπὸν οἶμαι διαβαίνειν ἄλλως τε καὶ πολεμίων πολλῶν ἔμπροσθεν ὄντων</span> _I think it hard to cross, especially when the enemy faces us in full force_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 5.6.9">X. A. 5.6.9</cite>, <span lang="grc">πάντων . . . ἀποστερεῖσθαι λῡπηρόν ἐστι . . . , ἄλλως τε κἂ̄ν ὑπ᾽ ἐχθροῦ τῳ τοῦτο συμβαίνῃ</span> _it is grievous to be deprived of anything, especially if this happens to any one at the hands of a personal enemy_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 18.5">D. 18.5</cite>. Cp. <span lang="grc">τά τ᾽ ἄλλα ἐτἱ̄μησε καὶ μῡρίους ἔδωκε δᾱρεικούς</span> _he both honoured me in other ways and gave me ten thousand darics_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 1.3.3">X. A. 1.3.3</cite>.

##### <a id="s2981"></a> §2981

<span lang="grc"> **τὲ . . . δέ** </span> is used when a writer begins as if he were going simply to _add_ the second member (_both . . . and_), but instead _contrasts_ it with the first. This combination of copulative and adversative particles is often rendered less harsh by the form of the <span lang="grc">δέ</span> clause and by other reasons. (**a**) The <span lang="grc">δέ</span> clause contains a <span lang="grc">καί</span>; as <span lang="grc">ἅμα</span> (<span lang="grc">ἔπειτα, ἔτι, πολλαχοῦ, ὡσαύτως</span>) <span lang="grc">δὲ καί</span>; e.g. <span lang="grc">ἔν τε τῇ τῶν ἐπῶν ποιήσει πολλαχοῦ δὲ καὶ ἄλλοθι</span>, lit. _both in the construction of epic poetry but also in many other cases_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Rep. 394c">P. R. 394c</cite>. (**b**) The second clause contains a formula with <span lang="grc">δέ</span> but not with <span lang="grc">καί</span>; as <span lang="grc">ἔτι δέ, τί δέ, τὸ δὲ κεφάλαιον, μετὰ δὲ ταῦτα</span>. Thus, <span lang="grc">πρότερόν τε . . . νῦν δέ</span> (_both_) _formerly . . . but now_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Hell. 7.1.24">X. H. 7.1.24</cite>. Cp. <cite data-ref="Plat. Laws 664b">P. L. 664b</cite>, 947 a, 967 d. (**c**) After a considerable interval occasioned by the extension of the <span lang="grc">τέ</span> clause, it is natural to resume with <span lang="grc">δέ</span>. So <cite data-ref="Thuc. 6.83">T. 6.83</cite>. 1, <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 7.8.11">X. A. 7.8.11</cite>, <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 2.1.22">X. C. 2.1.22</cite>, <cite data-ref="Lys. 2.17">L. 2.17</cite>.

##### <a id="s2982"></a> §2982

Rare combinations are, _e.g.:_

<span lang="grc">**ἢ** . . . **τέ**</span> instead of <span lang="grc">ἢ . . . ἤ</span>. Thus, <span lang="grc">ἢ παῖδες νεαροὶ χῆραί τε γυναῖκες</span> _either young children and_ (= _or_) _widowed women_ B 289. <span lang="grc">**τὲ** . . . **ἤ**</span> is often emended in <cite data-ref="Xen. Ec. 20.12">X. O. 20.12</cite>, <cite data-ref="Plat. Meno 95b">P. Men. 95b</cite>.

<span lang="grc"> **τὲ . . . οὐδέ** </span> (<span lang="grc"> **μηδέ** </span>) with <span lang="grc">τέ</span> instead of <span lang="grc">οὔτε</span> (<span lang="grc">μήτε</span>); as <cite data-ref="Eur. IT 697">E. I. T. 697</cite>, <cite data-ref="Plat. Stat. 271e">P. Pol. 271e</cite>. <span lang="grc">τέ</span> is not followed by <span lang="grc">οὔτε</span> (<span lang="grc">μήτε</span>).

##### <a id="s2983"></a> §2983

**Position of <span lang="grc">τέ</span>**.—<span lang="grc">τέ</span> usually follows the word with which the sentence or sentence-part to be connected is most concerned. Apart from many irregularities there are certain exceptions to this rule which are commonly observed.

**a.** <span lang="grc">τέ</span> may come between two words which go closely together, as between article (preposition, attributive genitive) and its noun. Thus, <span lang="grc">τό τε βαρβαρικὸν καὶ τὸ Ἑλληνικόν</span> _the barbarian and the Greek force_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 1.2.1">X. A. 1.2.1</cite>, <span lang="grc">εἶμι πρός τε λουτρὰ καὶ λειμῶνας</span> _I will go to the bathing places and the meadows_ <cite data-ref="Soph. Aj. 654">S. Aj. 654</cite> (for <span lang="grc">πρὸς λουτρά τε</span>). But <span lang="grc">ἡ πόλις. τε καὶ ἡμεῖς οἱ νόμοι</span> _the State and we the laws_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Crito 53a">P. Cr. 53a</cite>. <!-- page 669 -->

**b.** <span lang="grc">τέ</span> connecting an entire clause stands as near as possible to the beginning. Cp. <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 1.8.3">X. A. 1.8.3</cite>.

**c.** <span lang="grc">τέ</span> may stand after a word or expression which, though common to two members of a clause, is placed either at the beginning (especially after a preposition) or in the second member. Thus, <span lang="grc">ἅ τε δεῖ φίλια καὶ</span> (<span lang="grc">ἃ δεῖ</span>) <span lang="grc">πολέμια ἡμᾶς νομίζειν</span> _what we must consider as belonging to our friends and what to our enemies_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 5.2.21">X. C. 5.2.21</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἔν τε τῷ θερμοτέρῳ καὶ ψῡχροτέρῳ</span> _in the hotter and colder_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Phileb. 24b">P. Phil. 24b</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἅπᾱσι φίλον ἄνδρα τε σοφώτατον</span> _a man dear to all and most wise_ <cite data-ref="Aristoph. Wasps 1277">Ar. Vesp. 1277</cite>.

**d.** The freer position of <span lang="grc">τέ</span> is often due to the fact that several words are taken as forming a single notion. Thus, <span lang="grc">ἡ καλλίστη δὴ πολῑτείᾱ τε καὶ ὁ κάλλιστος ἀνήρ</span> _the very noblest constitution and the noblest man_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Rep. 562a">P. R. 562a</cite>.
