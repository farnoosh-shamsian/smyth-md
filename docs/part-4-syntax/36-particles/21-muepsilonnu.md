# PARTICLES: μέν

##### <a id="s2895"></a> §2895

<span lang="grc"> **μέν** </span> was originally an asseverative, emphatic particle (_surely_, _certainly_, _indeed_) and a weaker form of <span lang="grc">μήν</span>. Cp. Epic <span lang="grc">ἦ μέν, καὶ μέν, οὐ μέν</span> in asseverations and protestations. Asseverative <span lang="grc">μέν</span> survived as <span lang="grc">μέν</span> _solitarium_ and in combination with other particles. Antithetical (concessive) <span lang="grc">μέν</span> owes its origin to the fact that, as emphasis. may indicate a contrast, the clause in which <span lang="grc">μέν</span> stood was felt as preliminary to an adversative member of the sentence. Through association with this adversative member <span lang="grc">μέν</span> gradually lost its primitive asseverative force. <!-- page 655 -->

##### <a id="s2896"></a> §2896

<span lang="grc">μέν</span> _solitarium_ occurs when a clause with <span lang="grc">μέν</span> is not followed by a clause with <span lang="grc">δέ</span>. This is especially common when the antithetical clause is to be supplied in thought, as when <span lang="grc">μέν</span> emphasizes a statement made by a person with reference to himself as opposed to others (often with a tone of arrogance or of credulity). Here any possible opposition or difference of opinion, however justifiable, is left unexpressed. Thus, <span lang="grc">ἐγὼ μὲν οὐκ οἶδα</span> _I for my part do not know_ (though others _may_) <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 1.4.12">X. C. 1.4.12</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἀπέπλευσαν, ὡς μὲν τοῖς πλείστοις ἐδόκουν, φιλοτῑμηθέντες</span> _they sailed away since they were jealous as it seemed to the majority at least_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 1.4.7">X. A. 1.4.7</cite>. So in such phrases as <span lang="grc">δοκῶ μέν, ἡγοῦμαι μέν, οἶμαι μέν</span>.

##### <a id="s2897"></a> §2897

Sometimes <span lang="grc">μέν</span> _solitarium_ merely emphasizes a word in its clause and does not imply a contrast. Thus, <span lang="grc">ἐμοὶ μὲν οἰστέα τάδε</span> _this must be borne by me on my part_ <cite data-ref="Soph. OC 1360">S. O. C. 1360</cite>.

##### <a id="s2898"></a> §2898

<span lang="grc">μέν</span> _solitarium_ is commonest after personal pronouns; but occurs also after demonstrative pronouns (<cite data-ref="Lys. 25.16">L. 25.16</cite>), after relatives (<cite data-ref="Aeschin. 3.209">Aes. 3.209</cite>), after substantives without the article (<cite data-ref="Dem. 9.15">D. 9.15</cite>), or after the article and before its substantive (<cite data-ref="Lys. 29.1">L. 29.1</cite>), after adjectives (<cite data-ref="Lys. 1.27">L. 1.27</cite>), after adverbs (<cite data-ref="Lys. 12.91">L. 12.91</cite>), after verbs (<cite data-ref="Dem. 19.231">D. 19.231</cite>). In questions <span lang="grc">μέν</span> alone is rare (<cite data-ref="Plat. Meno 82b">P. Men. 82b</cite>).

##### <a id="s2899"></a> §2899

In combination with other particles, especially <span lang="grc">δή</span> and <span lang="grc">οὖν</span>, asseverative <span lang="grc">μέν</span> either has a simple confirmatory force or is used adversatively. The following cases must be distinguished from those in which <span lang="grc">μέν</span> is correlative to <span lang="grc">δέ</span>.

##### <a id="s2900"></a> §2900

<span lang="grc"> **μὲν δή** </span> expresses positive certainty, especially in conclusions. It is common in summing up and in transitions, and is used either alone or with other particles (sometimes it is followed by <span lang="grc">ἀλλά</span> or <span lang="grc">δέ</span>). Thus, <span lang="grc">ταῦτα μὲν δὴ τοιαῦτα</span> _so much for that_ <cite data-ref="Aesch. PB 500">A. Pr. 500</cite>. So also, e.g. <span lang="grc"> **ἀλλὰ μὲν δή** </span> _but certainly in fact_ (<span lang="grc">ἀλλ᾽ οὐδὲ μὲν δή</span> in rejecting an alternative); <span lang="grc"> **ει᾽ μὲν δή** </span> _if indeed in truth;_ <span lang="grc"> **καὶ μὲν δή** </span> _and in truth, and in fact_ (often in transitions); <span lang="grc"> **ου᾽ μὲν δή** </span> _certainly not at all_, _nor yet, in truth_ (often used adversatively).

##### <a id="s2901"></a> §2901

<span lang="grc"> **μὲν οὖν** </span> lit. _certainly in fact_, <span lang="grc">μέν</span> being a weaker form of <span lang="grc">μήν. μὲν οὖν</span> has two common uses, according as the particles have a compound force, or each has its own force.

**a.** The **compound** force of <span lang="grc">μὲν οὖν</span> is seen in affirmations; as in replies: <span lang="grc">πάνυ</span> (<span lang="grc">μάλιστα</span>) <span lang="grc">μὲν οὖν</span> _yes_, _by all means; certainly_, _by all means; aye truly_, <span lang="grc">εὖ μὲν οὖν οἶδα</span> _nay_, _I am sure of it_, <span lang="grc">οὐ μὲν οὖν</span> _indeed not_, <span lang="grc">ἆρ᾽ ου᾽ τόδε ἦν τὸ δένδρον ἐφ᾽ ὅπερ ἦγες ἡμᾶς; τοῦτο μὲν οὖν αὐτό</span> _isn't this the tree to which you were bringing us? To be sure this is it_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Phaedrus 230a">P. Phae. 230a</cite>.

**b.** The compound force appears also when <span lang="grc">μὲν οὖν</span> indicates a correction; _nay rather_ (_imo vero_); as <span lang="grc">λέγε σύ· σὺ μὲν οὖν μοι λέγε</span> _do you say. Nay_, _rather_ you <cite data-ref="Aristoph. Kn. 13">Ar. Eq. 13</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἄτοπον τὸ ἐνύπνιον, ὦ Σώκρατες. ἐναργὲς μὲν οὖν</span> _the dream is strange_, _Socrates. Nay rather, it was distinct_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Crito 44b">P. Cr. 44b</cite>.

**c. Each particle has its own force** especially where <span lang="grc">μὲν οὖν</span> indicates a transition to a new subject. Here <span lang="grc">μέν</span> points forward to an antithesis to follow and indicated by <span lang="grc">δέ, ἀλλά, μέντοι</span>, while <span lang="grc">οὖν</span> (inferential) connects with what precedes. Here _so then, therefore_ may be used in translation. Thus, <span lang="grc">Κλέαρχος μὲν οὖν τοσαῦτα εἶπε. Τισσαφέρνης δὲ ὧδε ἀπημείφθη</span> _such then were the words of Clearthus; and on the other hand Tissaphernes answered as follows_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 2.5.15">X. A. 2.5.15</cite> <!-- page 656 --> Sometimes <span lang="grc">μὲν οὖν</span> (like _igitur_) shows that a subject announced in general terms is now to be treated in detail (<cite data-ref="Plat. Phaedo 70c">P. Ph. 70c</cite>).

##### <a id="s2902"></a> §2902

Common collocations are <span lang="grc"> **ἀλλὰ μέν** </span> (<span lang="grc">ἀλλὰ . . . μέν</span>) _but for a fact_, <span lang="grc">**γὲ μέν**, **ἦ μέν**, **καὶ μέν**</span>.

##### <a id="s2903"></a> §2903

**Antithetical** (concessive) <span lang="grc">μέν</span> distinguishes the word or clause in which it stands from a following word or clause marked usually by <span lang="grc">δέ</span> or by other particles denoting contrast, such as <span lang="grc">ἀλλά, ἀτάρ, μέντοι, μήν</span>; and even by copulative <span lang="grc">τέ, καί</span> (Hom. <span lang="grc">ἠδέ</span>). <span lang="grc">μέν</span> never connects words, clauses, or sentences.

##### <a id="s2904"></a> §2904

<span lang="grc"> **μὲν . . . δέ** </span> serves to mark stronger or weaker contrasts of various kinds, and is sometimes to be rendered by _on the one hand . . . on the other hand_, _indeed . . . but;_ but is often to be left untranslated. The <span lang="grc">μέν</span> clause has a concessive force when it is logically subordinate (_while_, _though_, _whereas_, cp. [2170](../26-syntax-of-the-compound-sentence.md#s2170)). Thus, <span lang="grc">ἡ μὲν ψῡχὴ πολυχρόνιόν ἐστι, τὸ δὲ σῶμα ἀσθενέστερον καὶ ὀλιγοχρονιώτερον</span> _the soul lasts for a long time, the body is weaker and lasts for a shorter time_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Phaedo 87d">P. Ph. 87d</cite>, <span lang="grc">καὶ πρόσθεν μὲν δὴ πολλοὶ ἡμῶν ἦρχον μὲν οὐδενός, ἤρχοντο δέ· νῦν δὲ κατεσκεύασθε οὕτω πάντες οἱ παρόντες ὥστε ἄρχετε οἱ μὲν πλειόνων, οἱ δὲ μειόνων</span> _and whereas in fact many of us hitherto commanded no one, but were subject to the command of others, now however all of you who are present are so placed that you have command, some over more, others over fewer_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 8.1.4">X. C. 8.1.4</cite>.

**a.** So <span lang="grc">**ἄλλοτε μὲν . . . ἄλλοτε δέ**, **ἅμα μὲν . . . ἅμα δέ**</span> _at once . . . and_, _partly . . . partly_, <span lang="grc">**ἔνθα μὲν . . . ἔνθα δέ**, **ἐνταῦθα μὲν . . . ἐκεῖ δέ**, **πρῶτον μὲν . . . ἔπειτα δέ**</span> (or <span lang="grc"> **ἔπειτα** </span> alone). On <span lang="grc"> **ὁ μὲν . . . ὁ δέ** </span> see [1107](../16-the-article-origin-and-development.md#s1107). Instead of <span lang="grc">ὁ</span> (<span lang="grc">οἱ</span>) <span lang="grc">δέ</span> we find e.g. <span lang="grc">ἄλλος δέ, ἔνιοι δέ, ἔστι δ᾽ οἵ</span>. So <span lang="grc">τοῦτο μὲν . . . τοῦτ᾽ ἄλλο</span> (or <span lang="grc">αὖθις</span>).—<span lang="grc">μέν</span> may stand with a participle, <span lang="grc">δέ</span> with a finite verb, in an antithetical sentence. Example in 2147 c.

**b.** <span lang="grc">εἰ, οὐ</span> (<span lang="grc">μή</span>) standing before <span lang="grc">μὲν . . . δέ</span> exercise their force on _both_ opposed clauses.

##### <a id="s2905"></a> §2905

When several verbs referring to the same person or thing are contrasted, or when several attributes are contrasted, the first has <span lang="grc">μέν</span>, the others <span lang="grc">δέ</span>. Cp. Lyc. 5, <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 3.1.19">X. A. 3.1.19</cite>. But <span lang="grc">μέν</span> is sometimes omitted.

##### <a id="s2906"></a> §2906

<span lang="grc">μέν . . . δέ</span> is used in successive clauses which contain either the same word (_anaphora_) or a synonymous word; as <span lang="grc">ἐγὼ δὲ σύνειμι μὲν θεοῖς, σύνειμι δὲ ἀνθρώποις τοῖς ἀγαθοῖς</span> quoted in 1159, <span lang="grc">ἦλθε μὲν καὶ ἀπὸ τῆς Ἐρυθραίᾱς ἀγγελίᾱ, ἀφῑκνεῖτο δὲ καὶ πανταχόθεν</span> _news came from the district of Erythrae itself and arrived also from all quarters_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 3.33">T. 3.33</cite>. But <span lang="grc">μέν</span> is sometimes omitted, as <span lang="grc">στήσω σ᾽ ἄγων, στήσω δ᾽ ἐμαυτόν</span> _I will bring thee and stablish thee, and I will stablish myself_ <cite data-ref="Soph. OC 1342">S. O. C. 1342</cite>.

##### <a id="s2907"></a> §2907

If more than two clauses are contrasted, only the first clause has <span lang="grc">μέν</span>, while each of the following clauses has <span lang="grc">δέ</span> (<cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 1.3.14">X. A. 1.3.14</cite>, <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 4.2.28">X. C. 4.2.28</cite>).

##### <a id="s2908"></a> §2908

A contrast indicated by <span lang="grc">μέν</span> and <span lang="grc">δέ</span> may stand inside another contrast indicated in the same manner, as <span lang="grc">ὁ μὲν ἀνὴρ τοιαῦτα μὲν πεποίηκε, τοιαῦτα δὲ λέγει· ὑ̄μῶν δὲ σὺ πρῶτος, ὦ Κλέαρχε, ἀπόφηναι γνώμην ὅ τι σοι δοκεῖ</span> _the man has acted thus_, _and speaks thus; but do you_, _Clearchus_, _be the first to make known what you think best_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 1.6.9">X. A. 1.6.9</cite>.

##### <a id="s2909"></a> §2909

Two relative (or conditional) clauses each with <span lang="grc">μέν</span> may be followed <!-- page 657 --> by two demonstrative clauses each with <span lang="grc">δέ</span>; but the second <span lang="grc">δέ</span> is usually omitted, and there are other variations. Thus, <span lang="grc">ὁπόσοι μὲν . . . οὗτοι μὲν . . . ὁπόσοι δὲ . . . τούτους ὁρῶ</span> <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 3.1.43">X. A. 3.1.43</cite>, cp. <cite data-ref="Xen. Ec. 4.7">X. O. 4.7</cite>, <cite data-ref="Plat. Apol. 28e">P. A. 28e</cite>.

##### <a id="s2910"></a> §2910

A clause with <span lang="grc">μέν</span> is often followed by a contrasted clause without <span lang="grc">δέ</span> but with a particle containing an element of opposition, as <span lang="grc">πρῶτον μὲν . . . ἔπειτα . . . εἶτα</span>.

##### <a id="s2911"></a> §2911

A shift in the construction may cause <span lang="grc">δέ</span> to be omitted (<cite data-ref="Soph. Ant. 1199">S. Ant. 1199</cite>).

##### <a id="s2912"></a> §2912

<span lang="grc">μέν</span> after an emphatic demonstrative may resume <span lang="grc">μέν</span> of the antecedent clause (<cite data-ref="Dem. 2.18">D. 2.18</cite>).

##### <a id="s2913"></a> §2913

<span lang="grc"> **μὲν . . . τε** </span> (and even <span lang="grc">καί</span>) is used where the second clause is merely _added_ instead of being coördinated by means of <span lang="grc">δέ</span>. Thus, <span lang="grc">ταχὺ μὲν ὅποι ἔδει περιγιγνόμεθα ἀθρόοι τε τῷ ἄρχοντι ἑπόμενοι ἀνυπόστατοι ἦμεν</span> _we have quickly reached the places to which we had to go, and by following our leader in a compact body we have been invincible_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 8.1.3">X. C. 8.1.3</cite>.

##### <a id="s2914"></a> §2914

**Position of <span lang="grc">μέν</span>** (**and <span lang="grc">δέ</span>**).—<span lang="grc">μέν</span> and <span lang="grc">δέ</span> are commonly placed next to the words they contrast, and take precedence over other postpositive particles. But when two words belong closely together, <span lang="grc">μέν</span> and <span lang="grc">δέ</span> are placed between. Thus, when nouns with the article are contrasted, <span lang="grc">μέν</span> and <span lang="grc">δέ</span> stand after the article; if the nouns depend on prepositions <span lang="grc">μέν</span> and <span lang="grc">δέ</span> stand after the preposition and before the article.

**a.** But this rule may be neglected in order to emphasize the preceding word, as <span lang="grc">τὰ μὲν ἀνθρώπινα παρέντες, τὰ δαιμόνια δὲ σκοποῦντες</span> _neglecting human affairs_, _but speculating on things divine_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Mem. 1.1.12">X. M. 1.1.12</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἀνὰ τὸ σκοτεινὸν μέν</span> _in the darkness_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 3.22">T. 3.22</cite>.

**b.** If the noun has no article and is governed by a preposition, <span lang="grc">δέ</span> usually takes the third place.

**c.** Postponement of <span lang="grc">δέ</span> (and some other postpositive particles) to the fourth place is only _apparent_ after an introductory vocative, which is not regarded as forming an integral part of the sentence.

##### <a id="s2915"></a> §2915

<span lang="grc">μέν</span> and <span lang="grc">δέ</span> are sometimes referred to the entire clause or to the predicate and not to the words that are opposed to each other. This arrangement is often adopted to preserve the symmetry of the juxtaposed clause. <span lang="grc">μέν</span> and <span lang="grc">δέ</span> are thus often placed after personal or demonstrative pronouns. Thus, <span lang="grc">ἔλεγε μὲν ὡς τὸ πολύ, τοῖς δὲ βουλομένοις ἐξῆν ἀκούειν</span> Socrates _for the most part was wont to talk, while any who chose could listen_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Mem. 1.1.10">X. M. 1.1.10</cite>, <span lang="grc">πῶς ἂν πολλοὶ μὲν ἐπεθύ̄μουν τυραννεῖν . . . ; πῶς δὲ πάντες ἐζήλουν ἂν τοὺς τυράννους</span>; _why should many desire to possess despotic power? why should everybody envy despotic rulers?_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Hiero 1.9">X. Hi. 1.9</cite> (for <span lang="grc">πάντες δὲ πῶς ἐζήλουν ἄν</span>). Cp. <span lang="grc">ἐν μὲν τούτοις . . . ἐν ἐκείνοις δέ</span> Lyc. 140, <span lang="grc">περὶ αὑτῶν μὲν . . . περὶ δὲ τῶν δεσποτῶν</span> <cite data-ref="Lys. 7.35">L. 7.35</cite>, etc.

**a.** The transposition is often designed to produce a chiastic ([3020](../37-some-grammatical-and-rhetorical-figures.md#s3020)) order, as <span lang="grc">ἔπαθε μὲν οὐδέν, πολλὰ δὲ κακὰ ἐνόμιζε ποιῆσαι</span> _he suffered no loss, but thought that he had done a great deal of damage_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 3.4.2">X. A. 3.4.2</cite> (here <span lang="grc">οὐδέν</span> and <span lang="grc">πολλά</span> are brought close together).

##### <a id="s2916"></a> §2916

In poetry <span lang="grc">μέν</span> and <span lang="grc">δέ</span> often have a freer position than in prose. <span lang="grc">δέ</span> may often come _third_ when an emphatic word is placed before it, and even _fourth_. <!-- page 658 -->
