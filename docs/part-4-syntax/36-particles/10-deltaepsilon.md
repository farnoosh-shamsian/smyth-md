# PARTICLES: δέ

##### <a id="s2834"></a> §2834

<span lang="grc"> **δέ** </span> (postpositive) was originally an adverb with a force not unlike that of _on the other hand, on the contrary;_ later it became a conjunction commonly represented by _but_ or _and_, which are, however, mere makeshifts of translation. <span lang="grc">δέ</span> serves to mark that something is different from what precedes, but only to offset it, not to exclude or contradict it; it denotes only a slight contrast, and is therefore weaker than <span lang="grc">ἀλλά</span>, but stronger than <span lang="grc">καί. δέ</span> is adversative and copulative; but the two uses are not always clearly to be distinguished.

##### <a id="s2835"></a> §2835

**Adversative <span lang="grc">δέ</span>** often marks a silent contrast, as at the beginning of speeches (<span lang="grc">ἐγὼ δὲ οὕτω γιγνώσκω</span> <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 4.6.10">X. A. 4.6.10</cite>); in questions which imply opposition to something just said (<cite data-ref="Soph. OC 57">S. O. C. 57</cite>); in answers (<cite data-ref="Soph. OT 379">S. O. T. 379</cite>); in objections or corrections (<cite data-ref="Soph. Ant. 517">S. Ant. 517</cite>); in <span lang="grc">τὸ δέ, τὰ δέ</span> _on the contrary, whereas really_, where a true opinion is opposed to a false one; similarly in <span lang="grc">νῦν δέ</span> _but in fact, but as the case stands_. When <span lang="grc">δέ</span> is balanced by <span lang="grc">μέν</span> ([2904](21-muepsilonnu.md#s2904)) it is antithetical rather than adversative.

**a.** <span lang="grc">δέ</span> after a pronoun following a vocative produces a pause; as <span lang="grc">Νιόβᾱ σὲ δ᾽ ἔγωγε νέμω θεόν</span> _ah Niobe_, _thee I regard as divine_ <cite data-ref="Soph. El. 150">S. El. 150</cite>.

**b.** <span lang="grc">δέ</span> instead of <span lang="grc">ἀλλά</span> is rare except in the poets and Thucydides. Thus, <span lang="grc">προμηνύ̄σῃς γε τοῦτο μηδενὶ τοὖργον, κρυφῆ δὲ κεῦθε</span> _make known this plan to no one_, _but hide it in secret_ <cite data-ref="Soph. Ant. 85">S. Ant. 85</cite>, <span lang="grc">οὐκ ἐπὶ κακῷ, ἐπ᾽ ἐλευθερώσει δὲ τῶν Ἑλλήνων παρελήλυθα</span> _I have come, not to harm, but to liberate, the Greeks_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 4.86">T. 4.86</cite>. Sometimes <span lang="grc">οὐ μέν</span> precedes when <span lang="grc">δέ</span> is used like <span lang="grc">ἀλλά&lt;\*&gt;</span>(<cite data-ref="Thuc. 1.50">T. 1.50</cite>).

**c.** _But not_ is <span lang="grc">ἀλλ᾽ ου᾽</span> or <span lang="grc">οὐ μέντοι</span>, not <span lang="grc">οὐ δέ</span>, in order to avoid confusion with <span lang="grc">οὐδέ</span> _nor_, _not even_. But <span lang="grc">οὐ</span> and <span lang="grc">δέ</span> may be separated, as <span lang="grc">οὐ βουλομένων δέ . . . προσχωρεῖν</span> _but since they did not wish to surrender_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Hell. 1.6.13">X. H. 1.6.13</cite>.

##### <a id="s2836"></a> §2836

**Copulative <span lang="grc">δέ</span>** marks transition, and is the ordinary particle used in connecting successive clauses or sentences which add something new or different, but not opposed, to what precedes, and are not joined by other particles, such as <span lang="grc">γάρ</span> or <span lang="grc">οὖν</span>.

Copulative <span lang="grc">δέ</span> is common in marking _continuation_, especially when something subordinate is added. Thus, when a new phase of a narrative is developed (<cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 1.2.7">X. A. 1.2.7</cite>-8); where attention is called to a new point or person (as in <span lang="grc">τί δ᾽ ἔστιν</span>;); when an interrupted speech or narrative is resumed (<cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 1.6.41">X. C. 1.6.41</cite>, <cite data-ref="Soph. Trach. 281">S. Tr. 281</cite>); where a second relationship is added (<span lang="grc">μήτηρ βασιλέως, βασίλεια δ᾽ ἐμή</span> _the mother of the King_, _and my Queen_ <cite data-ref="Aesch. Pers. 151">A. Pers. 151</cite>, <span lang="grc">Ἠιόνα . . . Μενδαίων ἀποικίᾱν, πολεμίᾱν δὲ οὖσαν</span> he seized _Eïon_, _a colony of Mende_, _and which had been hostile_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 4.7">T. 4.7</cite>); <!-- page 645 --> when <span lang="grc">δέ</span> has a force like that of <span lang="grc">γάρ</span> (<cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 6.3.16">X. C. 6.3.16</cite>); and in <span lang="grc">καὶ . . . δέ</span> _and also_ (Epic <span lang="grc">καὶ δέ</span>), 2891.

##### <a id="s2837"></a> §2837

**Apodotic <span lang="grc">δέ</span>**.—The beginning of the principal clause (apodosis) of conditional and concessive sentences is often marked by <span lang="grc">δέ</span>. Apodotic <span lang="grc">δέ</span> is found also in the principal clause of causal, temporal, comparative, and relative sentences; and regularly gives greater emphasis to the main clause, which is thus distinctly set off against the subordinate clause. Apodotic <span lang="grc">δέ</span> is very common in Homer and Herodotus, not rare in Attic poetry, but infrequent in Attic prose, where it is used especially after an emphatic personal or demonstrative pronoun or when a participle represents the antecedent clause. Thus, <span lang="grc">εἷος ὁ ταῦθ᾽ ὥρμαινε . . ., ἦλθε δ᾽ Ἀθήνη</span> _while he was revolving these things, then came Athene_ A 193, <span lang="grc">εἰ οὖν ἐγὼ μὴ γιγνώσκω μήτε τὰ ὅσια μήτε τὰ δίκαια, ὑ̄μεῖς δὲ διδάξετέ με</span> _accordingly if I have no knowledge either of what is holy or what is just, do you then instruct me_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Hell. 4.1.33">X. H. 4.1.33</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἐπεὶ τοίνυν οὐ δύναμαί σε πείθειν μὴ ἐκθεῖναι, σὺ δὲ ὧδε ποίησον</span> _since therefore I am not able to persuade you not to expose it, do you then do as follows_ <cite data-ref="Hdt. 1.112">Hdt. 1.112</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἐκάθευδον . . . ὥσπερ οἱ ὁπλῖται οὕτω δὲ καὶ οἱ πελτασταί</span> _as the hoplites so also the peltasts sleep_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 8.5.12">X. C. 8.5.12</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἐπειδὴ δὲ ἀφικόμενοι μάχῃ ἐκράτησαν . . ., φαίνονται δ᾽ οὐδ᾽ ἐνταῦθα πά̄σῃ τῇ δυνάμει χρησάμενοι</span> _but when on their arrival they had conquered in battle, not even then did they appear to have made use of their entire force_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 1.11">T. 1.11</cite>, <span lang="grc">καί ποτε ὄντος πάγου . . . οὗτος δ᾽ ἐν τούτοις ἐξῄει</span> _and once when there was a frost he went out in the midst of this_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Sym. 220b">P. S. 220b</cite>.

**a.** Apodotic <span lang="grc">δέ</span> often resumes a <span lang="grc">δέ</span> in the subordinate clause and carries on the opposition expressed by that clause; as <span lang="grc">εἰ δὲ βούλεσθε . . . ἐκλεξάμενοι ὅποι ἂν βούλησθε κατασχεῖν . . ., πλοῖα δ᾽ ὑ̄μῖν πάρεστιν</span> _but if you wish to select_ some place _wherever you please and take possession of it, you have ships at command_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 5.6.20">X. A. 5.6.20</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἃ δ᾽ αἰσχύ̄νην ἡμῖν φέρει . . ., ταῦτα δὲ κατὰ χώρᾱν μένει</span> _but_ the terms _which cause us shame, these remain in force_ <cite data-ref="Isoc. 4.176">I. 4.176</cite>.

**b.** The use of apodotic <span lang="grc">δέ</span> should not be regarded as a survival of original _coördination_.

##### <a id="s2838"></a> §2838

**<span lang="grc">δέ</span> without <span lang="grc">μέν</span>**.—A clause with <span lang="grc">δέ</span> often has no correlative particle in the clause with which it is contrasted. Here <span lang="grc">μέν</span> is not used because the opposition in the first clause was too weak, or because the speaker did not intend to announce a following contrast or did not think he was going to use a contrasted <span lang="grc">δέ</span> clause. Sometimes the entire first clause may have to be supplied in thought from the general connection or from what has gone before. <span lang="grc">δέ</span> without <span lang="grc">μέν</span> in such cases is common in poetry, but not rare in prose, even in brief antitheses, as <span lang="grc">ἃ πάντες ἀεὶ γλίχονται λέγειν, ἀξίως δ᾽ οὐδεὶς εἰπεῖν δεδύνηται</span> exploits _which everybody continually desires to recount, but which no one has been able to set forth adequately_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 6.11">D. 6.11</cite>. See also [2835](#s2835).

**a.** When a relative construction passes over into a construction with a personal or demonstrative pronoun, the relative clause usually has no <span lang="grc">μέν</span>. Cp. Soph. Aj. 457, quoted in 2517.

**b.** <span lang="grc">οἱ δέ</span>, when opposed to a larger number of persons or things, is often used without <span lang="grc">οἱ μέν</span>, as <span lang="grc">προεληλυθότες ἐπὶ χῑλόν, οἱ δ᾽ ἐπὶ ξύλα</span> _having gone for fodder_, _and some for fuel_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 6.3.9">X. C. 6.3.9</cite>. <!-- page 646 -->

##### <a id="s2839"></a> §2839

**<span lang="grc">δέ</span> with other Particles**.—For example:

<span lang="grc"> **δ᾽ ἄρα** </span>, which sometimes follows <span lang="grc">μέν</span>.

<span lang="grc"> **δ᾽ αὖ** </span> and <span lang="grc"> **ὅμως δέ** </span> mark stronger opposition than <span lang="grc">δέ</span> alone.

<span lang="grc"> **δὲ δή** </span> _but then, but now, well but_ is often used in passing to a new point. In Aristophanes this collocation is used almost always in questions.
