# THE CASES: Introductory

<!-- page 312 -->

##### <a id="s1279"></a> §1279

Of the cases belonging to the Indo-European language, Greek has lost the free use of three: instrumental, locative, and ablative. A few of the _forms_ of these cases have been preserved ([341](../../part-2-inflection/07-adverbs.md#s341), [1449](05-free-uses-of-the-genitive.md#s1449), [1535](06-dative.md#s1535)); the syntactical _functions_ of the instrumental and locative were taken over by the dative; those of the ablative by the genitive. The genitive and dative cases are therefore _composite_ or _mixed_ cases.

N.—The reasons that led to the formation of _composite_ cases are either (1) formal or (2) functional. Thus (1) <span lang="grc">χώρᾳ</span> is both dat. and loc.; <span lang="grc">λόγοις</span> represents the instr. <span lang="grc">λόγοις</span> and the loc. <span lang="grc">λόγοισι</span>; in consonantal stems both ablative and genitive ended in <span lang="grc">-ος</span>; (2) _verbs of ruling_ may take either the dat. or the loc., hence the latter case would be absorbed by the former; furthermore the use of prepositions especially with loc. and instr. was attended by a certain indifference as regards the form of the case.

##### <a id="s1280"></a> §1280

Through the influence of one construction upon another it often becomes impossible to mark off the later from the original use of the genitive and dative. It must be remembered that since language is a natural growth and Greek was spoken and written before formal categories were set up by Grammar, all the uses of the cases cannot be apportioned with definiteness.

##### <a id="s1281"></a> §1281

The cases fall into two main divisions. Cases of the Subject: nominative (and vocative). Cases of the Predicate: accusative, dative. The genitive may define either the subject (with nouns) or the predicate (with verbs). On the nominative, see [938](../08-case-of-the-subject-the-nominative.md#s938) ff.

##### <a id="s1282"></a> §1282

The content of a thought may be expressed in different ways in different languages. Thus, <span lang="grc">πείθω σε</span>, but _persuadeo tibi_ (in classical Latin): and even in the same language, the same verb may have varying constructions to express different shades of meaning.
