# THE CASES: ACCUSATIVE

##### <a id="s1551"></a> §1551

The accusative is a form of defining or qualifying the verb.

**a.** The accusative derives its name from a mistranslation (_casus accusativus_) of the Greek (<span lang="grc">ἡ αἰτιᾱτικὴ πτῶσις</span>, properly _casus effectivus_, 1554 a).

##### <a id="s1552"></a> §1552

A noun stands in the accusative when the idea it expresses is most <!-- page 354 --> immediately (in contrast to the dative) and most completely (in contrast to the genitive) under the influence of the verbal conception (in contrast to the nominative).

##### <a id="s1553"></a> §1553

The accusative is the case of the direct object ([919](../02-syntax-of-the-simple-sentence.md#s919)). The accusative is used with all transitive verbs (and with some intransitive verbs used transitively), with some verbal nouns, and with adjectives.

##### <a id="s1554"></a> §1554

The direct object is of two kinds:

**a.** The internal object (object effected): <span lang="grc">ὁ ἀνὴρ τύπτει πολλὰ̄ς πληγά̄ς</span> _the man strikes many blows_.

N. 1.—Here the object is already contained (or implied) in the verb, and its addition is optional. The accusative of the internal object is sometimes called the accusative of content. The object stands in apposition to the result of the verbal action. The effect produced by the verb is either (1) transient, when the object is a _nomen actionis_, and disappears with the operation of the verb, as in <span lang="grc">μάχην μάχεσθαι</span> _to fight a battle_, or (2) permanent, and remains after the verbal action has ceased, as in <span lang="grc">τεῖχος τειχίζειν</span> _to build a wall_. The latter form is the accusative of result ([1578](#s1578)).

N. 2.—Almost any verb may take one of the varieties of the internal object.

**b.** The external object (object affected): <span lang="grc">ὁ ἀνὴρ τύπτει τὸν παῖδα</span> _the man strikes the boy_.

N.—Here the object is not contained in the verb, but is necessary to explain or define the character of the action in question. The external object stands outside the verbal action.

##### <a id="s1555"></a> §1555

Many verbs may take an accusative either of the external or of the internal object: <span lang="grc">τέμνειν ὕ̄λην</span> _fell timber_, <span lang="grc">τέμνειν τὰ̄ς τρίχας</span> _cut off the hair_, <span lang="grc">τέμνειν ὁδόν</span> _open a road_, but <span lang="grc">σπονδὰ̄ς</span> or <span lang="grc">ὅρκια τέμνειν</span>, with a specialized verbal idea, _to make a treaty_ by slaying a victim (pass. <span lang="grc">ὅρκια ἐτμήθη</span>), <span lang="grc">τέμνειν ὁδόν</span> _make one's way_ (poet.), <span lang="grc">τειχίζειν χωρίον</span> _fortify a place_, but <span lang="grc">τειχίζειν τεῖχος</span> _build a wall_. Cp. <cite data-ref="Eur. Supp. 1060">E. Supp. 1060</cite>: A. <span lang="grc">νῑκῶσα νί̄κην τίνα; μαθεῖν χρῄζω σέθεν</span>. B. <span lang="grc">πά̄σᾱς γυναῖκας, κτλ</span>. A. _Victorious in what victory? This I would learn of thee_. B. _Over all women_. Here the construction shifts from the internal to the external object.

##### <a id="s1556"></a> §1556

The direct object of an active transitive verb becomes the subject of the passive: <span lang="grc">ὁ παῖς ὑπὸ τοῦ ἀνδρὸς τύπτεται</span> _the boy is struck by the man_.

**a.** The object of a verb governing the genitive or dative as principal object may also become the subject of the passive ([1340](04-genitive-with-verbs.md#s1340)).

##### <a id="s1557"></a> §1557

In Greek many verbs are transitive the ordinary English equivalents of which are intransitive and require a preposition. So <span lang="grc">σιωπᾶν τι, σῑγᾶν τι</span> _to keep silence about something_.

##### <a id="s1558"></a> §1558

Many verbs that are usually intransitive are also used transitively in Greek. Thus, <span lang="grc">ἀσεβεῖν</span> _sin against_, <span lang="grc">δυσχεραίνειν</span> _be disgusted at_, <span lang="grc">χαίρειν</span> _rejoice at_, <span lang="grc">ἥδεσθαι</span> _be pleased at_, <span lang="grc">δακρύ̄ειν</span> _weep for_. Cp. [1595 b](#s1595). <!-- page 355 -->

**a.** Poetical: <span lang="grc">ᾁσσειν</span> _agitate_, <span lang="grc">περᾶν πόδα</span> _pass on her way_ <cite data-ref="Eur. Hec. 53">E. Hec. 53</cite>, <span lang="grc">πλεῖν</span> _sail_, <span lang="grc">κροταλίζειν</span> _rattle along_ (<span lang="grc">κροτεῖν</span> _strike_ <cite data-ref="Hdt. 6.58">Hdt. 6.58</cite>), <span lang="grc">λάμπειν</span> _make shine_, <span lang="grc">χορεύειν θεόν, ἑλίσσειν θεόν</span> _celebrate the god by choruses, by dancing_.

##### <a id="s1559"></a> §1559

Many intransitive verbs are used transitively when compounded with a preposition, e.g. <span lang="grc">ἀναμάχεσθαι</span> _fight over again_.—<span lang="grc">ἀπομάχεσθαι</span> _drive off_, <span lang="grc">ἀποστρέφεσθαι</span> _abandon_, <span lang="grc">ἀποχωρεῖν</span> _leave_.—<span lang="grc">διαβαίνειν</span> _pass over_, <span lang="grc">διαπλεῖν</span> _sail across_, <span lang="grc">διεξέρχεσθαι</span> _go through_.—<span lang="grc">εἰσιέναι</span> _come into the mind_, <span lang="grc">εἰσπλεῖν</span> _said into_.—<span lang="grc">ἐκβαίνειν</span> _pass_, <span lang="grc">ἐκτρέπεσθαι</span> _get out of the way of_, <span lang="grc">ἐξαναχωρεῖν</span> _shun_, <span lang="grc">ἐξίστασθαι</span> _avoid_.— <span lang="grc">ἐπιστρατεύειν</span> _march against_.—<span lang="grc">καταναυμαχεῖν</span> _beat at sea_, <span lang="grc">καταπολεμεῖν</span> _subdue completely_, <span lang="grc">καταπολῑτεύεσθαι</span> _reduce by policy_.—<span lang="grc">μετέρχεσθαι</span> _seek_, _pursue_, <span lang="grc">μετιέναι</span> _go in quest of_.—<span lang="grc">παραβαίνειν</span> _transgress_.—<span lang="grc">περιιέναι</span> _go round_, <span lang="grc">περιίστασθαι</span> _surround_.—<span lang="grc">προσοικεῖν</span> _dwell in_, <span lang="grc">προσπαίζειν</span> _sing in praise of_.—<span lang="grc">ὑπερβαίνειν</span> _omit_.— <span lang="grc">ὑπεξέρχεσθαι</span> _escape from_.—<span lang="grc">ὑπέρχεσθαι</span> _fawn on_, <span lang="grc">ὑποδύ̄εσθαι</span> _withstand_, <span lang="grc">ὑποχωρεῖν</span> _shun_, <span lang="grc">ὑφίστασθαι</span> _withstand_.

##### <a id="s1560"></a> §1560

Conversely, many verbs that are usually transitive are used intransitively (with gen., dat., or with a preposition). Some of these are mentioned in 1591, 1592, 1595. Sometimes there is a difference in meaning, as <span lang="grc">ἀρέσκειν</span> = _satisfy_, with accus., = _please_, with dat.

##### <a id="s1561"></a> §1561

The same verb may be used transitively or intransitively, often with little difference of signification. Cp. [1709](../20-the-verb-voices/02-active-voice.md#s1709). This is generally indicated in the treatment of the cases, e.g. <span lang="grc">αἰσθάνεσθαί τι</span> or <span lang="grc">τινος</span> _perceive something_, <span lang="grc">ἐνθῡμεῖσθαί τι</span> or <span lang="grc">τινι</span> _consider something_, <span lang="grc">μέμφεσθαί τινα</span> or <span lang="grc">τινι</span> _blame some one_.

##### <a id="s1562"></a> §1562

On <span lang="grc">δεῖ μοί τινος</span> and <span lang="grc">δεῖ μέ τινος</span> see [1400](05-free-uses-of-the-genitive.md#s1400). With the inf. the accus. is usual (dat. and inf. <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 3.4.35">X. A. 3.4.35</cite>). <span lang="grc">χρή μέ τινος</span> is poetical; with the inf. <span lang="grc">χρή</span> takes the accus. (except <cite data-ref="Lys. 28.10">L. 28.10</cite>, where some read <span lang="grc">δικαίους</span>). (<span lang="grc">χρή</span> is an old noun; cp. <span lang="grc">χρεώ, χρεία</span> _need_ and 793.)

## **INTERNAL OBJECT** (**OBJECT EFFECTED**)

### COGNATE ACCUSATIVE

##### <a id="s1563"></a> §1563

The cognate accusative is of two kinds, of which the second is an extension of the first.

##### <a id="s1564"></a> §1564

(I) The substantive in the accusative is of the _same origin_ as the verb.

<span lang="grc">πολλὴν φλυᾱρίᾱν φλυᾱροῦντα</span> _talking much nonsense_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Apol. 19c">P. A. 19c</cite>, <span lang="grc">ξυνέφυγε τὴν φυγὴν ταύτην</span> _he shared in the recent exile_ 21 a, <span lang="grc">τὴν ἐν Σαλαμῖνι ναυμαχίᾱν ναυμαχήσαντες</span> _victorious in the sea-fight at Salamis_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 59.97">D. 59.97</cite>, <span lang="grc">τὰ̄ς ὑποσχέσεις ἃ̄ς οὗτος ὑπῑσχνεῖτο</span> _the promises which he made_ 19. 47, <span lang="grc">ἡ αἰτίᾱ ἣν αἰτιῶνται</span> _the charge they bring_ <cite data-ref="Antiph. 6.27">Ant. 6.27</cite>.

**a.** Sometimes the verb may be suppressed, as <span lang="grc">ἡμῖν μὲν εὐχὰ̄ς τά̄σδε</span> (<span lang="grc">εὔχομαι</span>) _for us these prayers_ <cite data-ref="Aesch. Lib. 142">A. Ch. 142</cite>.

##### <a id="s1565"></a> §1565

The cognate accusative occurs even with adjectives of an intransitive character: <span lang="grc">μήτε τι σοφὸς ὢν τὴν ἐκείνων σοφίᾱν μήτε ἀμαθὴς τὴν ἀμαθίᾱν</span> _being neither at all wise after the fashion of their wisdom nor ignorant after the fashion of their ignorance_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Apol. 22e">P. A. 22e</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἀτί̄μους ἐποίησαν ἀτῑμίᾱν τοιά̄νδε ὥστε κτλ</span>. <!-- page 356 --> _they disfranchised them in such a way that_, etc. <cite data-ref="Thuc. 5.34">T. 5.34</cite> (<span lang="grc">ἀτί̄μους ἐποίησαν ῀ ἠτί̄μησαν</span>, cp. [1598](#s1598)).

##### <a id="s1566"></a> §1566

**Passive**: <span lang="grc">πόλεμος ἐπολεμεῖτο</span> _war was waged_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Hell. 4.8.1">X. H. 4.8.1</cite>.

##### <a id="s1567"></a> §1567

(II) The substantive in the accusative is of _kindred meaning_ with the verb.

<span lang="grc">ἐξῆλθον ἄλλᾱς ὁδούς</span> _they went forth on other expeditions_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Hell. 1.2.17">X. H. 1.2.17</cite>, <span lang="grc">τὸν ἱερὸν καλούμενον πόλεμον ἐστράτευσαν</span> _they waged what is called the Sacred War_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 1.112">T. 1.112</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἠσθένησε ταύτην τὴν νόσον</span> _he fell ill of this disease_ <cite data-ref="Isoc. 19.24">I. 19.24</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἀνθρώπου φύσιν βλαστών</span> _born to man's estate_ <cite data-ref="Soph. Aj. 760">S. Aj. 760</cite>.

##### <a id="s1568"></a> §1568

**Passive**: <span lang="grc">πόλεμος ἐταράχθη</span> _war was stirred up_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 18.151">D. 18.151</cite>.

##### <a id="s1569"></a> §1569

An extension of the cognate accusative appears in poetry with <span lang="grc">κεῖσθαι, στῆναι, καθίζειν</span> and like verbs: <span lang="grc">τόπον, ὅντινα κεῖται</span> _the place in which he is situated_ <cite data-ref="Soph. Phil. 145">S. Ph. 145</cite>, <span lang="grc">τί ἕστηκε πέτρᾱν</span>; _why stands she on the rock?_ <cite data-ref="Eur. Supp. 987">E. Supp. 987</cite>, <span lang="grc">τρίποδα καθίζων</span> _sitting on the tripod_ <cite data-ref="Eur. Orest. 956">E. Or. 956</cite>.

##### <a id="s1570"></a> §1570

An attributive word is usually necessary (but not in Hom.); otherwise the addition of the substantive to the verb would be tautologous. But the attribute is omitted:

**a.** When the nominal idea is specialized: <span lang="grc">φυλακὰ̄ς φυλάττειν</span> _to stand sentry_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 2.6.10">X. A. 2.6.10</cite>, <span lang="grc">φόρον φέρειν</span> _to pay tribute_ 5. 5. 7.

**b.** When the substantive is restricted by the article: <span lang="grc">τὸν πόλεμον πολεμεῖν</span> _to wage the present war_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 8.58">T. 8.58</cite>, <span lang="grc">τὴν πομπὴν πέμπειν</span> _to conduct the procession_ 6. 56.

**c.** When a plural substantive denotes repeated occurrences: <span lang="grc">ἐτριηράρχησε τριηραρχίᾱς</span> _he performed the duty of trierarch_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 45.85">D. 45.85</cite>.

**d.** In various expressions: <span lang="grc">Ὀλύμπια νῑκᾶν</span> _to win an Olympian victory_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 1.126">T. 1.126</cite>, <span lang="grc">τὴν ναυμαχίᾱν νῑκῆσαι</span> _to be victorious in the sea-fight_ <cite data-ref="Lys. 19.28">L. 19.28</cite>, <span lang="grc">θύ̄ειν τὰ εὐαγγέλια</span> _to offer a sacrifice in honour of good news_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Hell. 1.6.37">X. H. 1.6.37</cite>.

**e.** In poetry the use of a substantive to denote a special form of the action of the verb is much extended: <span lang="grc">στάζειν αἷμα</span> _to drip_ (drops of) _blood_ <cite data-ref="Soph. Phil. 783">S. Ph. 783</cite>, <span lang="grc">Ἄρηπνεῖν</span> _to breathe war_ <cite data-ref="Aesch. Ag. 375">A. Ag. 375</cite>, <span lang="grc">πῦρ δεδορκώς</span> _looking_ (a look of) _fire_ <cite data-ref="Hom. Od. 19.446"><span lang="grc">τ</span> 446</cite>. This use is common, especially in Aristophanes, with verbs signifying the _look_ of another than the speaker: <span lang="grc">βλέπειν νᾶπυ</span> _to look mustard_ Eq. 631, <span lang="grc">βλέπειν ἀπιστίᾱν</span> _to look unbelief_ Com. fr. 1. 341 (No. [309](../../part-2-inflection/04-substantives/08-declension-of-participles.md#s309)); cp. “looked his faith”: Holmes.

##### <a id="s1571"></a> §1571

The substantive without an attribute is (rarely) added to the verb as a more emphatic form of statement: <span lang="grc">λῆρον ληρεῖν</span> _to talk sheer nonsense_ Ar. Pl. 517, <span lang="grc">ὕβριν ὑβρίζειν</span> _to insult grievously_ <cite data-ref="Eur. Her. 708">E. H. F. 708</cite>. Often in Euripides.

##### <a id="s1572"></a> §1572

The substantive may be omitted, leaving only the adjectival attribute: <span lang="grc">παῖσον διπλῆν</span> (_scil_. <span lang="grc">πληγήν</span>) _strike twice_ (a double blow) <cite data-ref="Soph. El. 1415">S. El. 1415</cite>, <span lang="grc">τοῦτον ἀνέκραγον ὡς ὀλίγᾱς</span> (_scil_. <span lang="grc">πληγὰ̄ς</span>) <span lang="grc">παίσειεν</span> _they called out that he had dealt him_ too ([1063](../14-adjectives.md#s1063)) _few_ blows <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 5.8.12">X. A. 5.8.12</cite>. Cp. [1028](../14-adjectives.md#s1028).

##### <a id="s1573"></a> §1573

Usually an adjective, pronoun, or pronominal adjective is treated as a neuter substantive. Cp. <span lang="grc">μεγάλ᾽ ἁμαρτάνειν</span> _to commit grave errors_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 5.5">D. 5.5</cite> with <span lang="grc">μέγιστα ἁμαρτήματα ἁμαρτάνουσι</span> <cite data-ref="Plat. Gorg. 525d">P. G. 525d</cite>. The singular adjective is used in certain common phrases in prose, but is mainly poetical; the plural is ordinarily used in prose. <!-- page 357 -->

<span lang="grc">ἡδὺ γελᾶν</span> poet. (= <span lang="grc">ἡδὺν γέλωτα γελᾶν</span>) _to laugh sweetly_, <span lang="grc">μέγα</span> (<span lang="grc">ψεῦδος</span>) <span lang="grc">ψεύδεται</span> _he is a great liar_, <span lang="grc">μέγα φρονήσᾱς ἐπὶ τούτῳ</span> _highly elated at this_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 3.1.27">X. A. 3.1.27</cite>, <span lang="grc">μεῖζον φρονεῖ</span> _he is too proud_ 5. 6. 8, <span lang="grc">τὰ τῶν Ἑλλήνων φρονεῖν</span> _to be on the side of the Greeks_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 14.34">D. 14.34</cite>, <span lang="grc">μέγιστον ἐδύναντο</span> _had the greatest influence_ <cite data-ref="Lys. 30.14">L. 30.14</cite>, <span lang="grc">δεινὰ ὑβρίζειν</span> _to maltreat terribly_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 6.4.2">X. A. 6.4.2</cite>, <span lang="grc">ταὐτὰ ἐπρεσβεύομεν</span> _we fulfilled our mission as ambassadors in the same way_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 19.32">D. 19.32</cite>, <span lang="grc">τί βούλεται ἡμῖν χρῆσθαι</span>; _what use does he wish to make of us?_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 1.3.18">X. A. 1.3.18</cite> (= <span lang="grc">τίνα βούλεται χρείᾱν χρῆσθαι</span>, cp. <span lang="grc">χρῆσθαί τινι χρείαν</span> <cite data-ref="Plat. Laws 868b">P. L. 868b</cite>).

##### <a id="s1574"></a> §1574

**Passive**: <span lang="grc">τοῦτο οὐκ ἐψεύσθησαν</span> _they were not deceived in this_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 2.2.13">X. A. 2.2.13</cite>, <span lang="grc">ταῦτα οὐδεὶς ἂν πεισθείη</span> _no one would be persuaded of this_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Laws 836d">P. L. 836d</cite>.

##### <a id="s1575"></a> §1575

For a cognate accusative in conjunction with a second object, see [1620](#s1620).

##### <a id="s1576"></a> §1576

Note the expressions <span lang="grc">δικάζειν δίκην</span> _decide a case_, <span lang="grc">δικάζεσθαι δίκην τινί</span> _go to law with somebody_, <span lang="grc">διώκειν γραφήν τινα</span> _indict somebody_, <span lang="grc">φεύγειν δίκην τινός</span> _be put on one's trial for something;_ <span lang="grc">γράφεσθαί τινα γραφήν</span> _indict one for a public offence_, <span lang="grc">φεύγειν γραφήν</span> _be put on one's trial for a public offence_. Also <span lang="grc">ἀγωνίζεσθαι στάδιον</span> (= <span lang="grc">ἀγῶνα σταδίου</span>) _be a contestant in the race-course_, <span lang="grc">νῑκᾶν στάδιον</span> _be victorious in the race-course_, <span lang="grc">νῑκᾶν δίκην</span> _win a case_, <span lang="grc">νῑκᾶν γνώμην</span> _carry a resolution_ (pass. <span lang="grc">γνώμην ἡττᾶσθαι</span>), <span lang="grc">ὀφλεῖν δίκην</span> _lose a case_.

##### <a id="s1577"></a> §1577

The (rarer) dative (<span lang="grc">φόβῳ ταρβεῖν, βιαίῳ θανάτῳ ἀποθνῄσκειν, φεύγειν φυγῇ</span>) expresses the cause ([1517](06-dative.md#s1517)), manner ([1513](06-dative.md#s1513)), or means ([1507](06-dative.md#s1507)).

### ACCUSATIVE OF RESULT

##### <a id="s1578"></a> §1578

The accusative of result denotes the effect enduring after the verbal action has ceased.

<span lang="grc">ἕλκος οὐτάσαι</span> _to smite_ (and thus _make_) _a wound_ E 361 (so <span lang="grc">οὐλὴν ἐλαύνειν ψ</span> [74](../../part-1-letters-sounds-syllables-accent/02-euphony-of-vowels.md#s74)), <span lang="grc">πρεσβεύειν τὴν εἰρήνην</span> _to negotiate the peace_ (go as ambassadors (<span lang="grc">πρέσβεις</span>) to make the peace) <cite data-ref="Dem. 19.134">D. 19.134</cite>, but <span lang="grc">πρεσβεύειν πρεσβείᾱν</span> _to go on an embassy_ Dinarchus 1. 16, <span lang="grc">νόμισμα κόπτειν</span> _to coin money_ <cite data-ref="Hdt. 3.56">Hdt. 3.56</cite>, <span lang="grc">σπονδὰ̄ς</span>, or <span lang="grc">ὅρκια, τέμνειν</span> ([1555](#s1555)).

##### <a id="s1579"></a> §1579

Verbs signifying _to effect anything_ (<span lang="grc">αἴρειν</span> _raise_, <span lang="grc">αὔξειν</span> _exalt_, <span lang="grc">διδάσκειν</span> _teach_, <span lang="grc">τρέφειν</span> _rear_, <span lang="grc">παιδεύειν</span> _train_) show the result of their action upon a substantive or adjective predicate to the direct object: <span lang="grc">σὲ Θῆβαί γ᾽ οὐκ ἐπαίδευσαν κακόν</span> _Thebes did not train thee to be base_ <cite data-ref="Soph. OC 919">S. O. C. 919</cite>, <span lang="grc">τοῦτον τρέφειν τε καὶ αὔξειν μέγαν</span> _to nurse and exalt him into greatness_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Rep. 565c">P. R. 565c</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἐποικοδομήσαντες αὐτὸ ὑψηλότερον</span> _raising it higher_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 7.4">T. 7.4</cite>. Such predicate nouns are called _proleptic_. Passive: <span lang="grc">μέγας ἐκ μῑκροῦ Φίλιππος ηὔξηται</span> _Philip has grown from a mean to be a mighty person_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 9.21">D. 9.21</cite>. Cp. [1613](#s1613).

### ACCUSATIVE OF EXTENT

##### <a id="s1580"></a> §1580

The accusative denotes extent in space and time.

##### <a id="s1581"></a> §1581

**Space**.—The accusative denotes the space or way _over which_ an action is extended, and the measure of the space traversed.

<span lang="grc">ἄγειν</span> (<span lang="grc">στρατιὰ̄ν</span>) <span lang="grc">στενὰ̄ς ὁδούς</span> _to lead an army over narrow roads_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 1.6.43">X. C. 1.6.43</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἐξελαύνει σταθμοὺς τρεῖς, παρασάγγᾱς εἴκοσι καὶ δύο</span> _he advances three stages, twenty-_ <!-- page 358 --> _two parasangs_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 1.2.5">X. A. 1.2.5</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἀπέχει ἡ Πλάταια τῶν Θηβῶν σταδίους ἑβδομήκοντα</span> _Plataea is seventy stades distant from Thebes_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 2.5">T. 2.5</cite>.

**a.** This use is analogous to the cognate accusative after verbs of motion (<span lang="grc">ἐξόδους ἐξελθεῖν, πλεῖν θάλατταν</span>).

##### <a id="s1582"></a> §1582

**Time**.—The accusative denotes extent of time.

<span lang="grc">ἔμεινεν ἡμέρᾱς ἑπτά</span> _he remained seven days_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 1.2.6">X. A. 1.2.6</cite>, <span lang="grc">ξυμμαχίᾱν ἐποιήσαντο ἑκατὸν ἔτη</span> _they made an alliance for a hundred years_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 3.114">T. 3.114</cite>.

##### <a id="s1583"></a> §1583

The accusative of time implies that the action of the verb covers the _entire_ period. When emphasis is laid on the uninterrupted duration of an action, <span lang="grc">παρά</span> with the accusative ([1692](../19-prepositions.md#s1692). 3. b) and <span lang="grc">διά</span> with the genitive ([1685](../19-prepositions.md#s1685). 1. b) are used. The accusative of time is rarely employed where the dative ([1540](06-dative.md#s1540)) is properly in place: <span lang="grc">τήνδε τὴν ἡμέρᾱν</span> <cite data-ref="Aeschin. 3.7">Aes. 3.7</cite>.

##### <a id="s1584"></a> §1584

Duration of life may be expressed by <span lang="grc">γεγονώς</span>: <span lang="grc">ἔτη γεγονὼς ἑβδομήκοντα</span> _seventy years old_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Apol. 17d">P. A. 17d</cite>. (Also by <span lang="grc">εἶναι</span> and the genitive, 1327.)

##### <a id="s1585"></a> §1585

To mark (a) _how long_ a situation has lasted or (b) how much time has elapsed since something happened, an ordinal is used without the article, but often with the addition of <span lang="grc">οὑτοσί̄</span>. The current day or year is included. Thus (a) <span lang="grc">τὴν μητέρα τελευτήσᾱσαν τρίτον ἔτος τουτί̄</span> _my mother who died two years ago_ <cite data-ref="Lys. 24.6">L. 24.6</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἐπιδεδήμηκε τρίτην ἤδη ἡμέρᾱν</span> _he has been in the city since day before yesterday_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Prot. 309d">P. Pr. 309d</cite>. (b) <span lang="grc">ἀπηγγέλθη Φίλιππος τρίτον ἢ τέταρτον ἔτος τουτὶ̄ Ἡραῖον τεῖχος πολιορκῶν</span> _this is the third or fourth year since it was announced that Philip was besieging fort Heraeum_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 3.4">D. 3.4</cite>.

##### <a id="s1586"></a> §1586

On the accusative of extent in degree, see [1609](#s1609). With a comparative we find <span lang="grc">πολύ</span> and <span lang="grc">ὀλίγον</span> as well as <span lang="grc">πολλῷ</span> and <span lang="grc">ὀλίγῳ</span> ([1514](06-dative.md#s1514)); and always <span lang="grc">τί, τὶ, οὐδέν</span> with the comparative.

##### <a id="s1587"></a> §1587

Time and degree are often expressed by prepositions with the accusative. See Prepositions under <span lang="grc">ἀμφί, ἀνά, διά, ἐπί, κατά, παρά, πρός, ὑπό</span>.

### TERMINAL ACCUSATIVE (IN POETRY)

##### <a id="s1588"></a> §1588

In poetry after verbs of motion the accusative may be used without a preposition to express the goal.

<span lang="grc">ἄστυ Καδμεῖον μολών</span> _having come to the city of Cadmus_ <cite data-ref="Soph. OT 35">S. O. T. 35</cite>, <span lang="grc">πέμψομέν νιν Ἑλλάδα</span> _we will convey her to Greece_ <cite data-ref="Eur. Tro. 883">E. Tro. 883</cite>. Of _persons_ in Hom. (especially with <span lang="grc">ἱκνέομαι, ἵκω, ἱκά̄νω</span> = _reach_) and in the lyric parts of the drama: <span lang="grc">μνηστῆρας ἀφί̄κετο</span> _came unto the suitors_ <cite data-ref="Hom. Od. 1.332"><span lang="grc">α</span> 332</cite>. Cp. “arrived our coast”: Shakesp. In <cite data-ref="Hdt. 9.26">Hdt. 9.26</cite> <span lang="grc">φαμὲν ἡμέας ἱκνέεσθαι</span> means _we declare that it befits us_.

##### <a id="s1589"></a> §1589

The limit of motion is also expressed by <span lang="grc">-δε</span> (<span lang="grc">ἄστυδε</span> Hom., in prose, <span lang="grc">Ἀθήναζε ῀ Ἀθήνᾱς ¨ δε; χαμᾶζε</span> or <span lang="grc">χαμάζε ῀ χαμᾱς ¨ δε</span>, cp. <span lang="grc">χαμα-ί; οἴκαδε</span>) and, regularly in prose, by <span lang="grc">εἰς, ἐπί, παρά, πρός, ὡς</span> (with a person) with the accusative.

##### <a id="s1591"></a> §1591

(I) _To do anything to_ or _say anything of_ a person.

**a.** <span lang="grc">εὖ</span> (<span lang="grc">καλῶς</span>) <span lang="grc">ποιεῖν, δρᾶν</span> (rarely with <span lang="grc">πρά̄ττειν</span>), <span lang="grc">εὐεργετεῖν, ὀνινάναι, ὠφελεῖν</span> (also with dat.), <span lang="grc">θεραπεύειν, κακῶς ποιεῖν, κακοῦν, κακουργεῖν, βλάπτειν, ἀδικεῖν, ὑβρίζειν, βιάζεσθαι, ἀμείβεσθαι</span> _requite_, <span lang="grc">τῑμωρεῖσθαι</span> _punish_, <span lang="grc">λῡμαίνεσθαι</span> (also with dat.), <span lang="grc">λωβᾶσθαι</span> (also with dat.).

**b.** <span lang="grc">εὖ</span> (<span lang="grc">καλῶς</span>) <span lang="grc">λέγειν, εὐλογεῖν, κολακεύειν, θωπεύειν, προσκυνεῖν, κακῶς λέγειν, κακολογεῖν, κακηγορεῖν, λοιδορεῖν</span>.

<span lang="grc">συμφέρειν</span> and <span lang="grc">λῡσιτελεῖν</span> _profit_, <span lang="grc">βοηθεῖν</span> _help_, <span lang="grc">λοιδορεῖσθαι</span> _rail at_ take the dat., <span lang="grc">ἀδικεῖν</span> _injure_ and <span lang="grc">ὑβρίζειν</span> _insult_ also take <span lang="grc">εἴς τινα</span> or <span lang="grc">πρός τινα</span>.

##### <a id="s1593"></a> §1593

<span lang="grc">εὖ</span> (<span lang="grc">κακῶς</span>) <span lang="grc">ἀκούειν, πάσχειν</span> are used as the passives of <span lang="grc">εὖ</span> (<span lang="grc">κακῶς</span>) <span lang="grc">λέγειν, ποιεῖν</span>. Cp. [1752](../20-the-verb-voices/04-passive-voice.md#s1752).

##### <a id="s1594"></a> §1594

Many of the above-mentioned verbs take a double accusative ([1622](#s1622)).

##### <a id="s1595"></a> §1595

(II) Verbs expressing emotion and its manifestations.

**a.** <span lang="grc">φοβεῖσθαι, δεδιέναι, τρεῖν, ἐκπλήττεσθαι, καταπλήττεσθαι</span> _fear_, <span lang="grc">πτήσσειν</span> _crouch before_, <span lang="grc">εὐλαβεῖσθαι</span> _beware of_, <span lang="grc">θαρρεῖν</span> _have no fear of_ (have confidence in), <span lang="grc">αἰδεῖσθαι</span> _stand in awe of_, <span lang="grc">αἰσχύ̄νεσθαι</span> _feel shame before_, <span lang="grc">δυσχεραίνειν</span> _be disgusted at_, <span lang="grc">ἐλεεῖν</span> _pity_, <span lang="grc">πενθεῖν, θρηνεῖν, δακρύ̄ειν, κλά̄ειν</span> (<span lang="grc">κλαίειν</span>) _lament_, _weep over_.

**b.** <span lang="grc">χαίρειν</span> _rejoice at_ and <span lang="grc">ἥδεσθαι</span> _be pleased to hear_ take the accus. of a person only in the poets and only with a predicate participle ([2100](../22-the-participle.md#s2100)). <span lang="grc">αἰσχύ̄νεσθαι, χαίρειν, ἥδεσθαι, δυσχεραίνειν</span> usually take the dat. in prose. <span lang="grc">θαρρεῖν</span> may take the instr. dat. (<cite data-ref="Hdt. 3.76">Hdt. 3.76</cite>).

##### <a id="s1596"></a> §1596

(III) _Verbs of swearing_.

<span lang="grc">ὀμνύναι</span> _swear by_ (<span lang="grc">τοὺς θεούς</span>, pass. <span lang="grc">Ζεὺς ὀμώμοται</span>) and _swear to_ (<span lang="grc">τὸν ὅρκον</span>, pass. <span lang="grc">ὁ ὅρκος ὀμώμοται</span>). So <span lang="grc">ἐπιορκεῖν</span> _swear falsely by_.

**a.** <span lang="grc">ὀμνύναι τοὺς θεούς</span> may be an abbreviation of <span lang="grc">ὀμνύναι ὅρκον</span> (internal object) <span lang="grc">τῶν θεῶν</span>.

**b.** The accusative is used in asseverations with the adverbs of swearing <span lang="grc">μά, οὐ μά, ναὶ μά, νή</span>.

_Nay_, _by Zeus:_ <span lang="grc">μὰ</span> (<span lang="grc">τὸν</span>) <span lang="grc">Δία, οὐ μὰ</span> (<span lang="grc">τὸν</span>) <span lang="grc">Δία</span>.

_Yea_, _by Zeus:_ <span lang="grc">ναὶ μὰ</span> (<span lang="grc">τὸν</span>) <span lang="grc">Δία, νὴ</span> (<span lang="grc">τὸν</span>) <span lang="grc">Δία</span>.

<span lang="grc">μά</span> is negative, except when preceded by <span lang="grc">ναί. μά</span> may stand alone when a negative precedes (often in a question) or when a negative follows in the next clause: <span lang="grc">μὰ τὸν Ἀπόλλω, οὔκ</span> <cite data-ref="Aristoph. Thes. 269">Ar. Thesm. 269</cite>. <span lang="grc">μά</span> is sometimes omitted after <span lang="grc">οὐ</span>, and after <span lang="grc">ναί</span>: <span lang="grc">οὐ τὸν Ὄλυμπον</span> <cite data-ref="Soph. OT 1088">S. O. T. 1088</cite>, <span lang="grc">ναὶ τὰ̄ν κόρᾱν</span> <cite data-ref="Aristoph. Wasps 1438">Ar. Vesp. 1438</cite>.

**c.** The name of the deity may be omitted in Attic under the influence of sudden scrupulousness: <span lang="grc">μὰ τὸν—ου᾽ σύ γε</span> _not you, by_—<cite data-ref="Plat. Gorg. 466e">P. G. 466e</cite>.

##### <a id="s1597"></a> §1597

(IV) Various other verbs.

<span lang="grc">φεύγειν</span> _flee from_, <span lang="grc">ἀποδιδρά̄σκειν</span> _escape from_, <span lang="grc">ἐνεδρεύειν</span> _lie in wait for_, <span lang="grc">φθάνειν</span> _anticipate_, <span lang="grc">φυλάττεσθαι</span> _guard oneself against_, <span lang="grc">ἀμύ̄νεσθαι</span> _defend oneself against_, <span lang="grc">λανθάνειν</span> _escape the notice of_, <span lang="grc">μένειν</span> _wait for_, <span lang="grc">ἐκλείπειν</span> and <span lang="grc">ἐπιλείπειν</span> _give out_, _fail_ (<span lang="grc">τὸ στράτευμα ὁ σῖτος ἐπέλιπε</span> _corn failed the army_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 1.5.6">X. A. 1.5.6</cite>).

##### <a id="s1598"></a> §1598

The accusative is rarely found after verbal nouns and adjectives, and in periphrastic expressions equivalent to a transitive verb. (This usage is post-Homeric and chiefly poetical.) <!-- page 360 -->

<span lang="grc">χοὰ̄ς προπομπός</span> (= <span lang="grc">προπέμπουσα</span>) _escorting the libations_ <cite data-ref="Aesch. Lib. 23">A. Ch. 23</cite>, <span lang="grc">τὰ μετέωρα φροντιστής</span> _a speculator about things above the earth_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Apol. 18b">P. ## FREE USES OF THE ACCUSATIVE

### ACCUSATIVE OF RESPECT

##### <a id="s1600"></a> §1600

To verbs denoting a state, and to adjectives, an accusative may be added to denote a thing _in respect to which_ the verb or adjective is limited.

**a.** The accusative usually expresses a local relation or the instrument. The word restricted by the accusative usually denotes _like_ or _similar to, good_ or _better_, _bad_ or _worse_, a physical or a mental quality, or an emotion.

##### <a id="s1601"></a> §1601

The accusative of respect is employed

**a.** Of the parts of the body: <span lang="grc">ὁ ἄνθρωπος τὸν δάκτυλον ἀλγεῖ</span> _the man has a pain in his finger_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Rep. 462d">P. R. 462d</cite>, <span lang="grc">τυφλὸς τά τ᾽ ὦτα τόν τε νοῦν τά τ᾽ ὀ´μματ᾽ εἶ</span> _blind art thou in ears, and mind, and eyes_ <cite data-ref="Soph. OT 371">S. O. T. 371</cite>, <span lang="grc">πόδας ὠκὺς Ἀχιλλεύς</span> Hom.

N.—The accusative of the _part_ in apposition to the _whole_ ([985](../10-concord-of-subject-and-predicate.md#s985)) belongs here, as is seen by the passive. Cp. <span lang="grc">τὸν πλῆξ᾽ αὐχένα</span> _him he smote on the neck_ <cite data-ref="Hom. Il. 11.240"><span lang="grc">Λ</span> 240</cite> (<span lang="grc">βάλε θοῦρον Ἄρηα κατ᾽ αὐχένα Φ</span> [406](../../part-2-inflection/09-verbs/00-introduction.md#s406)) with <span lang="grc">βέβληαι κενεῶνα</span> _thou art smitten in the abdomen_ E 284.

**b.** Of qualities and attributes (nature, form, size, name, birth, number, etc.): <span lang="grc">διαφέρει γυνὴ ἀνδρὸς τὴν φύσιν</span> _woman differs from man in nature_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Rep. 453b">P. R. 453b</cite>, <span lang="grc">οὐδὲ ἔοικεν θνητὰ̄ς ἀ̄θανάτῃσι δέμας καὶ εἶδος ἐρίζειν</span> _nor is it seemly that mortal women should rival the immortals in form and appearance_ <cite data-ref="Hom. Od. 5.213"><span lang="grc">ε</span> 213</cite>, <span lang="grc">ποταμός, Κύδνος ὄνομα, εὖρος δύο πλέθρων</span> _a river_, _Cydnus by name, two plethra in width_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 1.2.23">X. A. 1.2.23</cite> (so with <span lang="grc">ὕψος, βάθος, μέγεθος</span>), <span lang="grc">πλῆθος ὡς δισχί̄λιοι</span> _about two thousand in number_ 4. 2. 2, <span lang="grc">λέξον ὅστις εἶ γένος</span> _tell me of what race thou art_ <cite data-ref="Eur. Ba. 460">E. Bacch. 460</cite>.

**c.** Of the sphere in general: <span lang="grc">δεινοὶ μάχην</span> _terrible in battle_ <cite data-ref="Aesch. Pers. 27">A. Pers. 27</cite>, <span lang="grc">γένεσθε τὴν διάνοιαν</span> _transfer yourselves in thought_ <cite data-ref="Aeschin. 3.153">Aes. 3.153</cite>, <span lang="grc">τὸ μὲν ἐπ᾽ ἐμοὶ οἴχομαι, τὸ δ᾽ ἐπὶ σοὶ σέσωσμαι</span> _so far as I myself was concerned I was lost, but through you am saved_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 5.4.11">X. C. 5.4.11</cite>. Often of indefinite relations: <span lang="grc">πάντα κακός</span> _base in all things_ <cite data-ref="Soph. OT 1421">S. O. T. 1421</cite>, <span lang="grc">ταῦτα ἀγαθὸς ἕκαστος ἡμῶν, ἅπερ σοφός, ἃ δὲ ἀμαθής, ταῦτα δὲ κακός</span> _each one of us is good in matters in which he is skilled, but bad in those in which he is ignorant_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Lach. 194d">P. Lach. 194d</cite>. <!-- page 361 -->

##### <a id="s1602"></a> §1602

Very rarely after substantives: <span lang="grc">χεῖρας αἰχμητής</span> _a warrior valiant with_ (_thy_) _arm_ <cite data-ref="Hom. Od. 16.242"><span lang="grc">π</span> 242</cite>, <span lang="grc">νεᾱνίαι τὰ̄ς ὄψεις</span> _youths by their appearance_ <cite data-ref="Lys. 10.29">L. 10.29</cite>.

##### <a id="s1603"></a> §1603

For the acccusative of respect the instrumental dative ([1516](06-dative.md#s1516)) is also employed, and also the prepositions <span lang="grc">ει᾿ς, κατά, πρός</span>, e.g. <span lang="grc">διαφέρειν ἀρετῇ</span> or <span lang="grc">εἰς ἀρετήν</span>.

##### <a id="s1604"></a> §1604

Not to be confused with the accusative of respect is the accusative after intransitive adjectives ([1565](#s1565)) or after the passives of 1632.

##### <a id="s1605"></a> §1605

The accusative of respect is probably in its origin, at least in part, an accusative of the internal object.

### ADVERBIAL ACCUSATIVE

##### <a id="s1606"></a> §1606

Many accusatives marking limitations of the verbal action serve the same function as adverbs.

##### <a id="s1607"></a> §1607

Most of these adverbial accusatives are accusatives of the internal object: thus, in <span lang="grc">τέλος δὲ εἶπε</span> _but at last he said_, <span lang="grc">τέλος</span> is to be regarded as standing in apposition to an unexpressed object of the verb—_words_, _which were the end_. Many adverbial accusatives are thus accusatives in apposition ([991](../10-concord-of-subject-and-predicate.md#s991)) and some are accusatives of respect ([1600](#s1600)). It is impossible to apportion all cases among the varieties of the accusatives; many may be placed under different heads. The use of adjectives as adverbs (<span lang="grc">μέγα πλούσιος</span> _very rich_) is often derived from the cognate accusative with verbs (<span lang="grc">μέγα πλουτεῖν</span>).

##### <a id="s1608"></a> §1608

**Manner**.—<span lang="grc">τρόπον τινά</span> _in some way_, <span lang="grc">τίνα τρόπον</span> _in what way?_ <span lang="grc">τόνδε</span> (<span lang="grc">τοῦτον</span>) <span lang="grc">το`ν τρόπον</span> _in this way_, <span lang="grc">πάντα τρόπον</span> _in every way_ (also <span lang="grc">παντὶ τρόπῳ</span>), <span lang="grc">τὴν ταχίστην</span> (<span lang="grc">ὁδόν</span>) _in the quickest way_, <span lang="grc">τὴν εὐθεῖαν</span> (<span lang="grc">ὁδόν</span>) _straightforward_, <span lang="grc">προῖκα, δωρεά̄ν</span> _gratis_ ([1616](#s1616)), <span lang="grc">δίκην</span> _after the fashion of_ (<span lang="grc">δίκην τοξότου</span> _like an archer_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Laws 705e">P. L. 705e</cite>), <span lang="grc">πρόφασιν</span> _in pretence_ (<span lang="grc">ἔπλεε πρόφασιν ἐπ᾽ Ἑλλησπόντου</span> _he sailed professedly for the Hellespont_ <cite data-ref="Hdt. 5.33">Hdt. 5.33</cite>), <span lang="grc">χάριν</span> _for the sake of_ (lit. _favour_)_:_ <span lang="grc">οὐ τὴν Ἀθηναίων χάριν ἐστρατεύοντο</span> _did not engage in the expedition out of good will to the Athenians_ <cite data-ref="Hdt. 5.99">Hdt. 5.99</cite>, <span lang="grc">τοῦ χάριν</span> _for what reason?_ <cite data-ref="Aristoph. Pl. 53">Ar. Plut. 53</cite>, <span lang="grc">τὴν σὴν ἥκω χάριν</span> _for thy sake I have come_ <cite data-ref="Soph. Phil. 1413">S. Ph. 1413</cite>. Cp. [993](../10-concord-of-subject-and-predicate.md#s993).

##### <a id="s1609"></a> §1609

**Measure and Degree**.—<span lang="grc">μέγα, μεγάλα</span> _greatly_, <span lang="grc">πολύ, πολλά</span> _much_, <span lang="grc">τὸ πολύ, τὰ πολλά</span> _for the most part_, <span lang="grc">ὅσον</span> _as much as_, <span lang="grc">οὐδέν, μηδέν</span> _not at all_, <span lang="grc">τοσοῦτον</span> _so much_, <span lang="grc">τὶ</span> _somewhat_, <span lang="grc">ἀρχήν</span> or <span lang="grc">τὴν ἀρχήν</span> _at all_ with <span lang="grc">οὐ</span> or <span lang="grc">μή</span> (<span lang="grc">ἐν τῷ παραχρῆμα οὐκ ἔστιν ἀρχὴν ὀρθῶς βουλεύεσθαι</span> _it is utterly impossible to deliberate correctly offhand_ <cite data-ref="Antiph. 5.73">Ant. 5.73</cite>).

##### <a id="s1610"></a> §1610

**Motive**.—<span lang="grc">τί</span> _why?_ <span lang="grc">τοῦτο, ταῦτα</span> _for this reason_ (cognate accus.): <span lang="grc">τί ἦλθες</span> _quid_ (_cur_) _venisti_ = <span lang="grc">τίνα ἷξιν ἦλθες; τοῦτο χαίρω</span> ( = <span lang="grc">ταύτην τὴν χαρὰ̄ν χαίρω</span>) _therefore I rejoice_, <span lang="grc">αὐτὰ ταῦτα ἥκω</span> _for this very reason have I come_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Prot. 310e">P. Pr. 310e</cite>, <span lang="grc">τοῦτ᾽ ἄχθεσθε</span> _for this reason you are vexed_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 3.2.20">X. A. 3.2.20</cite>.

##### <a id="s1611"></a> §1611

**Time and Succession** ([1582](#s1582)): <span lang="grc">τὸ νῦν</span> _now_, <span lang="grc">τὸ πάλαι</span> _of old_, <span lang="grc">πρότερον</span> _before_, <span lang="grc">τὸ πρότερον</span> _the former time_, <span lang="grc">πρῶτον</span> _first_, <span lang="grc">τὸ κατ᾽ ἀρχά̄ς</span> _in the beginning_, <span lang="grc">τὸ πρῶτον</span> _in the first place_, <span lang="grc">τὸ τελευταῖον</span> _in the last place_ (for <span lang="grc">τὸ δεύτερον</span> in a series use <span lang="grc">ἔπειτα</span> or <span lang="grc">ἔπειτα δέ</span>), <span lang="grc">τὸ λοιπόν</span> _for the future_, <span lang="grc">ἀκμήν</span> _at the point, just_, <span lang="grc">καιρόν</span> _in season_.

## TWO ACCUSATIVES WITH ONE VERB

<!-- page 362 -->

##### <a id="s1612"></a> §1612

A compound expression, consisting of the accusative of an abstract substantive and <span lang="grc">ποιεῖσθαι, τίθεσθαι, ἔχειν</span>, etc., is often treated as a simple verb; and, when transitive, governs the accusative: <span lang="grc">τὴν χώρᾱν καταδρομαῖς λείᾱν ἐποιεῖτο</span> ( = <span lang="grc">ἐλῄζετο</span>) _he ravaged the country by his incursions_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 8.41">T. 8.41</cite>, <span lang="grc">Ἰ̄λίου φθορὰ̄ς ψήφους ἔθεντο</span> ( = <span lang="grc">ἐψηφίσαντο</span>) _they voted for the destruction of Ilium_ <cite data-ref="Aesch. Ag. 814">A. Ag. 814</cite>, <span lang="grc">μομφὴν ἔχω ἓν μὲν πρῶτά σοι</span> ( = <span lang="grc">ἓν μέμφομαι</span>) _I blame thee first for one thing_ <cite data-ref="Eur. Orest. 1069">E. Or. 1069</cite>, <span lang="grc">τὰ δ᾽ ἐν μέσῳ λῆστιν ἴσχεις</span> ( = <span lang="grc">ἐπιλανθάνει</span>) _what lies between thou hast no memory of_ <cite data-ref="Soph. OC 583">S. O. C. 583</cite>. See [1598](#s1598). So with other periphrases in poetry: <span lang="grc">τέκνα μηκύ̄νω λόγον</span> ( = <span lang="grc">μακρότερον προσφωνῶ</span>) _I speak at length to my children_ <cite data-ref="Soph. OC 1120">S. O. C. 1120</cite>, <span lang="grc">εἰ δέ μ᾽ ὧδ᾽ ἀεὶ λόγους ἐξῆρχες</span> ( = <span lang="grc">ἤρχου λέγειν</span>) _if thou didst always_ (_begin to_) _address me thus_ <cite data-ref="Soph. El. 556">S. El. 556</cite>.

### EXTERNAL OBJECT AND PREDICATE ACCUSATIVE

##### <a id="s1613"></a> §1613

Verbs meaning _to appoint, call_, _choose_, _consider_, _make_, _name_, _show_, and the like, may take a second accusative as a predicate to the direct object.

<span lang="grc">στρατηγὸν αὐτὸν ἀπέδειξε</span> _he appointed him general_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 1.1.2">X. A. 1.1.2</cite>, <span lang="grc">πατέρα ἐμὲ ἐκαλεῖτε</span> _you were wont to call me father_ 7. 6. 38, <span lang="grc">αἱρεῖσθαι αὐτὸν τὸν Ἰνδῶν βασιλέᾱ δικαστήν</span> _to choose the king of the Indians himself to be arbitrator_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 2.4.8">X. C. 2.4.8</cite>, <span lang="grc">οὐ γὰρ δίκαιον οὔτε τοὺς κακοὺς μάτην χρηστοὺς νομίζειν οὔτε τοὺς χρηστοὺς κακούς</span> _for it is not just to consider bad men good at random, or good men bad_ <cite data-ref="Soph. OT 609">S. O. T. 609</cite>, <span lang="grc">Τῑμόθεον στρατηγὸν ἐχειροτόνησαν</span> _they elected Timotheus general_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Hell. 6.2.11">X. H. 6.2.11</cite>, <span lang="grc">τὴν σῑγήν σου ξυγχώρησιν θήσω</span> _I shall consider your silence as consent_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Crat. 435b">P. Crat. 435b</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἑαυτὸν δεσπότην πεποίηκεν</span> _he has made himself master_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 1.3.18">X. C. 1.3.18</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἐὰ̄ν ἐμὲ σὸν θεράποντα ποιήσῃ</span> _if you make me your servant_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Ec. 7.42">X. O. 7.42</cite>, <span lang="grc">εἰς τοὺς Ἕλληνας σαυτὸν σοφιστὴν παρέχων</span> _showing yourself a sophist before the Greeks_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Prot. 312a">P. Pr. 312a</cite>, <span lang="grc">εὐμαθῆ πάντα παρέχειν</span> _to render everything easy to learn_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Ec. 20.14">X. O. 20.14</cite>. Cp. [1579](#s1579).

##### <a id="s1614"></a> §1614

The absence of the article generally distinguishes the predicate noun from the object: <span lang="grc">ἐπηγγέλλετο τοὺς κόλακας τοὺς αὑτοῦ πλουσιωτάτους τῶν πολῑτῶν ποιήσειν</span> _he promised to make his flatterers the richest of the citizens_ <cite data-ref="Lys. 28.4">L. 28.4</cite>.

##### <a id="s1615"></a> §1615

Especially in Plato and Herodotus, after verbs signifying _to name, to call_, the predicate noun may be connected with the external object by (a redundant) <span lang="grc">εἶναι</span> ([911](../02-syntax-of-the-simple-sentence.md#s911)); <span lang="grc">σοφιστὴν ὀνομάζουσι τὸν ἄνδρα εἶναι</span> _they call the man a sophist_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Prot. 311e">P. Pr. 311e</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἐπωνυμίᾱν ἔχει σμῑκρός τε καὶ μέγας εἶναι</span> _he is called both short and tall_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Phaedo 102c">P. Ph. 102c</cite>. This is due to the analogy of verbs signifying _to think_ or _say_ ([1041](../14-adjectives.md#s1041)).

##### <a id="s1616"></a> §1616

A predicate accusative may stand in apposition to the object: <span lang="grc">ἔδωκα δωρειὰ̄ν τὰ λύτρα</span> _I gave_ them _the price of their ransom as a free gift_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 19.170">D. 19.170</cite>.

##### <a id="s1617"></a> §1617

This use is the source of many adverbial accusatives ([993](../10-concord-of-subject-and-predicate.md#s993), [1606](#s1606) ff.).

##### <a id="s1618"></a> §1618

**Passive**: both the object and the predicate accusative of the active construction become nominative ([1743](../20-the-verb-voices/04-passive-voice.md#s1743)) in the passive construction: <span lang="grc">αὐτὸς στρα-</span> <!-- page 363 --> <span lang="grc">τηγὸς ᾑρέθη</span> _he himself was chosen general_ <cite data-ref="Lys. 12.65">L. 12.65</cite>, <span lang="grc">αὐτοὶ νομοθέται κληθήσονται</span> _they shall themselves be called lawgivers_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Laws 681d">P. L. 681d</cite>.

### INTERNAL AND EXTERNAL OBJECT WITH ONE VERB

##### <a id="s1619"></a> §1619

Many verbs take both an internal and an external object.

##### <a id="s1620"></a> §1620

The external object refers to a person, the internal object (cognate accusative, 1563 ff.) refers to a thing. Here the internal object stands in closer relation to the verb.

<span lang="grc">ὁ πόλεμος ἀείμνηστον παιδείᾱν αὐτοὺς ἐπαίδευσε</span> _the war taught them a lesson they will hold in everlasting remembrance_ <cite data-ref="Aeschin. 3.148">Aes. 3.148</cite>, <span lang="grc">τοσοῦτον ἔχθος ἐχθαίρω σε</span> _I hate thee with such an hate_ <cite data-ref="Soph. El. 1034">S. El. 1034</cite>, <span lang="grc">Μέλητός με ἐγράψατο τὴν γραφὴν ταύτην</span> _Meletus brought this accusation against me_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Apol. 19b">P. A. 19b</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἕλκος, τό μιν βάλε</span> _the wound that he dealt him_ E 795 ([1578](#s1578)), <span lang="grc">Μιλτιάδης ὁ τὴν ἐν Μαραθῶνι μάχην τοὺς βαρβάρους νῑκήσᾱς</span> _Miltiades who won the battle at Marathon over the barbarians_ <cite data-ref="Aeschin. 3.181">Aes. 3.181</cite>, <span lang="grc">τὸν ἄνδρα τύπτειν τὰ̄ς πληγά̄ς</span> _to strike the man the blows_ Ant. 4. <cite data-ref="Hom. Od. 3.1"><span lang="grc">γ</span>. 1</cite>, <span lang="grc">καλοῦσί με τοῦτο τὸ ὄνομα</span> _they give me this appellation_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Ec. 7.3">X. O. 7.3</cite>.

##### <a id="s1621"></a> §1621

**Passive** ([1747](../20-the-verb-voices/04-passive-voice.md#s1747)): <span lang="grc">πᾶσαν θεραπείᾱν θεραπευόμενος</span> _receiving every manner of service_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Phaedrus 255a">P. Phae. 255a</cite>, <span lang="grc">τύπτεσθαι πεντήκοντα πληγά̄ς</span> _to be struck fifty blows_ <cite data-ref="Aeschin. 1.139">Aes. 1.139</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἡ κρίσις, ἣν ἐκρίθη</span> _the sentence that was pronounced_ upon him <cite data-ref="Lys. 13.50">L. 13.50</cite>, <span lang="grc">τὰ̄ς μάχᾱς, ὅσᾱς Πέρσαι ἡττήθησαν ἐῶ</span> _I omit the battles in which the Persians were defeated_ <cite data-ref="Isoc. 4.145">I. 4.145</cite>, <span lang="grc">ὄνομα ἓν κεκλημένοι Σικελιῶται</span> _called by the one name of Sicilians_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 4.64">T. 4.64</cite>.

##### <a id="s1622"></a> §1622

So with verbs signifying _to do anything to_ or _say anything of_ a person ([1591](#s1591)): <span lang="grc">πολλὰ ἀγαθὰ ὑ̄μᾶς ἐποίησεν</span> _he did you much good_ <cite data-ref="Lys. 5.3">L. 5.3</cite>, <span lang="grc">ταυτί̄ με ποιοῦσι</span> _that's what they are doing to me_ <cite data-ref="Aristoph. Wasps 696">Ar. Vesp. 696</cite>, <span lang="grc">τὰ τοιαῦτα ἐπαινῶ Ἀ̄γησίλᾱον</span> _I praise Agesilaus for such merits_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Ages. 10.1">X. Ages. 10.1</cite>, <span lang="grc">τοὺς Κορινθίους πολλά τε και&lt;\*&gt; κακὰ ἔλεγε</span> _he said many bad things about the Corinthians_ <cite data-ref="Hdt. 8.61">Hdt. 8.61</cite>. For the accusative of the thing, <span lang="grc">εὖ</span> (<span lang="grc">καλῶς</span>), <span lang="grc">κακῶς</span> may be substituted; and <span lang="grc">εἰς</span> and <span lang="grc">πρός</span> with the accusative occur.

##### <a id="s1623"></a> §1623

The accusative of the person may depend on the idea expressed by the combination of verb and accusative of the thing ([1612](#s1612)); as in <span lang="grc">τοὺς πολεμίους εἰργάσθαι κακά</span> _to have done harm to the enemy_ <cite data-ref="Lys. 21.8">L. 21.8</cite> (here <span lang="grc">εἰργάσθαι</span> of itself does not mean _to do anything to_ a person).

##### <a id="s1624"></a> §1624

When the dative of the person is used, something is done _for_ ([1474](06-dative.md#s1474)), not _to_ him: <span lang="grc">πάντα ἐποίησαν τοῖς ἀποθανοῦσιν</span> _they rendered all honours to the dead_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 4.2.23">X. A. 4.2.23</cite>. <span lang="grc">εἰς</span> or <span lang="grc">πρός</span> with the accusative is also employed.

##### <a id="s1625"></a> §1625

**Passive** of 1622: <span lang="grc">ὅσα ἄλλα ἡ πόλις ἠδικεῖτο</span> _all the other wrongs that the State has suffered_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 18.70">D. 18.70</cite>.

##### <a id="s1626"></a> §1626

_Verbs of dividing_ (<span lang="grc">νέμειν, κατανέμειν, διαιρεῖν, τέμνειν</span>) may take two accusatives, one of the thing divided, the other of its parts (cognate accus.). Thus, <span lang="grc">Κῦρος τὸ στράτευμα κατένειμε δώδεκα μέρη</span> _Cyrus divided the army into twelve divisions_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 7.5.13">X. C. 7.5.13</cite>. <span lang="grc">εἰς</span> or <span lang="grc">κατά</span> may be used with the accusative of the parts.

##### <a id="s1627"></a> §1627

**Passive**: <span lang="grc">διῄρηται ἡ ἀγορὰ̄ τέτταρα μέρη</span> _the Agora is divided into four parts_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 1.2.4">X. C. 1.2.4</cite>. <span lang="grc">εἰς</span> and <span lang="grc">κατά</span> may be used with the accusative of the parts.

### DOUBLE OBJECT WITH VERBS SIGNIFYING TO ASK, DEMAND, ETC.

<!-- page 364 -->

##### <a id="s1628"></a> §1628

Verbs signifying _to ask, clothe_ or _unclothe_, _conceal_, _demand_, _deprive_, _persuade_, _remind_, _teach_, take two objects in the accusative, one of a person, the other of a thing.

<span lang="grc">οὐ τοῦτ᾽ ἐρωτῶ σε</span> _that's not the question I'm asking you_ <cite data-ref="Aristoph. Cl. 641">Ar. Nub. 641</cite>; <span lang="grc">χιτῶνα τὸν ἑαυτοῦ ἐκεῖνον ἠμφίεσε</span> _he put his own tunic on him_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 1.3.17">X. C. 1.3.17</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἰδοὺ δ᾽ Ἀπόλλων αὐτὸς ἐκδύ̄ων ἐμὲ χρηστηρίᾱν ἐσθῆτα</span> _lo Apollo himself divests me of my oracular garb_ <cite data-ref="Aesch. Ag. 1269">A. Ag. 1269</cite>; <span lang="grc">τὴν θυγατέρα ἔκρυπτε τὸν θάνατον τοῦ ἀνδρός</span> _he concealed from his daughter her husband's death_ <cite data-ref="Lys. 32.7">L. 32.7</cite>; <span lang="grc">Κῦρον αἰτεῖν πλοῖα</span> _to ask Cyrus for boats_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 1.3.14">X. A. 1.3.14</cite>, <span lang="grc">ὡς ἐγώ ποτέ τινα ἢ ἐπρᾱξάμην μισθὸν ἢ ᾔτησα</span> _that I ever exacted or asked pay of any one_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Apol. 31c">P. A. 31c</cite>; <span lang="grc">τούτων τὴν τῑμὴν ἀποστερεῖ με</span> _he deprives me of the value of these things_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 28.13">D. 28.13</cite>; <span lang="grc">ὑ̄μᾶς τοῦτο οὐ πείθω</span> _I cannot persuade you of this_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Apol. 37a">P. A. 37a</cite>; <span lang="grc">ἀναμνήσω ὑ̄μᾶς καὶ τοὺς κινδύ̄νους</span> _I will remind you of the dangers also_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 3.2.11">X. A. 3.2.11</cite>; <span lang="grc">οὐδεὶς ἐδίδαξέ με ταύτην τὴν τέχνην</span> _nobody taught me this art_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Ec. 19.16">X. O. 19.16</cite>.

##### <a id="s1629"></a> §1629

Both person and thing are equally governed by the verb. The accusative of the person is the external object; the accusative of the thing is sometimes a cognate accusative (internal accusative).

##### <a id="s1630"></a> §1630

Some of these verbs also take the genitive or dative, or employ prepositions. Thus <span lang="grc">ἐρωτᾶν τινα περί τινος, αἰτεῖν</span> (<span lang="grc">αἰτεῖσθαί</span>) <span lang="grc">τι παρά τινος, ἀποστερεῖν</span> or <span lang="grc">ἀφαιρεῖσθαί τινά τινος</span> (<span lang="grc">τινός τι</span>) ([1394](05-free-uses-of-the-genitive.md#s1394)), or <span lang="grc">τινί τι</span> ([1483](06-dative.md#s1483)); <span lang="grc">ἀναμιμνῄσκειν τινά τινος</span> ([1356](04-genitive-with-verbs.md#s1356)); <span lang="grc">παιδεύειν τινά τινι</span> or <span lang="grc">τινὰ εἰς</span> (or <span lang="grc">πρός</span>) with the accusative.

##### <a id="s1631"></a> §1631

The poets employ this construction with _verbs of cleansing_ (a form of _depriving_): <span lang="grc">χρόα νίζετο ἅλμην</span> _he was washing the brine from his skin_ <cite data-ref="Hom. Od. 6.224"><span lang="grc">ζ</span> 224</cite>, <span lang="grc">αἷμα κάθηρον Σαρπηδόνα</span> _cleanse the blood from Sarpedon_ II 667. And with other verbs (in tragedy), e.g. <span lang="grc">τῑμωρεῖσθαι</span> _avenge on_, <span lang="grc">μετελθεῖν</span> _seek to avenge on_, <span lang="grc">μετιέναι</span> _execute judgment on_, <span lang="grc">ἐπισκήπτειν</span> _charge_.

##### <a id="s1632"></a> §1632

**Passive** ([1747](../20-the-verb-voices/04-passive-voice.md#s1747)): <span lang="grc">ὑπὸ βασιλέως πεπρᾱγμένος τοὺς φόρους</span> _having had the tribute demanded of him by the king_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 8.5">T. 8.5</cite>, <span lang="grc">ὅσοι ἵππους ἀπεστέρηνται</span> _all who have been deprived of their horses_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 6.1.12">X. C. 6.1.12</cite>, <span lang="grc">οὐκ ἐπείθοντο τὰ ἐσαγγελθέντα</span> _they would not credit the news_ <cite data-ref="Hdt. 8.81">Hdt. 8.81</cite>, <span lang="grc">μουσικὴν παιδευθείς</span> _having been instructed in music_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Menex. 236a">P. Menex. 236a</cite> (here <span lang="grc">μουσικῇ</span> is possible), <span lang="grc">οὐδὲν ἄλλο διδάσκεται ἄνθρωπος ἢ ἐπιστήμην</span> _man is taught nothing else except knowledge_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Meno 87c">P. Men. 87c</cite>.

##### <a id="s1633"></a> §1633

The accusative of extent ([1580](#s1580)) is freely used in the same sentence with other accusatives, as <span lang="grc">ὑπερενεγκόντες τὸν Λευκαδίων ἰσθμὸν τὰ̄ς ναῦς</span> _having hauled the ships across the isthmus of Leucas_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 3.81">T. 3.81</cite>.

On the accusative of the whole and part, see [985](../10-concord-of-subject-and-predicate.md#s985); on the accusative subject of the infinitive, see [1972](../21-verbal-nouns.md#s1972) ff.; on the accusative absolute, see [2076](../22-the-participle.md#s2076). See also under _Anacoluthon_.

## TWO VERBS WITH A COMMON OBJECT

##### <a id="s1634"></a> §1634

The case of an object common to two verbs is generally that demanded by the nearer: <span lang="grc">οὐ δεῖ τοῖς παιδοτρίβαις ἐγκαλεῖν οὐδ᾽ ἐκβάλλειν ἐκ τῶν πόλεων</span> _we must not accuse the trainer or banish him from the cities_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Gorg. 460d">P. G. 460d</cite>. <!-- page 365 -->

**a.** The farther verb may contain the main idea: <span lang="grc">ἐπιτῑμᾷ καὶ ἀποδοκιμάζει τισί</span> _he censures some and rejects them at the scrutiny_ <cite data-ref="Lys. 6.33">L. 6.33</cite>.

##### <a id="s1635"></a> §1635

The construction is usually ruled by the participle, not by the finite verb, when they have a common object but different constructions, and especially when the object stands nearer the participle: <span lang="grc">τούτῳ δοὺς ἡγεμόνας πορεύεσθαι ἐκέλευσεν ἡσύχως</span> _having given him guides he ordered him to proceed quietly_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 5.3.53">X. C. 5.3.53</cite>; and when the common object stands between, as <span lang="grc">προσπεσόντες τοῖς πρώτοις τρέπουσι</span> _falling upon the foremost they put them to flight_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 7.53">T. 7.53</cite>.

**a.** Sometimes the finite verb regulates the construction, as <span lang="grc">καλέσᾱς παρεκελεύετο τοῖς Ἕλλησι</span> _he summoned the Greeks and exhorted them_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 1.8.11">X. A. 1.8.11</cite>.
