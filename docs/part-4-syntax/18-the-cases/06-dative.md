# THE CASES: DATIVE

##### <a id="s1450"></a> §1450

The Greek dative does duty for three cases: the dative proper, and two lost cases, the instrumental and the locative. <!-- page 338 -->

**a.** The dative derives its name (<span lang="grc">ἡ δοτικὴ πτῶσις</span>, _casus dativus_) from the use with <span lang="grc">διδόναι</span> ([1469](#s1469)).

##### <a id="s1451"></a> §1451

The dative is a necessary complement of a verb when the information given by the verb is incomplete without the addition of the idea expressed by the dative. Thus, <span lang="grc">πείθεται</span> _he obeys_, calls for the addition of an idea to complete the sense, as <span lang="grc">τοῖς νόμοις</span> _the laws_.

##### <a id="s1452"></a> §1452

The dative as a voluntary complement of a verb adds something unessential to the completion of an idea. Thus, <span lang="grc">αὐτοῖς οἱ βάρβαροι ἀπῆλθον</span> _the barbarians departed—for them_ (to their advantage). Here belongs the dative of interest, 1474 ff.

##### <a id="s1453"></a> §1453

But the boundary line between the necessary and the voluntary complement is not always clearly marked. When the idea of the action, not the object of the action, is emphatic, a verb, usually requiring a dative to complete its meaning, may be used alone, as <span lang="grc">πείθεται</span> _he is obedient_.

##### <a id="s1454"></a> §1454

With many intransitive verbs the dative is the sole complement. With transitive verbs it is the indirect complement (dative of the _indirect_ or _remoter_ object, usually a person); that is, it further defines the meaning of a verb already defined in part by the accusative.

##### <a id="s1455"></a> §1455

Many verbs so vary in meaning that they may take the dative either alone or along with the accusative (sometimes the genitive). No rules can be given, and English usage is not always the same as Greek usage.

##### <a id="s1456"></a> §1456

The voice often determines the construction. Thus, <span lang="grc">πείθειν τινά</span> _to persuade some one_, <span lang="grc">πείθεσθαί τινι</span> _to persuade oneself for some one_ (_obey some one_), <span lang="grc">&lt;\*&gt;λεύειν τινὰ ταῦτα ποιεῖν</span> _to order some one to do this_, <span lang="grc">παρακελεύεσθαί τινι ταῦτα τοιεῖν</span> _to exhort some one to do this_.

## DATIVE PROPER

##### <a id="s1457"></a> §1457

The dative proper denotes that _to_ or _for_ which something is or is done.

##### <a id="s1458"></a> §1458

It is either (1) used with single words (verbs, adjectives, and sometimes with adverbs and substantives) or (2) it serves to define an entire sentence; herein unlike the genitive and accusative, which usually modify single members &lt;\*&gt; a sentence. The connection between dative and verb is less intimate than that between genitive or accusative and verb.

##### <a id="s1459"></a> §1459

The dative proper is largely personal, and denotes the person who is interested in or affected by the action; and includes 1461-1473 as well as 1474 ff. The dative proper is not often used with things; when so used there is usually personification or semi-personification.

## THE DATIVE DEPENDENT ON A SINGLE WORD

### DATIVE AS DIRECT COMPLEMENT OF VERBS

##### <a id="s1460"></a> §1460

The dative may be used as the sole complement of many verbs that are usually transitive in English. Such are <!-- page 339 -->

##### <a id="s1461"></a> §1461

(I) _To benefit, help_, _injure, please_, _displease, be friendly_ or _hostile_, _blame_, _be angry, threaten_, _envy_.

<span lang="grc">βοηθεῖν τοῖσιν ἠδικημένοις</span> _to help the wronged_ <cite data-ref="Eur. IA 79">E. I. A. 79</cite>, <span lang="grc">οὐκ ἂν ἠνώχλει νῦν ἡμῖν</span> _he would not now be troubling us_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 3.5">D. 3.5</cite>, <span lang="grc">&lt;\*&gt;ντὶ τοῦ συνεργεῖν ἑαυτοῖς τὰ συμφέροντα ἐπηρεάζουσιν ἀλλήλοις</span> _instead of coöperating for their mutual interests, they revile one another_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Mem. 3.5.16">X. M. 3.5.16</cite>, <span lang="grc">εἰ τοῖς πλέοτιν ἀρέσκοντές ἐσμεν, τοῖσδ᾽ ἂν μόνοις οὐκ ὀρθῶς ἀπαρέσκοιμεν</span> _if we are pleasing to the majority, it would not be right if we should displease them alone_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 1.38">T. 1.38</cite>, <span lang="grc">εὐνοεῖν τοῖς κακόνοις</span> _to be friendly to the ill-intentioned_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 8.2.1">X. C. 8.2.1</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἐμοὶ ὀργίζονται</span> _they are angry at me_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Apol. 23c">P. A. 23c</cite>, <span lang="grc">τῷ Θηρᾱμένει ἠπείλουν</span> _they threatened Theramenes_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 8.92">T. 8.92</cite>, <span lang="grc">οὐ φθονῶν τοῖς πλουτοῦσιν</span> _not cherishing envy against the rich_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 1.9.19">X. A. 1.9.19</cite>.

##### <a id="s1462"></a> §1462

Some _verbs of benefiting_ and _injuring_ take the accusative (<span lang="grc">ὠφελεῖν, βλάπτειν</span>, 1591 a); <span lang="grc">μῑσεῖν τινα</span> _hate some one_. <span lang="grc">λῡσιτελεῖν, συμφέρειν</span> _be of advantage_ take the dative.

##### <a id="s1463"></a> §1463

(II) _To meet, approach_, _yield_.

<span lang="grc">ἐπεὶ δὲ ἀπήντησαν αὐτοῖς οἱ στρατηγοί</span> _but when the generals met them_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 2.3.17">X. A. 2.3.17</cite>, <span lang="grc">περιτυγχάνει Φιλοκράτει</span> _he meets Philocrates_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Hell. 4.8.24">X. H. 4.8.24</cite>, <span lang="grc">ποίοις οὐ χρὴ θηρίοις πελάζειν</span> _what wild beasts one must not approach_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 1.4.7">X. C. 1.4.7</cite>, <span lang="grc">σὺ δ᾽ εἶκ᾽ ἀνάγκῃ καὶ θεοῖσι μὴ μάχου</span> _yield to necessity and war not with heaven_ E. fr. 716. On the genitive with _verbs of approaching_, see [1353](04-genitive-with-verbs.md#s1353).

##### <a id="s1464"></a> §1464

(III) _To obey, serve_, _pardon_, _trust_, _advise_, _command_, etc.

<span lang="grc">τοῖς νόμοις πείθου</span> _obey the laws_ <cite data-ref="Isoc. 1.16">I. 1.16</cite>, <span lang="grc">τῷ ὑ̄μετέρῳ ξυμφόρῳ ὑπακούειν</span> _to be subservient to your interests_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 5.98">T. 5.98</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἂ̄ν μηδεμιᾷ δουλεύῃς τῶν ἡδονῶν</span> _if you are the slave of no pleasure_ <cite data-ref="Isoc. 2.29">I. 2.29</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἐπίστευον αὐτῷ αἱ πόλεις</span> _the cities trusted him_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 1.9.8">X. A. 1.9.8</cite>, <span lang="grc">στρατηγῷ στρατιώταις παραινοῦντι</span> _a general advising his men_ P. Ion 540 d, <span lang="grc">τῷ Μῡσῷ ἐσήμηνε φεύγειν</span> _he ordered the Mysian to flee_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 5.2.30">X. A. 5.2.30</cite>, <span lang="grc">τῷ Κλεάρχῳ ἐβόᾱ ἄγειν</span> _he shouted to Clearchus to lead_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 1.8.12">X. A. 1.8.12</cite>.

##### <a id="s1465"></a> §1465

<span lang="grc">κελεύειν</span> _command_ (strictly _impel_) may be followed in Attic by the accusative and (usually) the infinitive; in Hom. by the dative either alone or with the infinitive. Many _verbs of commanding_ (<span lang="grc">παραγγέλλειν, διακελεύεσθαι</span>) take in Attic the accusative, not the dative, when used with the infinitive ([1996](../21-verbal-nouns.md#s1996) N.). <span lang="grc">ὑπακούειν</span> (and <span lang="grc">ἀκούειν</span> = _obey_) may take the genitive ([1366](04-genitive-with-verbs.md#s1366)).

##### <a id="s1466"></a> §1466

(IV) _To be like_ or _unlike_, _compare_, _befit_.

<span lang="grc">ἐοικέναι τοῖς τοιούτοις</span> _to be like such men_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Rep. 349d">P. R. 349d</cite>, <span lang="grc">τί οὖν πρέπει ἀνδρὶ πένητι</span>; _what then befits a poor man?_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Apol. 36d">P. A. 36d</cite>.

##### <a id="s1467"></a> §1467

The dative of the person and the genitive of the thing are used with the impersonals <span lang="grc">δεῖ</span> ([1400](05-free-uses-of-the-genitive.md#s1400)), <span lang="grc">μέτεστι, μέλει, μεταμέλει, προσήκει</span>. Thus, <span lang="grc">μισθοφόρων ἀνδρὶ τυράννῳ δεῖ</span> _a tyrant needs mercenaries_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Hiero 8.10">X. Hi. 8.10</cite>, <span lang="grc">ὡς οὐ μετὸν αὐτοῖς Ἐπιδάμνου</span> _inasmuch as they had nothing to do with Epidamnus_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 1.28">T. 1.28</cite>, <span lang="grc">οὐχ ὧν ἐβιά̄σατο μετέμελεν αὐτῷ</span> _he did not repent of his acts of violence_ <cite data-ref="Andoc. 4.17">And. 4.17</cite>, <span lang="grc">τούτῳ τῆς Βοιωτίᾱς προσήκει οὐδέν</span> _he has nothing to do with Boeotia_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 3.1.31">X. A. 3.1.31</cite>. <span lang="grc">ἔξεστί μοι</span> _it is in my power_ does not take the genitive. For the accusative instead of the dative, see [1400](05-free-uses-of-the-genitive.md#s1400). Cp. [1344](04-genitive-with-verbs.md#s1344). <!-- page 340 -->

**a.** For <span lang="grc">δοκεῖ μοι</span> _it seems to me_ (_mihi videtur_), <span lang="grc">δοκῶ μοι</span> (_mihi videor_) may be used. **b.** For other cases of the dative as direct complement see [1476](#s1476), [1481](#s1481).

##### <a id="s1468"></a> §1468

An intransitive verb taking the dative can form a personal passive, the dative becoming the nominative subject of the passive. Cp. [1745](../20-the-verb-voices/04-passive-voice.md#s1745).

### DATIVE AS INDIRECT COMPLEMENT OF VERBS

##### <a id="s1469"></a> §1469

Many verbs take the dative as the indirect object together with an accusative as the direct object. The indirect object is commonly introduced in English by _to_.

<span lang="grc">Κῦρος δίδωσιν αὐτῷ ἓξ μηνῶν μισθόν</span> _Cyrus gives him pay for six months_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 1.1.10">X. A. 1.1.10</cite>, <span lang="grc">τῷ Ὑρκανίῳ ἵππον ἐδωρήσατο</span> _he presented a horse to the Hyrcanian_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 8.4.24">X. C. 8.4.24</cite>, <span lang="grc">τὰ δὲ ἄλλα διανεῖμαι τοῖς στρατηγοῖς</span> _to distribute the rest to the generals_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 7.5.2">X. A. 7.5.2</cite>, <span lang="grc">μῑκρὸν μεγάλῳ εἰκάσαι</span> _to compare a small thing to a great thing_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 4.36">T. 4.36</cite>, <span lang="grc">πέμπων αὐτῷ ἄγγελον</span> _sending a messenger to him_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 1.3.8">X. A. 1.3.8</cite>, <span lang="grc">ὑπισχνοῦμαί σοι δέκα τάλαντα</span> _I promise you ten talents_ 1. 7. 18, <span lang="grc">τοῦτο σοὶ δ᾽ ἐφί̄εμαι</span> _I lay this charge upon thee_ <cite data-ref="Soph. Aj. 116">S. Aj. 116</cite>, <span lang="grc">παρῄνει τοῖς Ἀθηναίοις τοιάδε</span> _he advised the Athenians as follows_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 6.8">T. 6.8</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἐμοὶ ἐπιτρέψαι ταύτην τὴν ἀρχήν</span> _to entrust this command to me_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 6.1.31">X. A. 6.1.31</cite>. <span lang="grc">λέγειν ταῦτα τοῖς στρατιώταις</span> _to say this to the soldiers_ 1. 4. 11 (<span lang="grc">λέγειν πρός τινα</span> lacks the personal touch of the dative, which indicates interest in the person addressed). A dependent clause often represents the accusative.

##### <a id="s1470"></a> §1470

**Passive**.—The accusative of the active becomes the subject of the passive, the dative remains: <span lang="grc">ἐκείνῳ αὕτη ἡ χώρᾱ ἐδόθη</span> _this land was given to him_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Hell. 3.1.6">X. H. 3.1.6</cite>.

### DATIVE AS DIRECT OR INDIRECT COMPLEMENT OF VERBS

##### <a id="s1471"></a> §1471

Many verbs may take the dative either alone or with the accusative.

<span lang="grc">οὐδενὶ μέμφομαι</span> _I find fault with no one_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 21.190">D. 21.190</cite>, <span lang="grc">τί ἄν μοι μέμφοιο</span>; _what fault would you have to find with me?_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Ec. 2.15">X. O. 2.15</cite>; <span lang="grc">ὑπηρετῶ τοῖς θεοῖς</span> _I am a servant of the gods_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 8.2.22">X. C. 8.2.22</cite>, <span lang="grc">Ἔρωτι πᾶν ὑπηρετεῖ</span> _he serves Eros in everything_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Sym. 196c">P. S. 196c</cite>; <span lang="grc">παρακελεύονται τοῖς περὶ νί̄κης ἁμιλλωμένοις</span> _they exhort those who are striving for victory_ <cite data-ref="Isoc. 9.79">I. 9.79</cite>, <span lang="grc">ταῦτα τοῖς ὁπλί̄ταις παρακελεύομαι</span> _I address this exhortation to the hoplites_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 7.63">T. 7.63</cite>; <span lang="grc">ὀνειδίζετε τοῖς ἀδικοῦσιν</span> _you reproach the guilty_ <cite data-ref="Lys. 27.16">L. 27.16</cite> (also accus.), <span lang="grc">Θηβαίοις τὴν ἀμαθίᾱν ὀνειδίζουσι</span> _they upbraid the Thebans with their ignorance_ <cite data-ref="Isoc. 15.248">I. 15.248</cite>; <span lang="grc">θεοῖς εὐξάμενοι</span> _having prayed to the gods_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 3.58">T. 3.58</cite>, <span lang="grc">εὐξάμενοι τοῖς θεοῖς τἀ̄γαθά</span> _having prayed to the gods for success_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 2.3.1">X. C. 2.3.1</cite> (cp. <span lang="grc">αἰτεῖν τινά τι</span>, [1628](07-accusative.md#s1628)). So <span lang="grc">ἐπιτῑμᾶν</span> (<span lang="grc">ἐγκαλεῖν</span>) <span lang="grc">τινι</span> _to censure_ (_accuse_) _some one_, <span lang="grc">ἐπιτῑμᾶν</span> (<span lang="grc">ἐγκαλεῖν</span>) <span lang="grc">τί τινι</span> _censure something in_ (_bring an accusation against_) _some one_. So <span lang="grc">ἀπειλεῖν</span> _threaten;_ and <span lang="grc">ἀμύ̄νειν, ἀλέξειν, ἀρήγειν</span> _ward off_ (<span lang="grc">τινί τι</span> in poetry, [1483](#s1483)).

##### <a id="s1472"></a> §1472

<span lang="grc">τῑμωρεῖν</span> (poet. <span lang="grc">τῑμωρεῖσθαί</span>) <span lang="grc">τινι</span> means _to avenge some one_ (_take vengeance for some one_), as <span lang="grc">τῑμωρήσειν σοι τοῦ παιδὸς ὑπισχνοῦμαι</span> _I promise to avenge you because of_ (on the murderer of) _your son_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 4.6.8">X. C. 4.6.8</cite>, <span lang="grc">εἰ τῑμωρήσεις</span> <!-- page 341 --> <span lang="grc">Πατρόκλῳ τὸν φόνον</span> _if you avenge the murder of Patroclus_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Apol. 28c">P. A. 28c</cite>. <span lang="grc">τιμωρεῖσθαί</span> (rarely <span lang="grc">τῑμωρεῖν</span>) <span lang="grc">τινα</span> means _to avenge oneself upon some one_ (_punish some one_).

##### <a id="s1473"></a> §1473

For the dative of purpose (_to what end?_), common in Latin with a second dative (_dono dare_), Greek uses a predicate noun: <span lang="grc">ἐκείνῳ ἡ χώρᾱ δῶρον ἐδόθη</span> _the country was given to him as a gift_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Hell. 3.1.6">X. H. 3.1.6</cite>. The usage in Attic inscriptions (<span lang="grc">ἧλοι ταῖς θύραις</span> _nails for the doors_ C.I.A. /lref&gt;, add. 834 b, 1, [38](../../part-1-letters-sounds-syllables-accent/01-the-letters.md#s38)) is somewhat similar to the Latin usage. Cp. [1502](#s1502).

**a.** The infinitive was originally, at least in part, a dative of an abstract substantive, and served to mark purpose: <span lang="grc">τίς τ᾽ ἄρ σφωε θεῶν ἔριδι ξυνέηκε μάχεσθαι</span>; _who then of the gods brought the twain together_ (for) _to contend in strife?_ A 8. Cp. “what went ye out for to see?” St. Matth. 11. 8.

## DATIVE AS A MODIFIER OF THE SENTENCE

### DATIVE OF INTEREST

##### <a id="s1474"></a> §1474

The person _for whom_ something is or is done, or in reference to whose case an action is viewed, is put in the dative.

**a.** Many of the verbs in 1461 ff. take a dative of interest. 1476 ff. are special cases.

##### <a id="s1475"></a> §1475

After verbs of motion the dative (usually personal) is used, especially in poetry: <span lang="grc">χεῖρας ἐμοὶ ὀρέγοντας</span> _reaching out their hands to me_ <cite data-ref="Hom. Od. 12.257"><span lang="grc">μ</span> 257</cite>, <span lang="grc">ψῡχὰ̄ς Ἄϊδι προΐαψεν</span> _hurled their souls on to Hades_ (a person) A 3; rarely, in prose, after verbs not compounded with a preposition: <span lang="grc">σχόντες</span> (_scil_. <span lang="grc">τὰ̄ς ναῦς</span>) <span lang="grc">Π̔ηγίῳ</span> _putting in at Rhegium_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 7.1">T. 7.1</cite>. Cp. [1485](#s1485).

##### <a id="s1476"></a> §1476

**Dative of the Possessor**.—The person for whom a thing exists is put in the dative with <span lang="grc">εἶναι, γίγνεσθαι, ὑπάρχειν, φῦναι</span> (poet.), etc., when he is regarded as interested in its possession.

<span lang="grc">ἄλλοις μὲν χρήματά ἐστι, ἡμῖν δὲ ξύμμαχοι ἀγαθοί</span> _others have riches, we have good allies_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 1.86">T. 1.86</cite>, <span lang="grc">τῷ δικαίῳ παρὰ θεῶν δῶρα γίγνεται</span> _gifts are bestowed upon the just man by the gods_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Rep. 613e">P. R. 613e</cite>, <span lang="grc">ὑπάρχει ἡμῖν οὐδὲν τῶν ἐπιτηδείων</span> _we have no supply of provisions_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 2.2.11">X. A. 2.2.11</cite>, <span lang="grc">πᾶσι θνᾱτοῖς ἔφῡ μόρος</span> _death is the natural lot of all men_ <cite data-ref="Soph. El. 860">S. El. 860</cite>.

##### <a id="s1477"></a> §1477

So with _verbs of thinking_ and _perceiving:_ <span lang="grc">τὸν ἀγαθὸν ἄρχοντα βλέποντα νόμον ἀνθρώποις ἐνόμισεν</span> Cyrus _considered that a good ruler was a living law to man_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 8.1.22">X. C. 8.1.22</cite>, <span lang="grc">θαρροῦσι μάλιστα πολέμιοι, ὅταν τοῖς ἐναντίοις πρά̄γματα πυνθάνωνται</span> _the enemy are most courageous when they learn that the forces opposed to them are in trouble_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cav. 5.8">X. Hipp. 5.8</cite>.

##### <a id="s1478"></a> §1478

In the phrase <span lang="grc">ὄνομά</span> (<span lang="grc">ἐστί</span>) <span lang="grc">τινι</span> the name is put in the same case as <span lang="grc">ὄνομα</span>. Thus, <span lang="grc">ἔδοξα ἀκοῦσαι ὄνομα αὐτῷ εἶναι Ἀγάθωνα</span> _I thought I heard his name was Agathon_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Prot. 315e">P. Pr. 315e</cite>. <span lang="grc">ὄνομά μοί ἐστι</span> and <span lang="grc">ὄνομα</span> (<span lang="grc">ἐπωνυμίᾱν</span>) <span lang="grc">ἔχω</span> are treated as the passives of <span lang="grc">ὀνομάζω</span>. Cp. [1322 a](03-genitive.md#s1322).

##### <a id="s1479"></a> §1479

Here belong the phrases (1) <span lang="grc">τί</span> (<span lang="grc">ἐστιν</span>) <span lang="grc">ἐμοὶ καὶ σοί</span>; _what have I to do with thee?;_ cp. <span lang="grc">τί τῷ νόμῳ καὶ τῇ βασάνῳ</span>; _what have the law and torture in common?_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 29.36">D. 29.36</cite>. (2) <span lang="grc">τί ταῦτ᾽ ἐμοί</span>; _what have I to do with this?_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 54.17">D. 54.17</cite>. (3) <span lang="grc">τί ἐμοὶ πλέον</span>; _what gain have I?_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 5.5.34">X. C. 5.5.34</cite>. <!-- page 342 -->

##### <a id="s1480"></a> §1480

The dative of the possessor denotes that something is at the disposal of a person or has fallen to his share temporarily. The genitive of possession lays stress on the _person_ who owns something. The dative answers the question _what is it that he has?_, the genitive answers the question _who is it that has something?_ The uses of the two cases are often parallel, but not interchangeable. Thus, in <span lang="grc">Κῦρος, οὗ σὺ ἔσει τὸ ἀπὸ τοῦδε</span> _Cyrus_, _to whom you will henceforth belong_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 5.1.6">X. C. 5.1.6</cite>, <span lang="grc">ᾧ</span> would be inappropriate. With a noun in the genitive the dative of the possessor is used (<span lang="grc">τῶν ἑκατέροις ξυμμάχων</span> <cite data-ref="Thuc. 2.1">T. 2.1</cite>); with a noun in the dative, the genitive of the possessor (<span lang="grc">τοῖς ἑαυτῶν ξυμμάχοις</span> 1. [18](../../part-1-letters-sounds-syllables-accent/01-the-letters.md#s18)).

##### <a id="s1481"></a> §1481

**Dative of Advantage or Disadvantage** (_dativus commodi et incommodi_).—The person or thing for whose advantage or disadvantage, anything is or is done, is put in the dative. The dative often has to be translated as if the possessive genitive were used; but the meaning is different.

<span lang="grc">ἐπειδὴ αὐτοῖς οἱ βάρβαροι ἐκ τῆς χώρᾱς ἀπῆλθον</span> _after the barbarians had departed_ (for them, to their advantage) _from their country_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 1.89">T. 1.89</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἄλλο στράτευμα αὐτῷ συνελέγετο</span> _another army was being raised for him_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 1.1.9">X. A. 1.1.9</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἄλλῳ ὁ τοιοῦτος πλουτεῖ, καὶ οὐχ ἑαυτῷ</span> _such a man is rich for another, and not for himself_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Menex. 246e">P. Menex. 246e</cite>, <span lang="grc">στεφανοῦσθαι τῷ θεῷ</span> _to be crowned in honour of the god_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Hell. 4.3.21">X. H. 4.3.21</cite>, <span lang="grc">Φιλιστίδης ἔπρᾱττε Φιλίππῳ</span> _Philistides was working in the interest of Philip_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 9.59">D. 9.59</cite>, <span lang="grc">τὰ χρήματ᾽ αἴτι᾽ ἀνθρωποῖς κακῶν</span> _money_ is _a cause of misery to mankind_ E. Fr. 632, <span lang="grc">οἱ Θρᾷκες οἱ τῷ Δημοσθένει ὑστερήσαντες</span> _the Thracians who came too late_ (for, i.e.) _to help Demosthenes_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 7.29">T. 7.29</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἥδε ἡ ἡμέρᾱ τοῖς Ἕλλησι μεγάλων κακῶν ἄρξει</span> _this day will be to the Greeks the beginning of great sorrows_ 2. 12, <span lang="grc">ἄ̄ν τίς σοι τῶν οἰκετῶν ἀποδρᾷ</span> _if any of your slaves runs away_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Mem. 2.10.1">X. M. 2.10.1</cite>.

**a.** For the middle denoting to do something for oneself, see [1719](../20-the-verb-voices/03-middle-voice.md#s1719).

**b.** In the last example in 1481, as elsewhere, the dative of a personal pronoun is used where a possessive pronoun would explicitly denote the owner.

##### <a id="s1482"></a> §1482

A dative, dependent on the sentence, may appear to depend on a substantive: <span lang="grc">σοὶ δὲ δώσω ἄνδρα τῇ θυγατρί</span> _to you I will give a husband for your daughter_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 8.4.24">X. C. 8.4.24</cite>. Common in Hdt.

##### <a id="s1483"></a> §1483

With _verbs of depriving_, _warding off_, and the like, the dative of the person may be used: <span lang="grc">τὸ συστρατεύειν ἀφελεῖν σφίσιν ἐδεήθησαν</span> _they asked him to relieve them_ (lit. _take away for them_) _from serving in the war_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 7.1.44">X. C. 7.1.44</cite>, <span lang="grc">Δαναοῖσιν λοιγὸν ἄμῡνον</span> _ward off ruin from_ (for) _the Danai_ A 456. So <span lang="grc">ἀλέξειν τινί τι</span> (poet.). Cp. [1392](05-free-uses-of-the-genitive.md#s1392), [1628](07-accusative.md#s1628).

##### <a id="s1484"></a> §1484

With _verbs of receiving_ and _buying_, the person who _gives_ or _sells_ may stand in the dative. In <span lang="grc">δέχεσθαί τί τινι</span> (chiefly poetic) the dative denotes the interest of the recipient in the donor: <span lang="grc">Θέμιστι δέκτο δέπας</span> _she took the cup from_ (for, i.e. to please) _Themis_ O 87. So with <span lang="grc">πόσου πρίωμαί σοι τὰ χοιρίδια</span>; _at what price am I to buy the pigs of you?_ <cite data-ref="Aristoph. Ach. 812">Ar. Ach. 812</cite>.

##### <a id="s1485"></a> §1485

With verbs of motion the dative of the person _to whom_ is properly a dative of advantage or disadvantage: <span lang="grc">ἦλθε τοῖς Ἀθηναίοις ἡ ἀγγελίᾱ</span> _the message came to_ (for) _the Athenians_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 1.61">T. 1.61</cite>. Cp. [1475](#s1475).

##### <a id="s1486"></a> §1486

**Dative of Feeling** (Ethical Dative).—The personal pro<!-- page 343 --> nouns of the first and second person are often used to denote the interest of the speaker, or to secure the interest of the person spoken to, in an action or statement.

<span lang="grc">μέμνησθέ μοι μὴ θορυβεῖν</span> _pray remember not to make a disturbance_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Apol. 27b">P. A. 27b</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἀμουσότεροι γενήσονται ὑ̄μῖν οἱ νέοι</span> _your young men will grow less cultivated_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Rep. 546d">P. R. 546d</cite>, <span lang="grc">τοιοῦτο ὑ̄μῖν ἐστι ἡ τυραννίς</span> _such a thing, you know, is despotism_ <cite data-ref="Hdt. 5.92">Hdt. 5.92</cite> <span lang="grc">η, Ἀρταφέρνης ὑ̄μῖν Ὑστάσπεός ἐστι παῖς</span> _Artaphernes_, _you know, is Hystaspes' son_ 5. 30. The dative of feeling may denote surprise: <span lang="grc">ὦ μῆτερ, ὡς καλός μοι ὁ πάππος</span> _oh mother, how handsome grandpa is_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 1.3.2">X. C. 1.3.2</cite>. With the dative of feeling cp. “knock me here” Shakesp. _T. of Sh._ 1. 2. 8, “study me how to please the eye” _L. L. L._ i. 1. 80. <span lang="grc">τοὶ</span> _surely_, often used to introduce general statements or maxims, is a petrified dative of feeling (= <span lang="grc">σοί</span>).

**a.** This dative in the third person is very rare (<span lang="grc">αὐτῇ</span> in <cite data-ref="Plat. Rep. 343a">P. R. 343a</cite>).

**b.** This construction reproduces the familiar style of conversation and may often be translated by _I beg you, please_, _you see, let me tell you_, etc. Sometimes the idea cannot be given in translation. This dative is a form of 1481.

##### <a id="s1487"></a> §1487

<span lang="grc"> **ἐμοὶ βουλομένῳ ἐστί** </span>, etc.—Instead of a sentence with a finite verb, a participle usually denoting _inclination_ or _aversion_ is added to the dative of the person interested, which depends on a form of <span lang="grc">εἶναι, γ&lt;\*&gt;γνεσθαι</span>, etc.

<span lang="grc">τῷ πλήθει τῶν Πλαταιῶν οὐ βουλομένῳ ἦν τῶν Ἀθηναίων ἀφίστασθαι</span> _the Plataean democracy did not wish to revolt from the Athenians_ (= <span lang="grc">τὸ πλῆθος οὐκ ἐβούλετο ἀφίστασθαι</span>) <cite data-ref="Thuc. 2.3">T. 2.3</cite> (lit. _it was not for them when wishing_), <span lang="grc">ἂ̄ν βουλομένοις ἀκούειν ᾖ τουτοισί̄, μνησθήσομαι</span> _if these men_ (the jury) _desire to hear it_, _I shall take the matter up later_ (= <span lang="grc">ἂ̄ν οὗτοι ἀκούειν βούλωνται</span>) <cite data-ref="Dem. 18.11">D. 18.11</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἐπανέλθωμεν, εἴ σοι ἡδομένῳ ἐστίν</span> _let us go back if it is your pleasure to do so_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Phaedo 78b">P. Ph. 78b</cite>, <span lang="grc">εἰ μὴ ἀσμένοις ὑ̄μῖν ἀφῖγμαι</span> _if I have come against your will_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 4.85">T. 4.85</cite>, <span lang="grc">Νῑκίᾳ προσδεχομένῳ ἦν τὰ παρὰ τῶν Ἐγεσταίων</span> _Nicias was prepared for the news from the Egestaeans_ 6. 46, <span lang="grc">ἦν δὲ οὐ τῷ Ἀ̄γησιλά̄ῳ ἀχθομένῳ</span> _this was not displeasing to Agesilaus_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Hell. 5.3.13">X. H. 5.3.13</cite>. Cp. _quibus bellum volentibus erat_.

##### <a id="s1488"></a> §1488

**Dative of the Agent**.—With passive verbs (usually in the perfect and pluperfect) and regularly with verbal adjectives in <span lang="grc">-τός</span> and <span lang="grc">-τέος</span>, the person in whose interest an action is done, is put in the dative. The notion of agency does not belong to the dative, but it is a natural inference that the person interested is the agent.

<span lang="grc">ἐμοὶ καὶ τούτοις πέπρᾱκται</span> _has been done by_ (for) _me and these men_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 19.205">D. 19.205</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἐπειδὴ αὐτοῖς παρεσκεύαστο</span> _when they had got their preparations ready_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 1.46">T. 1.46</cite>, <span lang="grc">τοσαῦτά μοι εἰρήσθω</span> _let so much have been said by me_ <cite data-ref="Lys. 24.4">L. 24.4</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἐψηφίσθαι τῇ βουλῇ</span> _let it have been decreed by the senate_ C.I.A. /lref&gt;

**a.** With verbal adjectives in <span lang="grc">-τός</span> and <span lang="grc">-τέος</span> ([2149](../23-verbal-adjectives-in-tauepsilonomicronsigma.md#s2149)): <span lang="grc">τοῖς οἴκοι ζηλωτός</span> _envied by those at home_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 1.7.4">X. A. 1.7.4</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἡμῖν γ᾽ ὑπὲρ τῆς ἐλευθερίᾱς ἀγωνιστέον</span> _we at least must struggle to defend our freedom_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 9.70">D. 9.70</cite>. For the accus. with <span lang="grc">-τέον</span>, see [2152 a](../23-verbal-adjectives-in-tauepsilonomicronsigma.md#s2152).

##### <a id="s1489"></a> §1489

The usual restriction of the dative to tenses of completed action seems to be due to the fact that the agent is represented as placed in the position of <!-- page 344 --> viewing an already completed action in the light of its relation to himself (interest, advantage, possession).

##### <a id="s1490"></a> §1490

The dative of the agent is rarely employed with other tenses than perfect and pluperfect: <span lang="grc">λέγεται ἡμῖν</span> _is said by us_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Laws 715b">P. L. 715b</cite>, <span lang="grc">τοῖς Κερκῡραίοις οὐχ ἑωρῶντο</span> the ships _were not seen by_ (were invisible to) _the Corcyraeans_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 1.51">T. 1.51</cite>; present, <cite data-ref="Thuc. 4.64">T. 4.64</cite>, 109; aorist <cite data-ref="Thuc. 2.7">T. 2.7</cite>.

##### <a id="s1491"></a> §1491

The person _by whom_ (not _for whom_) an action is explicitly said to be done, is put in the genitive with <span lang="grc">ὑπό</span> ([1698](../19-prepositions.md#s1698). 1. b).

##### <a id="s1492"></a> §1492

The dative of the personal agent is used (1) when the subject is impersonal, the verb being transitive or intransitive, (2) when the subject is persal and the person is treated as a thing in order to express scorn (twice only in the orators: <cite data-ref="Dem. 19.247">D. 19.247</cite>, 57. [10](../../part-1-letters-sounds-syllables-accent/01-the-letters.md#s10)).

##### <a id="s1493"></a> §1493

<span lang="grc">ὑπό</span> with the genitive of the personal agent is used (1) when the subject is a person, a city, a country, or is otherwise quasi-personal, (2) when the verb is intransitive even if the subject is a thing, as <span lang="grc">τῶν τειχῶν ὑπὸ τῶν βαρβάρων πεπτωκότων</span> _the walls having been destroyed by the barbarians_ <cite data-ref="Aeschin. 2.172">Aes. 2.172</cite>, (3) in a few cases with an impersonal subject, usually for the sake of emphasis, as <span lang="grc">ὡς ἑταίρᾱ ἦν . . . ὑπὸ τῶν ἄλλων οἰκείων καὶ ὑπὸ τῶν γειτόνων μεμαρτύρηται</span> _that she was an hetaera has been testified by the rest of his relatives and by his neighbours_ <cite data-ref="Isaeus 3.13">Is. 3.13</cite>.

a. <span lang="grc">νῑκᾶσθαι, ἡττᾶσθαι</span> _to be conquered_ may be followed by the dative of a person, by <span lang="grc">ὑπό τινος</span>, or by the genitive ([1402](05-free-uses-of-the-genitive.md#s1402)).

##### <a id="s1494"></a> §1494

When the agent is a thing, not a person, the dative is commonly used whether the subject is personal or impersonal. If the subject is personal, <span lang="grc">ὑπό</span> may be used; in which case the inanimate agent is personified (see [1698](../19-prepositions.md#s1698). 1. N. 1). <span lang="grc">ὑπό</span> is rarely used when the subject is impersonal. <span lang="grc">ὑπό</span> is never used with the impersonal perfect passive of an intransitive verb.

### DATIVE OF RELATION

##### <a id="s1495"></a> §1495

The dative may be used of a person to whose case the statement of the predicate is limited.

<span lang="grc">φευγειν αὐτοῖς ἀσφαλέστερόν ἐστιν ἢ ἡμῖν</span> _it is safer for them to flee than for us_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 3.2.19">X. A. 3.2.19</cite>, <span lang="grc">τριήρει ἐστὶν εἰς Ἡρά̄κλειαν ἡμέρᾱς μακρᾶς πλοῦς</span> _for a trireme it is a long day's sail to Heraclea_ 6. 4. 2. Such cases as <span lang="grc">δρόμος ἐγένετο τοῖς στρατιώταις</span> _the soldiers began to run_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 1.2.17">X. A. 1.2.17</cite> belong here rather than under 1476 or 1488.

a. <span lang="grc">ὡς</span> restrictive is often added: <span lang="grc">μακρὰ̄ ὡς γέροντι ὁδός</span> _a long road_ (at least) _for an old man_ <cite data-ref="Soph. OC 20">S. O. C. 20</cite>, <span lang="grc">σωφροσύνης δὲ ὡς πλήθει οὐ τὰ τοιάδε μέγιστα</span>; _for the mass of men are not the chief points of temperance such as these?_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Rep. 389d">P. R. 389d</cite>.

##### <a id="s1496"></a> §1496

**Dative of Reference**.—The dative of a noun or pronoun often denotes the person in whose opinion a statement holds good.

<span lang="grc">γάμους τοὺς πρώτους ἐγάμει Πέρσῃσι ὁ Δᾱρεῖος</span> _Darius contracted marriages most distinguished in the eyes of the Persians_ <cite data-ref="Hdt. 3.88">Hdt. 3.88</cite>, <span lang="grc">πᾶσι νῑκᾶν τοῖς κριταῖς</span> _to be victorious in the judgment of all the judges_ <cite data-ref="Aristoph. Birds 445">Ar. Av. 445</cite>, <span lang="grc">πολλοῖσιν οἰκτρός</span> _pitiful in the eyes of many_ <cite data-ref="Soph. Trach. 1071">S. Tr. 1071</cite>. <span lang="grc">παρά</span> is often used, as in <span lang="grc">παρὰ Δᾱρείῳ</span> <span lang="grc">κριτῇ</span> _in the opinion of Darius_ <cite data-ref="Hdt. 3.160">Hdt. 3.160</cite>. <!-- page 345 -->

##### <a id="s1497"></a> §1497

The dative participle, without a noun or pronoun, is frequently used in the singular or plural to denote indefinitely the person judging or observing. This construction is most common with participles of _verbs of coming_ or _going_ and with participles of _verbs of considering_.

<span lang="grc">ἡ Θρᾴκη ἐστὶν ἐπὶ δεξιὰ εἰς τὸν Πόντον εἰσπλέοντι</span> _Thrace is on the right as you sail into the Pontus_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 6.4.1">X. A. 6.4.1</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἔλεγον ὅτι ἡ ὁδὸς διαβάντι τὸν ποταμὸν ἐπὶ Λῡδίᾱν φέροι</span> _they said that, when you had crossed the river, the road led to Lydia_ 3. 5. 15, <span lang="grc">οὐκ οὖν ἄτοπον διαλογιζομένοις τὰ̄ς δωρεὰ̄ς νῡνὶ πλείους εἶναι</span>; _is it not strange_, _when we reflect, that gifts are more frequent now?_ <cite data-ref="Aeschin. 3.179">Aes. 3.179</cite>, <span lang="grc">τὸ μὲν ἔξωθεν ἁπτομένῳ σῶμα οὐκ ἄγᾱν θερμὸν ἦν</span> _if you touched the surface the body was not very hot_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 2.49">T. 2.49</cite>, <span lang="grc">προ`ς ὠφέλειαν σκοπουμένῳ ὁ ἐπαινέτης τοῦ δικαίου ἀληθεύει</span> _if you look at the matter from the point of view of advantage, the panegyrist of justice speaks the truth_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Rep. 589c">P. R. 589c</cite>. So (<span lang="grc">ὡς</span>) <span lang="grc">συνελόντι εἰπεῖν</span> (<cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 3.1.38">X. A. 3.1.38</cite>) _to speak briefly_ (lit. _for one having brought the matter into small compass_), <span lang="grc">συνελόντι</span> <cite data-ref="Dem. 4.7">D. 4.7</cite>.

**a.** The participle of _verbs of coming_ or _going_ is commonly used in statements of geographical situation.

**b.** The present participle is more common than the aorist in the case of all verbs belonging under 1497.

##### <a id="s1498"></a> §1498

**Dative of the Participle expressing Time**.—In expressions of time a participle is often used with the dative of the person interested in the action of the subject, and especially to express the time that has passed _since_ an action has occurred (cp. “and this is the sixth month with her, who was called barren” St. Luke i. [36](../../part-1-letters-sounds-syllables-accent/01-the-letters.md#s36)).

<span lang="grc">ἀποροῦντι δ᾽ αὐτῷ ἔρχεται Προμηθεύς</span> _Prometheus comes to him in his perplexity_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Prot. 321c">P. Pr. 321c</cite>, <span lang="grc">Ξενοφῶντι πορευομένῳ οἱ ἱππεῖς ἐντυγχάνουσι πρεσβύ̄ταις</span> _while Xenophon was on the march, his horsemen fell in with some old men_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 6.3.10">X. A. 6.3.10</cite>. The idiom is often transferred from persons to things: <span lang="grc">ἡμέραι μάλιστα ἦσαν τῇ Μυτιλήνῃ ἑᾱλωκυίᾳ ἑπτά, ὅτ᾽ ἐς τὸ Ἔμβατον κατέπλευσαν</span> _about seven days had passed since the capture of Mytilene_, _when they sailed into Embatum_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 3.29">T. 3.29</cite>. This construction is frequent in Hom. and Hdt. The participle is rarely omitted (<cite data-ref="Thuc. 1.13">T. 1.13</cite>.).

**a.** A temporal clause may take the place of the participle: <span lang="grc">τῇ στρατιᾷ, ἀφ᾽ οὗ ἐξέπλευσεν εἰς Σικελίᾱν, ἤδη ἐστὶ δύο καὶ πεντήκοντα ἔτη</span> _it is already fifty-two years since the expedition sailed to Sicily_ <cite data-ref="Isaeus 6.14">Is. 6.14</cite>.

### DATIVE WITH ADJECTIVES, ETC.

##### <a id="s1499"></a> §1499

Adjectives, adverbs, and substantives, of kindred meaning with the foregoing verbs, take the dative to define their meaning.

<span lang="grc">βασιλεῖ φίλοι</span> _friendly to the king_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 2.1.20">X. A. 2.1.20</cite>, <span lang="grc">εὔνους τῷ δήμῳ</span> _well disposed to the people_ <cite data-ref="Andoc. 4.16">And. 4.16</cite>, <span lang="grc">τοῖς ϝόμοις ἔνοχος</span> _subject to the laws_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 21.35">D. 21.35</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἐχθρὸν ἐλευθερίᾳ καὶ νόμοις ἐναντίον</span> _hostile to liberty and opposed to law_ 6. 25, <span lang="grc">ξυμμαχίᾳ πίσυνοι</span> _relying on the alliance_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 6.2">T. 6.2</cite>, <span lang="grc">φόρῳ ὑπήκοοι</span> _subject to tribute_ 7. 57, <span lang="grc">ἢν ποιῆτε ὅμοια τοῖς λο´γοις</span> _if you act in accordance with your words_ 2. 72, <span lang="grc">στρατὸς ἴσος καὶ παραπλήσιος τῷ προτέρῳ</span> _an army equal or nearly so to the former_ 7. 42, <!-- page 346 --> <span lang="grc">ἀδελφὰ τὰ βουλεύματα τοῖς ἔργοις</span> _plans like the deeds_ <cite data-ref="Lys. 2.64">L. 2.64</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἀλλήλοις ἀνομοίως</span> _in a way unlike to each other_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Tim. 36d">P. Tim. 36d</cite>. For substantives see [1502](#s1502).

**a.** Some adjectives, as <span lang="grc">φίλος, ἐχθρός</span>, may be treated as substantives and take the genitive. Some adjectives often differ slightly in meaning when they take the genitive.

##### <a id="s1500"></a> §1500

**With <span lang="grc">ὁ αὐτός</span>** _the same_.—<span lang="grc">τὴν αὐτὴν γνώμην ἐμοὶ ἔχειν</span> _to be of the same mind as I am_ <cite data-ref="Lys. 3.21">L. 3.21</cite>, <span lang="grc">τοῦ αὐτοῦ ἐμοὶ πατρός</span> _of the same father as I am_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 40.34">D. 40.34</cite>, <span lang="grc">ταὐτὰ φρονῶν ἐμοί</span> _agreeing with me_ 18. 304.

##### <a id="s1501"></a> §1501

With adjectives and adverbs of similarity and dissimilarity the comparison is often condensed (_brachylogy_) _:_ <span lang="grc">ὁμοίᾱν ταῖς δούλαις εἶχε τὴν ἐσθῆτα</span> _she had a dress on like_ (that of) _her servants_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 5.1.4">X. C. 5.1.4</cite> (the possessor for the thing possessed, = <span lang="grc">τῇ ἐσθῆτι τῶν δουλῶν</span>), <span lang="grc">Ὀρφεῖ γλῶσσα ἡ ἐναντίᾱ</span> _a tongue unlike_ (that of) _Orpheus_ <cite data-ref="Aesch. Ag. 1629">A. Ag. 1629</cite>.

**a.** After adjectives and adverbs of likeness we also find <span lang="grc">καί, ὅσπερ</span> (<span lang="grc">ὥσπερ</span>). Thus, <span lang="grc">παθεῖν ταὐτὸν ὅπερ πολλάκις πρότερον πεπόνθατε</span> _to suffer the same as you have often suffered before_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 1.8">D. 1.8</cite>, <span lang="grc">οὐχ ὁμοίως πεποιήκᾱσι καὶ Ὅμηρος</span> _they have not composed their poetry as Homer did_ P. Ion 531 d.

##### <a id="s1502"></a> §1502

The dative after substantives is chiefly used when the substantive expresses the act denoted by the kindred verb requiring the dative: <span lang="grc">ἐπιβουλὴ ἐμοί</span> _a plot against me_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 5.6.29">X. A. 5.6.29</cite>, <span lang="grc">διάδοχος Κλεάνδρῳ</span> _a successor to Cleander_ 7. 2. 5, <span lang="grc">ἡ ἐμὴ τῷ θεῷ ὑπηρεσίᾱ</span> _my service to the god_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Apol. 30a">P. A. 30a</cite>. But also in other cases: <span lang="grc">φιλίᾱ τοῖς Ἀθηναίοις</span> _friendship for the Athenians_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 5.5">T. 5.5</cite>, <span lang="grc">ὕμνοι θεοῖς</span> _hymns to the gods_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Rep. 607a">P. R. 607a</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἐφόδια τοῖς στρατευομένοις</span> _supplies for the troops_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 3.20">D. 3.20</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἧλοι ταῖς θύραις</span> _nails for the doors_ ([1473](#s1473)).

**a.** Both a genitive and a dative may depend on the same substantive: <span lang="grc">ἡ τοῦ θεοῦ δόσις ὑ̄μῖν</span> _the god's gift to you_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Apol. 30d">P. A. 30d</cite>.

### INSTRUMENTAL DATIVE

##### <a id="s1503"></a> §1503

The Greek dative, as the representative of the lost instrumental case, denotes that _by which_ or _with which_ an action is done or accompanied. It is of two kinds: (1) The instrumental dative proper; (2) The comitative dative.

##### <a id="s1504"></a> §1504

When the idea denoted by the noun in the dative is the _instrument_ or _means_, it falls under (1); if it is a person (not regarded as the instrument or means) or any other living being, or a thing regarded as a person, it belongs under (2); if an action, under (2).

##### <a id="s1505"></a> §1505

Abstract substantives with or without an attributive often stand in the instrumental dative instead of the cognate accusative ([1577](07-accusative.md#s1577)).

### INSTRUMENTAL DATIVE PROPER

##### <a id="s1506"></a> §1506

The dative denotes instrument or means, manner, and cause.

##### <a id="s1507"></a> §1507

**Instrument or Means**.—<span lang="grc">ἔβαλλέ με λίθοις</span> _he hit me with stones_ <cite data-ref="Lys. 3.8">L. 3.8</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἵ̄ησι τῇ ἀξί̄νῃ</span> _he hurls his ax_ at him (_hurls with his ax_) <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 1.5.12">X. A. 1.5.12</cite>, <span lang="grc">ταῖς μαχαίραις</span> <!-- page 347 --> <span lang="grc">κόπτοντες</span> _hacking_ them _with their swords_ 4. 6. 26, <span lang="grc">οὐδὲν ἤνυε τούτοις</span> _he accomplished nothing by this_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 21.104">D. 21.104</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἐζημίωσαν χρήμασιν</span> _they punished_ him _by a fine_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 2.65">T. 2.65</cite>, <span lang="grc">ὕ̄οντος πολλῷ</span> (<span lang="grc">ὕδατι</span>) _during a heavy rain_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Hell. 1.1.16">X. H. 1.1.16</cite> ([934](../07-omission-of-the-subject.md#s934)). So with <span lang="grc">δέχεσθαι</span>: <span lang="grc">τῶν πόλεων οὐ δεχομένων αὐτοὺς ἀγορᾷ οὐδὲ ἄστει, ὕδατι δὲ καὶ ὅρμῳ</span> _as the cities did not admit them to a market nor even into the town, but_ (only) _to water and anchorage_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 6.44">T. 6.44</cite>. Often with passives: <span lang="grc">ᾠκοδομημένον πλίνθοις</span> _built of bricks_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 2.4.12">X. A. 2.4.12</cite>.

**a.** The instrumental dative is often akin to the comitative dative: <span lang="grc">ἀλώμενος νηί τε καὶ ἑτάροισι</span> _wandering with his ship and companions_ <cite data-ref="Hom. Od. 11.161"><span lang="grc">λ</span> 161</cite>, <span lang="grc">νηυσὶν οἰχήσονται</span> _they shall go with their ships_ <cite data-ref="Hom. Il. 24.731"><span lang="grc">Ω</span> 731</cite>, <span lang="grc">θῡμῷ καὶ ῥώμῃ τὸ πλέον ἐναυμα´χουν ἢ ἐπιστήμῃ</span> _they fought with passionate violence and brute force rather than by a system of tactics_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 1.49">T. 1.49</cite>.

**b.** Persons may be regarded as instruments: <span lang="grc">φυλαττόμενοι φύλαξι</span> _defending themselves by pickets_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 6.4.27">X. A. 6.4.27</cite>. Often in poetry (<cite data-ref="Soph. Ant. 164">S. Ant. 164</cite>).

**c.** _Verbs of raining_ or _snowing_ take the dative or accusative ([1570 a](07-accusative.md#s1570)).

##### <a id="s1508"></a> §1508

Under **Means** fall:

**a.** The _dative of price_ (cp. [1372](04-genitive-with-verbs.md#s1372)): <span lang="grc">μέρει τῶν ἀδικημάτων τὸν κίνδῡνον ἐξεπρίαντο</span> _they freed themselves from the danger at the price of a part of their unjust gains_ <cite data-ref="Lys. 27.6">L. 27.6</cite>.

**b.** Rarely, the dative with _verbs of filling_ (cp. [1369](04-genitive-with-verbs.md#s1369)): <span lang="grc">δάκρυσι πᾶν τὸ στράτευμα πλησθέν</span> _the entire army being filled with tears_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 7.75">T. 7.75</cite>.

**c.** The _dative of material_ and _constituent parts:_ <span lang="grc">κατεσκευάσατο ἅρματα τροχοῖς ἰσχῡροῖς</span> _he made chariots with strong wheels_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 6.1.29">X. C. 6.1.29</cite>.

##### <a id="s1509"></a> §1509

<span lang="grc">χρῆσθαι</span> _use_ (strictly _employ oneself with, get something done with;_ cp. _uti_), and sometimes <span lang="grc">νομίζειν</span>, take the dative. Thus, <span lang="grc">οὔτε τούτοις</span> (<span lang="grc">τοῖς νομίμοις</span>) <span lang="grc">χρῆται οὔθ᾽ οἷς ἡ ἄλλη Ἑλλὰς νομίζει</span> _neither acts according to these institutions nor observes those accepted by the rest of Greece_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 1.77">T. 1.77</cite>. A predicate noun may be added to the dative: <span lang="grc">τούτοις χρῶνται δορυφόροις</span> _they make use of them as a body-guard_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Hiero 5.3">X. Hi. 5.3</cite>. The use to which an object is put may be expressed by a neuter pronoun in the accus. ([1573](07-accusative.md#s1573)); <span lang="grc">τί χρησόμεθα τούτῳ</span>; _what use shall we make of it?_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 3.6">D. 3.6</cite>.

##### <a id="s1510"></a> §1510

The instrumental dative occurs after substantives: <span lang="grc">μί̄μησις σχήμασι</span> _imitation by means of gestures_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Rep. 397b">P. R. 397b</cite>.

##### <a id="s1511"></a> §1511

The instrumental dative of means is often, especially in poetry, reinforced by the prepositions <span lang="grc">ἐν, σύν, ὑπό</span>: <span lang="grc">ἐν λόγοις πείθειν</span> _to persuade by words_ <cite data-ref="Soph. Phil. 1393">S. Ph. 1393</cite>, <span lang="grc">οἱ θεοὶ ἐν τοῖς ἱεροῖς ἐσήμηναν</span> _the gods have shown by the victims_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 6.1.31">X. A. 6.1.31</cite>; <span lang="grc">σὺν γήρᾳ βαρεῖς</span> _heavy with old age_ <cite data-ref="Soph. OT 17">S. O. T. 17</cite>; <span lang="grc">πόλις χερσὶν ὑφ᾽ ἡμετέρῃσιν ἁλοῦσα</span> _a city captured by our hands_ B 374.

##### <a id="s1512"></a> §1512

**Dative of Standard of Judgment**.—That by which anything is measured, or judged, is put in the dative: <span lang="grc">ξυνεμετρήσαντο ταῖς ἐπιβολαῖς τῶν πλίνθων</span> _they measured_ the ladders _by the layers of bricks_. <cite data-ref="Thuc. 3.20">T. 3.20</cite>, <span lang="grc">τῷδε δῆλον ἦν</span> _it was plain from what followed_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 2.3.1">X. A. 2.3.1</cite>, <span lang="grc">οἷς πρὸς τοὺς ἄλλους πεποίηκε δεῖ τεκμαίρεσθαι</span> _we must judge by what he has done to the rest_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 9.10">D. 9.10</cite>, <span lang="grc">τίνι χρὴ κρί̄νεσθαι τὰ μέλλοντα καλῶς κριθήσεσθαι; ἆρ᾽ οὐκ ἐμπειρίᾳ τε καὶ φρονήσει καὶ λόγῳ</span>; _by what standard must we judge that the judgment may be correct? Is it not by_ <!-- page 348 --> _experience and wisdom and reasoning?_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Rep. 582a">P. R. 582a</cite>. With _verbs of judging_ <span lang="grc">ἐκ</span> and <span lang="grc">ἀπό</span> are common.

##### <a id="s1513"></a> §1513

**Manner** (see also [1527](#s1527)).—The dative of manner is used with comparative adjectives and other expressions of comparison to mark the degree by which one thing differs from another (**Dative of Measure of Difference**).

<span lang="grc">κεφαλῇ ἐλά̄ττων</span> _a head shorter_ (lit. _by the head_) <cite data-ref="Plat. Phaedo 101a">P. Ph. 101a</cite>, <span lang="grc">οὐ πολλαῖς ἡμέραις ὕστερον ἦλθεν</span> _he arrived not many days later_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Hell. 1.1.1">X. H. 1.1.1</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἰόντες δέκα ἡμέραις πρὸ Παναθηναίων</span> _coming ten days before the Panathenaic festival_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 5.47">T. 5.47</cite>, <span lang="grc">τοσούτῳ ἥδῑον ζῶ ὅσῳ πλείω κέκτημαι</span> _the more I possess the more pleasant is my life_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 8.3.40">X. C. 8.3.40</cite>, <span lang="grc">πολλῷ μείζων ἐγίγνετο ἡ βοὴ ὅσῳ δὴ πλείους ἐγίγνοντο</span> _the shouting became much louder as the men increased in number_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 4.7.23">X. A. 4.7.23</cite>. So with <span lang="grc">πολλῷ</span> _by much_, <span lang="grc">ὀλίγῳ</span> _by little_, <span lang="grc">τῷ παντί</span> _in every respect_ (by all odds).

**a.** With the superlative: <span lang="grc">μακρῷ ἄριστα</span> _by far the best_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Laws 858e">P. L. 858e</cite>.

##### <a id="s1514"></a> §1514

With comparatives the accusatives ([1586](07-accusative.md#s1586)) <span lang="grc">τί, τὶ, οὐδέν, μηδέν</span> without a substantive are always used: <span lang="grc">οὐδὲν ἧττον</span> _nihilo minus_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 7.5.9">X. A. 7.5.9</cite>. In Attic prose (except in Thuc.) <span lang="grc">πολύ</span> and <span lang="grc">ὀλίγον</span> are more common than <span lang="grc">πολλῷ</span> and <span lang="grc">ὀλίγῳ</span> with comparatives. Hom. has only <span lang="grc">πολὺ μείζων</span>.

##### <a id="s1515"></a> §1515

Measure of difference may be expressed by <span lang="grc">ἔν τινι; εἴς τι, κατά τι;</span> or by <span lang="grc">ἐπί τινι</span>.

##### <a id="s1516"></a> §1516

The dative of manner may denote the particular point of view from which a statement is made. This occurs chiefly with intransitive adjectives but also with intransitive verbs (**Dative of Respect**). (Cp. [1600](07-accusative.md#s1600).)

<span lang="grc">ἀνὴρ ἡλικίᾳ ἔτι νέος</span> _a man still young in years_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 5.43">T. 5.43</cite>, <span lang="grc">τοῖς σώμασι τὸ πλέον ἰσχύ̄ουσα ἢ τοῖς χρήμασιν</span> a power _stronger in men than in money_ 1. 121, <span lang="grc">ἀσθενὴς τῷ σώματι</span> _weak in body_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 21.165">D. 21.165</cite>, <span lang="grc">τῇ φωνῇ τρᾱχύς</span> _harsh of voice_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 2.6.9">X. A. 2.6.9</cite>, <span lang="grc">φρονήσει διαφέρων</span> _distinguished in understanding_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 2.3.5">X. C. 2.3.5</cite>, <span lang="grc">τῶν τότε δυνάμει προύχων</span> _superior in power to the men of that time_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 1.9">T. 1.9</cite>, <span lang="grc">ὀνόματι σπονδαί</span> _a truce so far as the name goes_ 6. 10.

**a.** The accusative of respect ([1600](07-accusative.md#s1600)) is often nearly equivalent to the dative of respect.

##### <a id="s1517"></a> §1517

**Cause**.—The dative, especially with verbs of emotion, expresses the occasion (external cause) or the motive (internal cause).

Occasion: <span lang="grc">τῇ τύχῃ ἐλπίσᾱς</span> _confident by reason of his good fortune_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 3.97">T. 3.97</cite>, <span lang="grc">θαυμάζω τῇ ἀποκλῄσει μου τῶν πυλῶν</span> _I am astonished at being shut out of the gates_ 4. 85, <span lang="grc">τούτοις ἥσθη</span> _he was pleased at this_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 1.9.26">X. A. 1.9.26</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἠχθόμεθα τοῖς γεγενημένοις</span> _we were troubled at what had occurred_ 5. 7. 20, <span lang="grc">χαλεπῶς φέρω τοῖς παροῦσι πρά̄γμασιν</span> _I am troubled at the present occurrences_ 1. 3. 3. Motive: <span lang="grc">φιλίᾳ καὶ εὐνοίᾳ ἑπόμενοι</span> _following out of friendship and good will_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 2.6.13">X. A. 2.6.13</cite>. Occasion and motive: <span lang="grc">οἱ μὲν ἀπορίᾳ ἀκολούθων, οἱ δὲ ἀπιστίᾳ</span> _some_ (carried their own food) _because they lacked servants, others through distrust of them_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 7.75">T. 7.75</cite>, <span lang="grc">ὕβρει καὶ οὐκ οἴνῳ τοῦτο ποιῶν</span> _doing this out of insolence and not because he was drunk_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 21.74">D. 21.74</cite>. <!-- page 349 -->

##### <a id="s1518"></a> §1518

Some verbs of emotion take <span lang="grc">ἐπί</span> (with dat.) to denote the cause; so always <span lang="grc">μέγα φρονεῖν</span> _to plume oneself_, and often <span lang="grc">χαίρειν</span> _rejoice_, <span lang="grc">λῡπεῖσθαι</span> _grieve_, <span lang="grc">ἀγανακτεῖν</span> _be vexed_, <span lang="grc">αἰσχύ̄νεσθαι</span> _be ashamed_. Many verbs take the genitive ([1405](05-free-uses-of-the-genitive.md#s1405)).

##### <a id="s1519"></a> §1519

The dative of cause sometimes approximates to a dative of purpose ([1473](#s1473)): <span lang="grc">Ἀθηναῖοι ἐφ᾽ ἡμᾶς ὥρμηνται Λεοντί̄νων κατοικίσει</span> _the Athenians have set out against us_ (_with a view to_) _to restore the Leontines_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 6.33">T. 6.33</cite>. This construction is common with other verbal nouns in Thucydides.

##### <a id="s1520"></a> §1520

Cause is often expressed by <span lang="grc">διά</span> with the accusative, <span lang="grc">ὑπό</span> with the genitive, less frequently by <span lang="grc">ἀμφί</span> or <span lang="grc">περί</span> with the dative (poet.) or <span lang="grc">ὑπέρ</span> with the genitive (poet.).

### COMITATIVE DATIVE

##### <a id="s1521"></a> §1521

The comitative form of the instrumental dative denotes the persons or things which accompany or take part in an action.

##### <a id="s1522"></a> §1522

Prepositions of accompaniment (<span lang="grc">μετά</span> with gen., <span lang="grc">σύν</span>) are often used, especially when the verb does not denote accompaniment or union.

##### <a id="s1523"></a> §1523

**Dative of Association**.—The dative is used with words denoting friendly or hostile association or intercourse. This dative is especially common in the plural and after middle verbs.

**a.** <span lang="grc">κακοῖς ὁμῑλῶν καὐτὸς ἐκβήσῃ κακός</span> _if thou associate with the evil, in the end thou too wilt become evil thyself_ Men. Sent. 274, <span lang="grc">ἀλλήλοις διειλέγμεθα</span> _we have conversed with each other_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Apol. 37a">P. A. 37a</cite>, <span lang="grc">τῷ πλήθει τὰ ῥηθέντα κοινώσαντες</span> _communicating to the people what had been said_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 2.72">T. 2.72</cite>, <span lang="grc">δεόμενοι τοὺς φεύγοντας ξυναλλάξαι σφίσι</span> _asking that they reconcile their exiles with them_ 1. 24, <span lang="grc">εἰς λόγους σοι ἐλθεῖν</span> _to have an interview with you_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 2.5.4">X. A. 2.5.4</cite>, <span lang="grc">μετεσχήκαμεν ὑ̄μῖν θυσιῶν</span> _we have participated in your festivals_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Hell. 2.4.20">X. H. 2.4.20</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἀλλήλοις σπονδὰ̄ς ἐποιήσαντο</span> _they made a truce with one another_ 3. 2. 20, <span lang="grc">αὐτοῖς διὰ φιλίᾱς ἰέναι</span> _to enter into friendship with them_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 3.2.8">X. A. 3.2.8</cite>. So with _verbs of meeting:_ <span lang="grc">προσέρχεσθαι, προστυγχάνειν</span> and <span lang="grc">ἐντυγχάνειν, ἀπαντᾶν</span>.

**b.** <span lang="grc">πολλοῖς ὀλίγοι μαχόμενοι</span> _few fighting with many_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 4.36">T. 4.36</cite>, <span lang="grc">Κύ̄ρῳ πολεμοῦντες</span> _waging war with Cyrus_ 1. 13, <span lang="grc">ἀμφισβητοῦσι μὲν δι᾽ εὔνοιαν οἱ φίλοι τοῖς φίλοις, ἐρίζουσι δὲ οἱ διάφοροι ἀλλήλοις</span> _friends dispute with friends good-naturedly_, _but adversaries wrangle with one another_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Prot. 337b">P. Pr. 337b</cite>, <span lang="grc">δίκᾱς ἀλλήλοις δικάζονται</span> _they bring lawsuits against one another_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Mem. 3.5.16">X. M. 3.5.16</cite>, <span lang="grc">διαφέρεσθαι τούτοις</span> _to be at variance with these men_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 18.31">D. 18.31</cite> (and so many compounds of <span lang="grc">διά</span>), <span lang="grc">οὐκ ἔφη τοὺς λόγους τοῖς ἔργοις ὁμολογεῖν</span> _he said their words did not agree with their deeds_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 5.55">T. 5.55</cite>. So also <span lang="grc">τινὶ διὰ πολέμου</span> (<span lang="grc">διὰ μάχης, εἰς χεῖρας</span>) <span lang="grc">ἰέναι, τινὶ ὁμόσε χωρεῖν</span>, etc.

N. 1.—<span lang="grc">πολεμεῖν</span> (<span lang="grc">μάχεσθαι</span>) <span lang="grc">σύν τινι</span> (<span lang="grc">μετά τινος</span>) means _to wage war in conjunction with some one_.

N. 2.—Verbs of friendly or hostile association, and especially periphrases with <span lang="grc">ποιεῖσθαι</span> (<span lang="grc">πόλεμον, σπονδά̄ς</span>), often take the accusative with <span lang="grc">πρός</span>.

##### <a id="s1524"></a> §1524

**Dative of Accompaniment**.—The dative of accompaniment is used with verbs signifying _to accompany, follow_, etc.

<span lang="grc">ἀκολουθεῖν τῷ ἡγουμένῳ</span> _to follow the leader_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Rep. 474c">P. R. 474c</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἕπεσθαι ὑ̄μῖν βούλομαι</span> <!-- page 350 --> _I am willing to follow you_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 3.1.25">X. A. 3.1.25</cite>. <span lang="grc">μετά</span> with the genitive is often used, as are <span lang="grc">σύν</span> and <span lang="grc">ἅμα</span> with the dative.

##### <a id="s1525"></a> §1525

**With <span lang="grc">αὐτός</span>**.—The idea of accompaniment is often expressed by <span lang="grc">αὐτός</span> joined to the dative. This use is common when the destruction of a person or thing is referred to. Thus, <span lang="grc">τῶν ϝεῶν μία αὐτοῖς ἀνδράσιν</span> _one of the ships with its crew_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 4.14">T. 4.14</cite>, <span lang="grc">εἶπεν ἥκειν εἰς τὰ̄ς τάξεις αὐτοῖς στεφάνοις</span> _he bade them come to their posts, crowns and all_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 3.3.40">X. C. 3.3.40</cite>. The article after <span lang="grc">αὐτός</span> is rare; and <span lang="grc">σύν</span> is rarely added (<cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 2.2.9">X. C. 2.2.9</cite>). Hom. has this dative only with lifeless objects.

##### <a id="s1526"></a> §1526

**Dative of Military Accompaniment**.—The dative is used in the description of military movements to denote the accompaniment (troops, ships, etc.) of a leader: <span lang="grc">ἐξελαύνει τῷ στρατεύματι παντί</span> _he marches out with all his army_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 1.7.14">X. A. 1.7.14</cite>. <span lang="grc">σύν</span> is often used with words denoting troops (<cite data-ref="Thuc. 6.62">T. 6.62</cite>).

**a.** An extension of this usage occurs when the persons in the dative are essentially the same as the persons forming the subject (distributive use): <span lang="grc">ἡμῖν ἐφείποντο οἱ πολέμιοι καὶ ἱππικῷ καὶ πελταστικῷ</span> _the enemy pursued us with their cavalry and peltasts_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 7.6.29">X. A. 7.6.29</cite>.

**b.** The dative of military accompaniment is often equivalent to a dative of means when the verb does not denote the leadership of a general.

##### <a id="s1527"></a> §1527

**Dative of Accompanying Circumstance**.—The dative, usually of an abstract substantive, may denote accompanying circumstance and manner.

**a.** The substantive has an attribute: <span lang="grc">πολλῇ βοῇ προσέκειντο</span> _they attacked with loud shouts_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 4.127">T. 4.127</cite>, <span lang="grc">παντὶ σθένει</span> _with all one's might_ 5. 23, <span lang="grc">τύχῃ ἀγαθῇ</span> _with good fortune_ C.I.A. /lref&gt;So <span lang="grc">παντὶ</span> (<span lang="grc">οὐδενὶ, ἄλλῳ, τούτῳ τῷ</span>) <span lang="grc">τρόπῳ</span>. Manner may be expressed by the adjective, as <span lang="grc">βιαίῳ θανάτῳ ἀποθνῄσκειν</span> _to die_ (_by_) _a violent death_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Hiero 4.3">X. Hi. 4.3</cite> (= <span lang="grc">βίᾳ</span>).

**b.** Many particular substantives have no attribute and are used adverbially: <span lang="grc">θεῖν δρόμῳ</span> _to run at full speed_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 1.8.19">X. A. 1.8.19</cite>, <span lang="grc">βίᾳ</span> _by force_, <span lang="grc">δίκῃ</span> _justly_, <span lang="grc">δόλῳ</span> _by craft_, (<span lang="grc">τῷ</span>) <span lang="grc">ἔργῳ</span> _in fact_, <span lang="grc">ἡσυχῇ</span> _quietly_, <span lang="grc">κομιδῇ</span> (_with care_) _entirely_, <span lang="grc">κόσμῳ</span> _in order_, _duly_, <span lang="grc">κύκλῳ</span> _round about_, (<span lang="grc">τῷ</span>) <span lang="grc">λόγῳ</span> _in word_, <span lang="grc">προφάσει</span> _ostensibly_, <span lang="grc">σῑγῇ, σιωπῇ</span> _in silence_, <span lang="grc">σπουδῇ</span> _hastily_, _with difficulty_, <span lang="grc">τῇ ἀληθείᾳ</span> _in truth_, <span lang="grc">τῷ ὄντι</span> _in reality_, <span lang="grc">ὀργῇ</span> _in anger_, <span lang="grc">φυγῇ</span> _in hasty flight_.

N.—When no adjective is used, prepositional phrases or adverbs are generally employed: <span lang="grc">σὺν κραυγῇ, σὺν δίκῃ, μετὰ δίκης, πρὸς βίᾱν</span> (or <span lang="grc">βιαίως</span>).

**c.** Here belongs the dative of feminine adjectives with a substantive (<span lang="grc">ὁδῷ</span>, etc.) omitted, as <span lang="grc">ταύτῃ</span> _in this way, here_, <span lang="grc">ἄλλῃ</span> _in another way, elsewhere_, <span lang="grc">πῇ, ᾗ</span> _in what_ (_which_) _way_. So <span lang="grc">δημοσίᾳ</span> _at public expense_, <span lang="grc">ἰδίᾳ</span> _privately_, <span lang="grc">κοινῇ</span> _in common_, <span lang="grc">πεζῇ</span> _on foot_.

N.—Some of these forms are instrumental rather than comitative, e.g. <span lang="grc">ταύτῃ</span>.

##### <a id="s1528"></a> §1528

**Space and Time**.—The dative of space and time may sometimes be regarded as comitative.

**a.** Space: _the way by which_ (_qua_), as <span lang="grc">ἐπορεύετο τῇ ὁδῷ ἣν πρότερον ἐποιήσατο</span> _he marched by the road_ (or _on the road_?) _which he had made before_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 2.98">T. 2.98</cite>; **b.** Time: <span lang="grc">κατηγόρει ὡς ἐκείνη τῷ χ&lt;\*&gt;όνῳ πεισθείη</span> _she charged that she had been_ <!-- page 351 --> _persuaded in_ (_by_) _the course of time_ <cite data-ref="Lys. 1.20">L. 1.20</cite>. Some of these uses are instrumental rather than comitative.

### WITH ADJECTIVES, ETC.

##### <a id="s1529"></a> §1529

Many adjectives and adverbs, and some substantives, take the instrumental dative by the same construction as the corresponding verbs.

<span lang="grc">σύμμαχος αὐτοῖς</span> _their ally_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 9.58">D. 9.58</cite>, <span lang="grc">χώρᾱ ὅμορος τῇ Λακεδαιμονίων</span> _a country bordering on that of the Lacedaemonians_ 15. 22, <span lang="grc">ἀκόλουθα τούτοις</span> _conformable to this_ 18. 257. So <span lang="grc">κοινός</span> (cp. [1414](05-free-uses-of-the-genitive.md#s1414)), <span lang="grc">σύμφωνος, συγγενής, μεταίτιος</span>, and <span lang="grc">διάφορος</span> meaning _at variance with_.—<span lang="grc">ἑπομένως τῷ νόμῳ</span> _conformably to the law_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Laws 844e">P. L. 844e</cite>, <span lang="grc">ὁ ἑξῆς νόμος τούτῳ</span> _the law next to this_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 21.10">D. 21.10</cite>. Many of the adjectives belonging here also take the genitive when the idea of possession or connection is marked.—<span lang="grc">ἅμα</span> chiefly in the meaning _at the same time_.—<span lang="grc">κοινωνίᾱ τοῖς ἀνδράσι</span> _intercourse with men_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Rep. 466c">P. R. 466c</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἐπιδρομὴ τῷ τειχίσματι</span> _attack on the fort_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 4.23">T. 4.23</cite>.

### LOCATIVE DATIVE

##### <a id="s1530"></a> §1530

The dative as the representative of the locative is used to express place and time.

**a.** On the instrumental dative of space and time, see [1528](#s1528).

##### <a id="s1531"></a> §1531

**Dative of Place**.—In poetry the dative without a preposition is used to denote place.

**a.** _Where a person or thing is:_ <span lang="grc">στὰ̄ς μέσῳ ἕρκεϊ</span> _taking his stand in the middle of the court_ <cite data-ref="Hom. Il. 24.306"><span lang="grc">Ω</span> 306</cite>, <span lang="grc">γῇ ἔκειτο</span> _she lay on the ground_ <cite data-ref="Soph. OT 1266">S. O. T. 1266</cite>, <span lang="grc">ναίειν ὄρεσιν</span> _to dwell among the mountains_ O. T. 1451. Often of the parts of the body (Hom. <span lang="grc">θῡμῷ, καρδίῃ</span>, etc.). With persons (generally in the plural): <span lang="grc">ἀριπρεπὴς Τρώεσσιν</span> _conspicuous among the Trojans_ Z 477. <span lang="grc">τοῖσι δ᾽ ἀνέστη</span> A 68 may be _rose up among them_ or a dative proper (_for them_).

**b.** _Place whither_ (limit of motion): <span lang="grc">πεδίῳ πέσε</span> _fell on the ground_ E 82, <span lang="grc">κολεῷ ἄορ θέο</span> _put thy sword into its sheath_ <cite data-ref="Hom. Od. 10.333"><span lang="grc">κ</span> 333</cite>.

##### <a id="s1532"></a> §1532

After verbs of motion the dative, as distinguished from the locative, denotes direction _towards_ and is used of persons ([1485](#s1485)), and is a form of the dative of interest.

##### <a id="s1533"></a> §1533

Many verbs capable of taking the locative dative in poetry, require, in prose, the aid of a preposition in composition. The limit of motion is usually ([1589](07-accusative.md#s1589)) expressed by the accusative with a preposition (e.g. <span lang="grc">εἰς, πρός</span>).

##### <a id="s1534"></a> §1534

In prose the dative of place (chiefly _place where_) is used only of proper names: <span lang="grc">Πῡθοῖ</span> _at Pytho_, <span lang="grc">Ἰσθμοῖ</span> _at the Isthmus_, <span lang="grc">Σαλαμῖνι</span> _at Salamis_, <span lang="grc">Ὀλυμπίᾱσι</span> _at Olympia_, <span lang="grc">Ἀθήνησι</span> _at Athens_ (inscr.); especially with the names of Attic demes, as <span lang="grc">Φαληροῖ, Θορικοῖ, Μαραθῶνι</span>. But <span lang="grc">ἐν Μαραθῶνι</span> and <span lang="grc">ἐν Πλαταιαῖς</span> occur. Some deme-names require <span lang="grc">ἐν</span>, as <span lang="grc">ἐν Κοίλῃ</span>.

##### <a id="s1535"></a> §1535

Many adverbs are genuine locatives, as <span lang="grc">οἴκοι, πάλαι, πανδημεί, Φαληροῖ; Ἀθήνησι, Πλαταιᾶσι</span>; others are datives in form, as <span lang="grc">κύκλῳ, Πλαταιαῖς</span>.

##### <a id="s1536"></a> §1536

With names of countries and places, <span lang="grc">ἐν</span> is more common than the <!-- page 352 --> locative dative, and, with the above exceptions, the place _where_ is expressed in Attic prose with <span lang="grc">ἐν</span>.

##### <a id="s1537"></a> §1537

_Verbs of ruling_ often take the dative, especially in Homer: <span lang="grc">Μυρμιδόνεσσιν ἄνασσε</span> <cite data-ref="Hom. Il. 1.180"><span lang="grc">Α</span> 180</cite>, <span lang="grc">Γιγάντεσσιν βασίλευεν η</span> 59, <span lang="grc">ἦρχε δ᾽ ἄρα σφιν Ἀγαμέμνων Ξ</span> 134. Rarely in prose: <span lang="grc">ἡγεῖσθαί τινι</span> _to serve as guide_ (leader) _to some one_, <span lang="grc">ἐπιστατεῖν τινι</span> _to be set over one;_ <span lang="grc">ἄρχειν τινί</span> means only = _to be archon_ (<span lang="grc">Πῡθοδώρου ἄρχοντος Ἀθηναίοις</span> <cite data-ref="Thuc. 2.2">T. 2.2</cite>). Cp. [1371](04-genitive-with-verbs.md#s1371).

**a.** Only when stress is not laid on the idea of supremacy is the dative, instead of the genitive ([1370](04-genitive-with-verbs.md#s1370)), used with _verbs of ruling_.

##### <a id="s1538"></a> §1538

It is not clear whether the dative with _verbs of ruling_ is a dative proper (_for_), a locative (_among;_ cp. <span lang="grc">ἐν Φαίηξιν ἄνασσε η</span> [62](../../part-1-letters-sounds-syllables-accent/02-euphony-of-vowels.md#s62)), or an instrumental (_by_). <span lang="grc">ἄρχειν, ἡγεῖσθαι</span> may take the dative proper, <span lang="grc">ἀνάσσειν, βασιλεύειν, κρατεῖν</span> may take the locative dative.

##### <a id="s1539"></a> §1539

**Dative of Time**.—The dative without a preposition is commonly used to denote a definite point of time (chiefly _day_, _night_, _month_, _year_, _season_) _at which_ an action occurred. The dative contrasts one point of time with another, and is usually accompanied by an attributive.

##### <a id="s1540"></a> §1540

The dative denotes the time _at which_ an action takes place and the date of an event.

<span lang="grc">ταύτην μὲν τὴν ἡμέρᾱν αὐτοῦ ἔμειναν, τῇ δὲ ὑστεραίᾳ κτλ</span>. _throughout that day they waited there, but on the day following_, etc. <cite data-ref="Xen. Hell. 1.1.14">X. H. 1.1.14</cite>. So <span lang="grc">τῇ προτεραίᾳ</span> _the day before_, <span lang="grc">τῇ δευτέρᾳ</span> _the second day_, <span lang="grc">Ἐλαφηβολιῶνος μηνὸς ἕκτῃ</span> (<span lang="grc">ἡμέρᾳ</span>) <span lang="grc">φθίνοντος</span> _on the sixth of waning Elaphebolion_ <cite data-ref="Aeschin. 2.90">Aes. 2.90</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἕνῃ καὶ ϝέᾳ</span> _on the last of the month_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 18.29">D. 18.29</cite>; <span lang="grc">τρίτῳ μηνί</span> _in the third month_ <cite data-ref="Lys. 21.1">L. 21.1</cite>, <span lang="grc">περιιόντι τῷ θέρει</span> _when summer was coming to an end_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 1.30">T. 1.30</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἐξηκοστῷ ἔτει</span> _in the sixtieth year_ 1. 12; also with <span lang="grc">ὥρᾳ</span> (<span lang="grc">χειμῶνος ὥρᾳ</span> _in the winter season_ <cite data-ref="Andoc. 1.137">And. 1.137</cite>).

##### <a id="s1541"></a> §1541

The names of the regular recurring festivals which serve to date an &lt;\*&gt;ccurrence stand in the dative: <span lang="grc">Παναθηναίοις</span> _at the Panathenaea_ <cite data-ref="Dem. 21.156">D. 21.156</cite>, <span lang="grc">&lt;\*&gt;οῖς Διονῡσίοις</span> _at the Dionysia_ 21. 1, <span lang="grc">ταῖς πομπαῖς</span> _at the processions_ 21. 171, <span lang="grc">τοῖς &lt;\*&gt;οαγῳδοῖς</span> _at the representations of the tragedies_ <cite data-ref="Aeschin. 3.176">Aes. 3.176</cite>. <span lang="grc">ἐν</span> is rarely added.

##### <a id="s1542"></a> §1542

<span lang="grc">ἐν</span> is added:

**a.** To words denoting time when there is no attributive: <span lang="grc">ἐν τῷ χειμῶνι</span> _in winter_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Ec. 17.3">X. O. 17.3</cite>; cp. [1444](05-free-uses-of-the-genitive.md#s1444). **b.** When the attributive is a pronoun (sometimes): (<span lang="grc">ἐν</span>) <span lang="grc">ἐκείνῃ τῇ ἡμέρᾳ</span>. **c.** To statements of the time within the limits of which an event may take place (where <span lang="grc">ἐντός</span> with the genitive is common); to statements of how much time anything takes; with numbers, <span lang="grc">ὀλίγος, πολύς</span>, etc. Thus, <span lang="grc">ἐν τρισὶν ἡμέραις</span> _for_ (during) _three days_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 4.8.8">X. A. 4.8.8</cite>, <span lang="grc">οὐ ῥᾴδιον τὰ ἐν ἅπαντι τῷ χρόνῳ πρᾱχθέντα ἐν μιᾷ ἡμέρᾳ δηλωθῆναι</span> _it is not easy to set forth in a single day the acts of all time_ <cite data-ref="Lys. 2.54">L. 2.54</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἐξελέσθαι τὴν διαβολὴν ἐν οὕτως ὀλίγῳ χρόνῳ</span> _to clear myself of calumny in so brief a time_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Apol. 19a">P. A. 19a</cite>. <span lang="grc">ἐν</span> is rarely omitted in prose, and chiefly when there is an attributive: <span lang="grc">μιᾷ νυκτὶ</span> <cite data-ref="Thuc. 6.27">T. 6.27</cite>. **d.** Always with adjectives or adverbs used substantively: <span lang="grc">ἐν τῷ παρόντι, ἐν τῷ τότε</span>. **e.** To words denoting the date of an event, not a point of time: <span lang="grc">ἐν τῇ προτέρᾳ πρεσβείᾳ</span> _in the first embassy_ <cite data-ref="Aeschin. 2.123">Aes. 2.123</cite>. Thuc. employs <span lang="grc">ἐν</span>, as <span lang="grc">ἐν τῇ ὑστεραίᾳ ἐκκλησίᾳ</span> _in_ <!-- page 353 --> _the assembly held the day after_ 1. 44, but usu. the simple dative, as <span lang="grc">μάχῃ</span> _in the battle_ 3. 54, <span lang="grc">ἐκείνῃ τῇ ἐσβολῇ</span> _in that incursion_ 2. 20, <span lang="grc">τῇ προτέρᾳ ἐκκλησίᾳ</span> _in the first assembly_ 1. 44.

##### <a id="s1543"></a> §1543

The dative and genitive of time are sometimes employed with only a slight difference ([1447 a](05-free-uses-of-the-genitive.md#s1447)).

### DATIVE WITH COMPOUND VERBS

##### <a id="s1544"></a> §1544

Many compound verbs take the dative because of their meaning as a whole. So <span lang="grc">ἀντέχειν</span> _hold out against_, <span lang="grc">ἀμφισβητεῖν</span> _dispute with_ ([1523 b](#s1523)).

##### <a id="s1545"></a> §1545

The dative is used with verbs compounded with <span lang="grc">σύν</span> (regularly), with many compounded with <span lang="grc">ἐν, ἐπί</span>, and with some compounded with <span lang="grc">παρά, περί, πρός</span>, and <span lang="grc">ὑπό</span>, because the preposition keeps a sense that requires the dative.

<span lang="grc">ἐμβλέψᾱς αὐτῷ</span> _looking at him_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Charm. 162d">P. Charm. 162d</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἐλπίδας ἐμποιεῖν ἀνθρώποις</span> _to create expectations in men_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Cyrop. 1.6.19">X. C. 1.6.19</cite>, <span lang="grc">αὐτοῖς ἐπέπεσε τὸ Ἑλληνικόν</span> _the Greek force fell upon them_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 4.1.10">X. A. 4.1.10</cite>, <span lang="grc">ἐπέκειντο αὐτοῖς</span> _they pressed hard upon them_ 5. 2. 5, <span lang="grc">συναδικεῖν αὐτοῖς</span> _to be their accomplice in wrong-doing_ 2. 6. 27, <span lang="grc">ξυνίσᾱσι Μελήτῳ ψευδομένῳ</span> _they are conscious that Meletus is speaking falsely_ (i.e. they know it as well as he does) <cite data-ref="Plat. Apol. 34b">P. A. 34b</cite>, <span lang="grc">οὗτοι οὐ παρεγένοντο βασιλεῖ</span> _these did not join the king_ <cite data-ref="Xen. Anab. 5.6.8">X. A. 5.6.8</cite>, <span lang="grc">παρέστω ὑ̄μῖν ὁ κῆρυξ</span> _let the herald come with us_ 3. 1. 46, <span lang="grc">Ξενοφῶντι προσέτρεχον δύο νεᾱνίσκω</span> _two youths ran up to Xenophon_ 4. 3. 10, <span lang="grc">ὑποκεῖσθαι τῷ ἄρχοντι</span> _to be subject to the ruler_ <cite data-ref="Plat. Gorg. 510c">P. G. 510c</cite>.

**a.** So especially with verbs of motion and rest formed from <span lang="grc">ἰέναι, πί̄πτειν, τιθέναι, τρέχειν, εἶναι, γίγνεσθαι, κεῖσθαι</span>, etc.

##### <a id="s1546"></a> §1546

Some verbs of motion compounded with <span lang="grc">παρά, περί, ὑπό</span> take the accusative ([1559](07-accusative.md#s1559)).

##### <a id="s1547"></a> §1547

Some verbs have an alternative construction, e.g. <span lang="grc">περιβάλλειν</span>: <span lang="grc">τινί τι</span> _&lt;\*&gt;nvest a person with something_, <span lang="grc">τί τινι</span> _surround something with something_.

##### <a id="s1548"></a> §1548

Compounds of <span lang="grc">σύν</span> take the instrumental, compounds of <span lang="grc">ἐν</span> take the locative dative.

##### <a id="s1549"></a> §1549

When the idea of place is emphatic, the preposition may be repeated: <span lang="grc">ἐμμείναντες ἐν τῇ Ἀττικῇ</span> _remaining in Attica_ <cite data-ref="Thuc. 2.23">T. 2.23</cite>; but it is generally not repeated when the idea is figurative: <span lang="grc">τοῖς ὅρκοις ἐμμένων</span> _abiding by one's oath_ <cite data-ref="Isoc. 1.13">I. 1.13</cite>. <span lang="grc">μετά</span> may be used after compounds of <span lang="grc">σύν</span>: <span lang="grc">μετ᾽ ἐμοῦ συνέπλει</span> _he sailed in company with me_ <cite data-ref="Lys. 21.8">L. 21.8</cite>.

##### <a id="s1550"></a> §1550

The prepositions are more frequently repeated in prose than in poetry.
